**The town then.** One of Thuringia's oldest towns (a documented history back to 704), seat of a small county, and the deepest of deep Bach country — the clan had supplied Arnstadt's musicians for nearly a century, and the twin brother of Bach's father had been a town musician here. When the town hired the nineteen-year-old Sebastian, they were, in their own minds, hiring a known quantity: another Bach. The consistory minutes record their surprise.

**The church and the organ.** The Neue Kirche ("New Church," rebuilt after a fire, today renamed the *Bachkirche*) received its new organ from the builder J. F. Wender in 1703; Bach examined it, then presided over it. Modest by Hamburg standards (two manuals, ~23 stops) but new, sound, and *his* — the first instrument Bach could call his own, on which the early organ works took shape.

**The geography of the era's drama.** The market square where the Geyersbach scuffle happened lies steps from the church; the consistory rooms where he was scolded, likewise. The entire theater of Bach's most turbulent employment fits inside a five-minute walk.

**What survives today.** The Bachkirche still holds services with the restored Wender organ's original console preserved in the town museum; the market square carries a famously *young* Bach memorial statue (lounging, open-collared — the difficult young genius, not the wigged patriarch), which this timeline endorses as the era's correct portrait.
