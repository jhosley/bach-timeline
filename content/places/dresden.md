**The city then.** Everything Leipzig wasn't: the Saxon Elector-Kings' residence — Catholic court (the rulers had converted for the Polish crown; the populace stayed Lutheran), Italian opera, and **the best orchestra in Europe**, whose salaried virtuosi Bach held up to his own council [as the standard Leipzig refused to fund](../events/leipzig-2/memo-1730.md) ("one need only go to Dresden," the memo says in effect, "to see what musicians paid to master their instruments sound like").

**Bach's Dresden.** The stage of [the Marchand non-duel](../events/weimar/marchand-1717.md) (1717); the court to which he addressed [the 1733 Missa and the title campaign](../events/leipzig-2/dresden-title-1733-36.md); the scene of celebrated organ recitals (the 1736 Frauenkirche Silbermann concert that publicly rang in his court title); his son Friedemann's first post (Sophienkirche, 1733); and a semi-regular escape — he reportedly joked about going to Dresden "to hear the pretty little songs" at the opera. Aspiration, leverage, and pleasure: the capital was all three.

**What survives today.** The Frauenkirche — destroyed in the 1945 firestorm, left as rubble through the DDR decades, rebuilt stone by stone (reconsecrated 2005) — with a new organ; the Hofkirche holds a great Silbermann. Dresden's double identity in Bach's story (splendor; the standard Leipzig wouldn't pay for) reads differently, and more movingly, against its later history.
