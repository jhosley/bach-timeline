**The city then.** Saxony's commercial powerhouse (~30,000 people): trade-fair city three times a year, publishing capital, home of a famous university — bourgeois, busy, and proud, with no court and no prince in residence. Music answered to the **town council** and the churches; intellectual life to the university; fashion to the fairs, which brought all Europe (and its newest tastes) through the gates twice a year. After a lifetime of courts and small towns, Bach spent his last twenty-seven years in Germany's most modern city — the setting of both his largest opportunities and his most grinding institutional fights.

**The Bach geography.** The **Thomasschule** on its square: school, dormitory, and the cantor's apartment where the family lived and the copying workshop ran (composing room directly beside the schoolboys' quarters). The **Thomaskirche** and **Nikolaikirche**, alternating hosts of the principal Sunday music; the Neue Kirche and Petrikirche making up the four. **Zimmermann's coffee house** on Catherine Street and its summer garden — the Collegium's concert hall and the birthplace of the keyboard concerto. The university's Paulinerkirche, subject of an early jurisdiction battle over fees.

**What survives today.** The Thomaskirche stands, with Bach's remains beneath the choir (moved there in 1950) and the **Thomanerchor** — the boys' choir he directed, still singing, now eight centuries old; the Bach-Archiv and Bach Museum face it across the square. The Nikolaikirche stands (its later fame: the 1989 peaceful revolution began at its prayer meetings). The Thomasschule building and Zimmermann's were lost; Café Zimmermann's site is marked. The city's June **Bachfest** turns the whole geography into a festival stage.
