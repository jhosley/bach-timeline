**What it is.** Officially a concerto for violin and two recorders (Bach's puzzling term *fiauti d'echo*, "echo flutes," still debated); in practice a brilliant violin concerto wearing pastoral clothing. The recorders own the gentle opening idea; the violin gradually reveals itself as the engine, with passagework as demanding as anything Bach wrote for the instrument — presumably a portrait of the Cöthen concertmaster Joseph Spiess.

**Listen for.** The second movement's echo games — phrases answered softly, the "echo" of the flutes' name made audible. The finale is the most rigorous movement in the whole set: a full **fugue** for orchestra whose subject enters in all the string parts before the soloists complicate it — momentum and learning fused so completely you can dance to the counterpoint.

**Craft.** Compare the finale with No. 2's fugal finale: there the fugue is distributed among four soloists, here it belongs to the orchestra with soloists as decoration. Bach later rewrote the whole concerto with harpsichord replacing the violin (BWV 1057, Leipzig) — his own works were raw material to him, a workshop habit the timeline tracks under *parody and transcription*.
