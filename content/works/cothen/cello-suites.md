Of all the instruments Bach could have chosen for a solo literature, the cello was the least likely. In his day it was the ensemble's bass servant — the voice that held the floor down while others danced on it, exactly as the harpsichord was the ensemble's servant before Brandenburg No. 5 staged its coup. Writing six unaccompanied dance suites for such an instrument was the same act of emancipation: a declaration that the accompanist had a soul, and stories of its own to tell. Every suite follows one floor plan — Prelude, Allemande, Courante, Sarabande, then a pair of lighter *galanteries* (minuets, bourrées, or gavottes), and a closing Gigue. The same skeleton six times over; six utterly different worlds built on it, rising steadily in difficulty and depth from the amiable First to the radiant Sixth, which asks for a five-string instrument and seems to ask for a larger sky as well.

Honesty requires a word about what we actually know. No autograph of the suites survives. The principal source is a manuscript copy made by Anna Magdalena, Bach's second wife, around 1727–31, and the Cöthen dating — placing the suites beside the violin *Sei Solo* in the instrumental golden age — is scholarly consensus rather than documented fact: inferred from style and circumstance, not from a dated title page. The music's authority is beyond dispute; its paper trail is thinner than we would like.

The suites teach the same lesson as the violin *[Sei Solo](sei-solo.md)*: a single line implying many voices, the listener's ear supplying the counterpoint the instrument cannot sustain. But here the lesson comes with a crucial difference — the bass register is included. A solo violin must imply its foundations; a solo cello *owns* them, so each suite is a complete harmonic world with no upstairs neighbor, self-sufficient from the ground up. The best way in is through the Preludes, where each suite's character is set before a single dance begins: the First's rolling arpeggios, pure harmony in motion; the Second's dark recitative; the Fifth's French-overture gravity, all dotted rhythms and ceremonial shadow. Learn the six Preludes and you hold the keys to six houses.

As craft, the set is a masterclass in something easy to miss in Bach's grander works: how thoroughly *dance* underlies his abstract thinking. Every movement here keeps its dance's meter, its tempo character, its step-feel — the body's memory of the ballroom — even at maximum contrapuntal sophistication. An allemande remains an allemande; a gigue still kicks. The craft essays lean on the sarabandes in particular as the clearest examples in all of Bach of harmony expressed through a single unaccompanied line: slow enough to hear the implied voices assemble, chord by chord, out of one melody.

The strangest fact about these six suites is that their fame is a twentieth-century event — and its origin story is one of the anchors of Arc III. In 1890, a thirteen-year-old cellist named Pablo [Casals](../../people/pablo-casals.md) found a battered copy of the suites in a secondhand shop in Barcelona. He practiced them for twelve years before daring to perform one in public, and recorded them in the 1930s, as war closed over Europe — an old man setting down music of order and consolation while the world unmade itself. Before Casals, the suites were exercises, student fodder shelved beside the scale books. After him, they were scripture: the solo cellist's rite of passage and the instrument's founding document. It is a Bach story in miniature — a masterwork waiting in plain sight for the performer who could hear what it was. Each suite has its own card, BWV 1007 through 1012.
