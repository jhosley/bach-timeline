**What it is.** Six dance suites for the least likely solo instrument of its day — the cello was the ensemble's bass servant, as the harpsichord was before Brandenburg No. 5; giving it an unaccompanied recital literature was the same act of emancipation. Every suite follows one floor plan: Prelude — Allemande — Courante — Sarabande — a pair of lighter *galanteries* (minuets, bourrées, or gavottes) — Gigue. Same skeleton six times; six utterly different worlds, rising in difficulty and depth from the amiable First to the radiant Sixth.

**Listen for.** The suites teach the same lesson as the violin *Sei Solo* — one line implying many voices — but with the bass register included, so each suite is a complete harmonic world with no upstairs neighbor. Start with each suite's **Prelude**, where the character is set: the First's rolling arpeggios, the Second's dark recitative, the Fifth's French-overture gravity.

**Craft.** A masterclass in how *dance* underlies Bach's abstract thinking: every movement keeps its dance's meter, tempo character, and step-feel even at maximum sophistication. The craft essays use the sarabandes as the clearest examples of harmony expressed through a single line.

**Reception thread.** The suites' fame is a twentieth-century event, and its origin story anchors Arc III: thirteen-year-old **Pablo Casals** finding a battered copy in a Barcelona secondhand shop in 1890, practicing them for twelve years before performing one in public, recording them in the 1930s as war closed over Europe. Before Casals they were exercises; after him, scripture. Individual cards: BWV 1007–1012.
