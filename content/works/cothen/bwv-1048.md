**What it is.** Nine string players in three choirs of three — no designated soloists at all. The concerto principle is dissolved into pure ensemble conversation: any player may momentarily lead, and the "solo group" is whichever trio has the ball.

**Listen for.** The first movement's material splitting and recombining among the three choirs — close listening reveals it is nearly all built from the opening bar's three-note turning figure, an object lesson in Bach's economy (one idea, total saturation; the same principle the Inventions teach). Then the famous puzzle: the "second movement" as notated is just **two chords** — a Phrygian cadence — which performers treat as anything from a bare two-chord hinge to a platform for an improvised violin or harpsichord fantasy. Bach expected *something* to happen there; he trusted his players. The finale is a whirling gigue-like perpetuum mobile.

**Craft.** The set's thesis stated most purely: the one-and-the-many question answered with radical equality. Its nine-part texture also makes it the Brandenburg most like a piece of counterpoint scaled up to orchestra.
