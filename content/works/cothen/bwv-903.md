**What it is.** The wildest keyboard piece Bach ever released into circulation: a Fantasia of sweeping scales, broken diminished-seventh cascades, and a long central **recitative** — the harpsichord impersonating a tragic opera singer, complete with speech rhythms and harmonic gasps — followed by a fugue on a slithering chromatic subject that keeps escalating to a massive octave-reinforced climax.

**Listen for.** The recitative section: instrumental music *talking*, decades before anyone coined "expressionist." And the harmony throughout — this is Bach's chromatic vocabulary (the voice-leading of pain: half-steps, diminished sevenths, remote modulations) at maximum concentration, the same language that saturates "Komm, süßes Kreuz" and the Crucifixus.

**Craft.** Prime evidence for what contemporaries said was untranscribable: Bach's **improvising**. Pieces like this are the notated shadow of what visitors heard him extemporize (recall Reincken in Hamburg, 1720). The improvisation essay pairs it with the *Musical Offering*'s ricercar — the other famous shadow of a Bach improvisation, from the end of the road.

**Reception thread.** A rare non-eclipse work: it stayed famous through the eighteenth century in manuscript, and the Romantics adored it — Mendelssohn performed it, Liszt taught it — precisely because it sounded like *them*. It was many nineteenth-century listeners' proof that the "learned" Bach had lava underneath.
