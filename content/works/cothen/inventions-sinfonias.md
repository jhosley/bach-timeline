Thirty short keyboard pieces — fifteen in two voices, called Inventions, and fifteen in three, called Sinfonias — arranged by ascending key. On the face of it, a modest teaching collection; in fact, one of the most consequential pedagogical documents in the history of music, because of what its title page promises. Bach's 1723 fair copy opens with the most complete statement of his teaching philosophy we possess: an "honest guide" by which lovers of the keyboard will learn "to play cleanly in two voices," then to manage "three obbligato parts," and along the way "to acquire good *inventiones*" — good musical ideas — "to develop them well, and above all to achieve a *cantabile* style of playing, and thereby gain a strong foretaste of *composition*."

Read that last clause again: a beginner's book that promises composition. Not finger exercises with composition as a distant reward for the talented few — composition as the stated goal of learning to play at all. For Bach, playing and composing were one literacy, the way reading and writing are one literacy; you would no more train a musician who could only execute than teach a child to read but never to write. The Inventions are that philosophy made into thirty graded lessons, and the collection had already been road-tested at home: earlier versions, titled Praeambula and Fantasias, appear in [Wilhelm Friedemann's Clavierbüchlein of 1720](../../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md), before the 1723 fair copy fixed the set in its final form.

The word in the title deserves its own pause. *Inventio* is a term borrowed from rhetoric, where invention did not mean making something from nothing — it meant *finding*: locating the core of your argument, the idea from which everything else could be drawn. That is exactly how these pieces work, and it is the single thing to listen for. Each one grows entirely from its opening idea. The C-major Invention takes a rising scale figure — and nothing else — and examines it from every angle: answered between the hands, turned upside down, chained into sequences, until a complete piece stands where one bar of material began. The F major runs the same experiment on a leaping fanfare. And the famous E-flat and F-minor Sinfonias show what the method can carry: three voices sustaining real pathos in under two minutes, expression achieved not in spite of the discipline but through it.

Within this catalog, the collection is the foundation stone of the whole Craft section. The second craft essay — the Inventions as a method — walks through precisely how one idea becomes a complete piece via imitation, inversion, and sequence, because those same operations, scaled up, are what build the fugues of the Well-Tempered Clavier and, at the far end of the line, [*The Art of Fugue*](../leipzig/art-of-fugue.md). This is the deepest continuity in Bach's instrumental thinking: the beginner playing the C-major Invention and the master engraving the final contrapuncti are doing the same thing at different magnitudes — finding an idea and developing it totally. Learn to hear it in two voices here, where the texture is transparent, and you will be able to follow it anywhere in Bach. Each of the thirty pieces has its own card, where the individual designs get their due.

The reception story is short, and unique on this timeline: there isn't one, in the usual sense. The Inventions and Sinfonias have never been out of pedagogical use — from 1723 to this morning, an unbroken chain of students at keyboards, generation after generation, working through the same thirty lessons. While the Passions slept and the cantatas scattered, this book stayed open on music stands. It is the rare Bach corpus with no eclipse to recover from, which gives it a quiet distinction: of all the routes by which Bach's mind reached the future, the humblest — a teaching book for beginners — is the one that never once broke.

*Where the lessons began at home: [the Clavierbüchlein for Wilhelm Friedemann, 1720](../../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md). Where the method ends: [The Art of Fugue](../leipzig/art-of-fugue.md).*
