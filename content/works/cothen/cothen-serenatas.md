**What it is.** The one regular *vocal* duty of the Cöthen years: congratulatory serenatas — festive secular cantatas of arias, duets, and recitatives on allegorical texts (Time, Fame, Fortune, and friends singing the prince's praises) — for Leopold's birthday each 10 December and for New Year's Day. [Anna Magdalena](../../people/anna-magdalena-bach.md), court soprano, sang in them; this music is likely how the Bachs met.

**Listen for.** Mostly, you can't — not in original form. The scores were occasional pieces, used once; what survives is their afterlife: back in Leipzig, needing music fast, Bach re-texted the best of them as church works. *Der Himmel dacht auf Anhalts Ruhm* (BWV 66a, birthday 1718) became the Easter cantata *Erfreut euch, ihr Herzen* (BWV 66); the New Year serenata BWV 134a became the Easter cantata BWV 134; *Durchlauchtster Leopold* (BWV 173a) became the Pentecost cantata BWV 173.

**Craft.** The timeline's introduction to **parody technique** — Bach's systematic re-texting of existing music for new purposes, which peaks with the [B minor Mass](../leipzig/b-minor-mass.md) and [Christmas Oratorio](../leipzig/christmas-oratorio.md). The scandal it presents to Romantic notions of sincerity (praise for a prince becoming praise for the risen Christ, unchanged music) dissolves once you grasp the era's view: well-made music was *craft object*, transferable to any worthy text — and studying what Bach altered in transfer is one of the best windows into what he thought music *means*.
