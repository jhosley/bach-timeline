**What it is.** The strangest sonority in the set: no violins at all. Two violas lead, shadowed by two violas da gamba (the fretted, soft-spoken older cousin of the cello — by 1721 already an antique), over cello and violone. The sound is dark honey; the register cap makes the whole piece feel lit from below.

**Listen for.** The opening: the two violas chase each other in strict **canon** (exact imitation at a fixed distance — here just half a beat apart), creating a shimmering, seasick pursuit over a repeating bass. It's the set's most audacious idea hiding in its plainest texture. The gambas — old instruments given easy parts — mostly murmur inner harmony; a persistent (undocumentable but plausible) tradition imagines Prince Leopold playing one of them alongside his Capelle. The finale is another gigue, mellow where No. 3's was fierce.

**Craft.** The canon craft essay uses this opening as its first orchestral example: strict imitation deployed not as learned display but as *texture* — you feel the tension before you ever know its name. Also the set's endpoint in the instrumentation survey: No. 1's maximal brilliance answered by voluntary twilight.
