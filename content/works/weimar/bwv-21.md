**What it is.** The biggest of the Weimar cantatas — two parts, eleven movements, framing the sermon — and Bach's own favorite tour piece: he labeled it *per ogni tempo* ("for any time"), carried it from post to post, and (plausibly) [showed it to Hamburg in 1720](../../events/cothen/hamburg-reincken-1720.md). A psalm-drama of affliction turned to praise: C minor to C major, engineered across forty minutes.

**Listen for.** The opening sinfonia's oboe-and-violin dialogue of pure sorrow; the choruses that made its fame (*Ich hatte viel Bekümmernis*, with its abrupt stop on "aber" — *but* — the theology hinging on one conjunction); the **dialogue movements** between the Soul (soprano) and Christ (bass) — opera's love-duet grammar sanctified, a form the chorale cantatas will perfect. The finale: *Das Lamm, das erwürget ist* — Revelation's throne-scene with trumpets, the young Bach unveiling the C-major blaze he'd withheld for half an hour.

**Craft.** Mattheson — the era's great Hamburg critic — mocked this very work's word-repetitions in print (the timeline's first documented *review* of Bach, 1725); the R4 rhetoric essay weighs the complaint honestly.

**Why it matters.** The portfolio cantata: what Bach himself chose to show strangers. Study it as his own answer to "what can you do?"
