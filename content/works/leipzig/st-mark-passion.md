On Good Friday 1731, at Vespers in the Thomaskirche, Leipzig heard Bach's third documented Passion. No one alive has heard it since. The music of the St. Mark Passion is lost — one of the major casualties of [the estate's dispersal](../../events/leipzig-3/death-1750.md) after Bach's death — and that is precisely why it belongs on this timeline. The date and the occasion are documented; the performance happened; a full Passion by Bach at the height of his Leipzig powers sounded in the Thomaskirche and then, at some point in the following decades, simply ceased to exist as music. Every other work card in the catalog invites you to listen; this one asks you to consider the silence around the listening, because the St. Mark is the most instructive absence in the whole corpus — instructive because, unusually for a lost work, we know a great deal about what we are missing.

Instructive, because the absence has a shape. What survives is the complete printed libretto by [Picander](../../people/picander.md), Bach's regular Leipzig collaborator, and from that text alone scholars can deduce a remarkable amount about the work's design — and about its economy. The libretto's metrical fingerprints reveal that the work was substantially parody, Bach's standing practice of fitting existing music with new words: the great frame-choruses and several of the arias were almost certainly drawn from the [Trauer-Ode](bwv-198.md) of 1727. The poetry's meters match that earlier music too closely for coincidence. And because those source movements survive, the St. Mark Passion occupies a strange middle state between existence and loss: not recoverable, but partially recoverable — a building whose stones still exist, scattered through other buildings, with only the floor plan to say where they once stood.

Hence the cottage industry of scholarly reconstructions, several of them recorded — Jordi Savall's among a number of rival versions — each one guessing differently at the recitatives and chorales that parody cannot supply. Listening to a reconstruction is its own distinct experience, and the card recommends it as an exercise. You will hear the Trauer-Ode's lute-and-gamba-shaded mourning re-aimed at Golgotha, grief composed for one occasion serving another. But listen also for the seams. Knowing that what you are hearing is a reconstruction, ask where the joins show — which passages carry the unmistakable weight of the surviving Bach movements, and which betray the reconstructor's educated guesswork. There is no better training for understanding what, exactly, is *Bach* in a Bach work: the exercise forces you to articulate what his music does that a skilled imitation does not.

The larger reason this card exists is statistical, and it should temper everything else the timeline says. The Obituary credits Bach with five Passions. Two survive whole. The St. Mark survives as a shadow — a libretto and a scatter of identifiable sources. The others are titles at best. And the Passions are not the exception: perhaps two-fifths of the cantatas are simply gone, along with untold other music. The corpus we study, analyze, and generalize about is the *surviving* corpus, shaped as much by the accidents of inheritance and custody as by anything Bach intended. The catalog carries this card as the standing reminder of that incomplete floor: every confident statement about "Bach's music" — its development, its habits, its range — is a statement about the fraction that happened to come down to us. Hold that in mind whenever the timeline speaks in generalities: the scholarship's confidence rests on a foundation with documented holes in it, and the St. Mark is the hole whose edges we can measure most precisely. That is the card's real function. It is a work card for a work that is present only as an outline — like the chalk trace of something that was certainly, documentedly there — and it stands in the catalog on behalf of everything that left no trace at all.
