**What it is.** Bach's third documented Passion — Good Friday 1731 — and the timeline's most instructive *absence*: the music is **lost**, one of the major casualties of [the estate's dispersal](../../events/leipzig-3/death-1750.md). What survives is Picander's complete printed libretto, and from it scholars deduce the work's design — and its economy: the metrical fingerprints show it was substantially **parody**, its great frame-choruses and several arias almost certainly drawn from the [Trauer-Ode](bwv-198.md) of 1727. Because those source movements survive, the Mark Passion is *partially recoverable* — hence the cottage industry of scholarly reconstructions (each guessing differently at recitatives and chorales), several recorded.

**Listen for** (in reconstruction). The Trauer-Ode's lute-and-gamba-shaded mourning re-aimed at Golgotha — and, as an exercise, the seams: knowing it's a reconstruction, ask where the joins show. There is no better training for understanding what, exactly, is *Bach* in a Bach work.

**Why it's on the timeline.** The Obituary credits Bach with **five Passions**; two survive whole, Mark survives as a shadow, and the others are titles at best. The catalog carries this card as the standing reminder that the corpus we study is the *surviving* corpus — perhaps two-fifths of the cantatas and most of the Passions are simply gone, and every generalization about "Bach's music" stands on that incomplete floor.
