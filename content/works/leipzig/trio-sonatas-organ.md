**What it is.** Six three-movement sonatas in which one organist must be a chamber trio: right hand one voice, left hand another, feet the bass — three lines of total independence, nothing doubled, nowhere to hide. Compiled, per Forkel, to finish **Friedemann's** training ([the Clavierbüchlein boy](../../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md), now nearing twenty) — and organists ever since have ranked them the most pitilessly difficult "easy-sounding" music in the repertoire.

**Listen for.** The paradox of register: galant, Italianate, *light* — singing slow movements, dancing finales — while being contrapuntally absolute. The E-flat sonata's opening makes the point in a minute: a melody, its answer, a bass, all catchable by ear, all inviolable. Nothing sounds learned; everything is.

**Craft.** The R4 trio-texture essay's keyboard anchor, beside [the violin-harpsichord sonatas](../cothen/violin-harpsichord-sonatas.md): three-voice thinking as Bach's core discipline — the Sinfonias' promise ([15 three-part pieces](../cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md)) delivered at journeyman-final level. The family pedagogy thread's capstone document.

**Why it matters.** What "finishing school" meant in the Bach household: not repertoire but *transparency* — the ability to make three simultaneous truths sound like ease. Friedemann became the greatest organist of the next generation; this was the diploma.
