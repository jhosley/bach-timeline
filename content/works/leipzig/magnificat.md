The Magnificat is Mary's canticle set in twelve movements that average under three minutes each, and that arithmetic is the key to the whole work. This is Bach's large-choral style at its most concentrated: no recitatives, no chorales, no da capo longueurs — none of the machinery that gives the Passions and cantatas their expansive pacing. Every verse of the canticle gets a distinct, complete musical design, stated and finished before it can outstay a single idea. Because no movement lasts long enough to coast, nothing coasts: the work moves from full festival chorus to intimate solo and back with a swiftness the bigger works never attempt, and the effect on a first hearing is of Bach's entire vocal art passing in review at double speed. For five-part choir, festival orchestra, and five soloists, written for Vespers on the high feasts, it compresses everything Bach's big vocal works do into half an hour — which is precisely why this card recommends it as the place to start.

Two versions survive, and the pair is a study in itself. The original, in E-flat, was written for Christmas 1723 — Bach's first Christmas in Leipzig — with four seasonal Christmas movements interpolated into the canticle. About a decade later, around 1733, he revised it into the familiar D-major version: the Christmas insertions gone, freeing the work for Vespers on any high feast; the trumpets happier in their new key; details polished throughout. Because both autographs survive, this is the catalog's cleanest two-version revision case — E-flat and D side by side, the same work before and after a decade of second thoughts, with every change legible.

What to listen for, above all, is text-painting deployed as structure rather than decoration. Bach does not merely illustrate the words; he builds each movement's entire design out of its verse's central image. In *omnes generationes*, "all generations," the fugal entries pile in on top of one another until the texture becomes an overlapping crowd — all generations, audibly arriving at once, the counterpoint itself enacting the multitude. In *deposuit potentes*, the mighty are hurled *down* the scale, while *exaltavit* lifts the humble upward — the verse's great reversal drawn directly in melodic line. In *Suscepit Israel*, trebles float above the ancient Magnificat chant intoned by oboes: the old church singing inside the new, plainchant carried like a relic within Bach's modern texture, at the verse about God remembering his promise to Israel. Each device is the form of its movement, not an ornament on it.

And then the ending, which every composer since has envied. The final verse of the doxology is *Sicut erat in principio* — "as it was in the beginning" — and Bach sets it by literally restating the opening music. The text describes return; the music returns. It is the perfect pun: theology, form, and wit collapsed into a single decision, the work's architecture closing like a well-made door, and the listener who catches it gets the rare pleasure of hearing a joke and a profundity in the same instant. Nothing in the work better captures its character — maximum meaning per minute, delivered with the lightest possible touch — and nothing better rewards arriving at the ending with the opening still fresh in your ear, which the work's brevity conveniently guarantees.

The Magnificat's role in this catalog is the study-sized door to the big choral works. Essentially everything that happens in the [Mass in B minor](b-minor-mass.md) happens here at one-quarter length: the festival choruses, the intimate solo movements, the archaic chant embedded in modern polyphony, the architectural framing. A listener who absorbs these thirty minutes closely — and thirty minutes is genuinely enough — arrives at the two-hour summa already knowing its vocabulary. That is the card's standing advice: if you take one large vocal work into close analysis first, take this one. Thirty minutes, every technique, no filler.
