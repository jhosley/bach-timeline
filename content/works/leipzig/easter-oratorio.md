**What it is.** The odd one out among the oratorios: **no Evangelist, no Gospel narration** — instead four named characters (Peter, John, the two Marys) racing to the empty tomb and reacting in aria. It began, weeks earlier, as a pastoral birthday entertainment for the Duke of Weissenfels — shepherds and shepherdesses re-costumed as apostles at speed (Easter 1725 fell during the chorale-cantata cycle's most punishing stretch; parody was survival).

**Listen for.** The opening pair — a trumpet-blazing sinfonia, then an adagio oboe lament, then the racing duet-turned-chorus *Kommt, eilet und laufet* ("come, hurry, and run" — the disciples' footrace made music): festival splendor with a smile in it. The tenor's *Sanfte soll mein Todeskummer* over recorder-cushioned strings — death as slumber — is one of Bach's gentlest arias anywhere.

**Craft.** Study it beside [the Christmas Oratorio](christmas-oratorio.md) as the parody workshop's *quick* mode versus its planned mode — and note what the operatic character-form (his one quasi-dramatic sacred work) does and doesn't permit him.

**Why it matters.** Proof the sacred-secular membrane ran both directions on short notice — and a reminder that the Leipzig Easter of 1725, which also produced the second version of [the John Passion](st-john-passion.md), packed more first-rate music into one week than most careers.
