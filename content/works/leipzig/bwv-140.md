**What it is.** The late, perfect chorale cantata — written for a Sunday so deep in the church year it almost never occurs (the 27th after Trinity requires a very early Easter; Bach's Leipzig calendar produced it exactly twice). The Gospel: the midnight cry, the wise and foolish virgins, the Bridegroom's arrival — and Nicolai's great "king of chorales" to match.

**Listen for.** Three immortal movements on one tune. The opening fantasia: the watchmen's dotted march below, the hymn ringing in the sopranos above, and a syncopated orchestral fabric that seems to stride and dance at once. The central movement: tenors singing the hymn's second verse against a **unison string countermelody** of such perfect independence that Bach himself published it later for organ ([the Schübler chorales](../../events/leipzig-2/clavier-ubung.md)) — two tunes, complete alone, perfect together: counterpoint's whole promise in four minutes. Between them, the Soul-and-Christ **love duets** (with violino piccolo halo) — the Song of Songs grammar at its most operatic and most chaste.

**Craft.** The R4 chorale essay's culminating example: fantasia, cantus-firmus-with-countermelody, and plain harmonization — the three canonical ways to set a hymn, all in one work, each definitive.

**Why it matters.** If the cantatas had to be represented by one work in a time capsule, the vote usually lands here.
