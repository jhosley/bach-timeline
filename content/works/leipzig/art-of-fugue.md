The Art of Fugue is fourteen fugues and four canons, every one of them grown from a single plain D-minor subject. That is the entire premise, and its radicalism is easy to miss for sounding so dry: one unremarkable theme, stated at the outset, and then every species of fugal thought applied to it in ascending order of difficulty — simple fugues first; then counter-fugues built on the subject's inversion, the theme turned upside down and made to generate its own counterpoint; then double and triple fugues combining it with new subjects; then the mirror fugues, in which an entire fugue is answered by its own exact reflection, every voice inverted, two complete pieces standing to each other as a body to its image in still water. The strictness ratchets movement by movement, each contrapunctus raising the constraint and refusing to sound constrained — until the quadruple summit, unfinished in the sources, where a third subject enters spelling B–A–C–H, the composer signing his name into the fabric of the final fugue, and the music stops mid-air. [The event card](../../events/leipzig-3/art-of-fugue-begun.md) tells the biography — the autograph of about 1742, the expansion for engraving in the last years, the posthumous publication of 1751; this card is about how to listen.

Heard as a journey, the cycle has clear waypoints. Contrapunctus 1 gives you the subject bare and the rules stated: this is the world, these are its laws. Contrapunctus 4 lets the inversion sing on its own, the upside-down theme revealed as a melody in its own right. Contrapunctus 9 is the racing double fugue everyone loves first — the movement to play for someone who suspects the work is a textbook. Contrapunctus 11 is the triple fugue's chromatic storm, the cycle's expressive extreme, where the discipline generates not dryness but vertigo. The mirror pair, 12 and 13, should be heard back to back, both halves in sequence; there is no better way to teach your ear what "inverted world" means than to hear a whole fugue and then its reflection. And then Contrapunctus 14, the B–A–C–H fugue — and the silence where the rest should be.

There is also the question of what the work should sound like at all, because Bach left it in open score: four voices on four staves, no instrument named. The notation is playable — and surely meant — at the keyboard, and one mind at one keyboard, all four voices under ten fingers, is the scholarly consensus for the work's home. But the open score *invites* the question rather than settling it, and three centuries of answers are now part of the work: string quartet or viols, which embody the four voices as four breathing players; even full orchestra. Choosing a recording means choosing an answer to the instrument-question, and comparing answers — the same contrapunctus as keyboard soliloquy and as string conversation — is one of the pleasures the work uniquely offers.

Within the Craft section, The Art of Fugue is the fugue essay's final chapter, and more than that: it is the section's whole thesis at terminal scale. The idea that a single subject, rigorously developed, can yield a complete musical world begins in this catalog with the [two-voice Inventions](../cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) — one idea, total development, for beginners. This is the same principle carried to the end of the line: one idea, total development, for the discipline itself. No occasion called for it; no patron ordered it. The occasion was fugue.

The reception history is the timeline's pattern at its starkest. The 1751 publication sold roughly thirty copies, and the engraved plates were nearly melted down for their copper — the summa of Western counterpoint, almost recycled by weight. Then the twentieth century reversed the verdict completely, making the work a monument of pure mind: the emblem of Bach-as-structure, order without occasion, that Arc III's modernists claimed for their own. The unfinished final page, breaking off just after the composer's name enters the counterpoint, became the most eloquent silence in music — not because Bach planned it, but because history did.
