The Mass in B minor is the complete Latin Mass Ordinary at maximal scale — two hours of music, twenty-seven movements, for five-part choir expanding to eight, festival orchestra, and soloists. It was assembled in Bach's last working years, 1748–49, but "composed" is the wrong verb for most of it: Bach built the Mass from the finest sacred music of his whole life, re-texted and perfected — the Missa he had presented at Dresden in 1733, the Sanctus of 1724, movements parodied from across three decades, the earliest source reaching back to 1714. [The event card](../../events/leipzig-3/b-minor-mass-1749.md) tells the biographical story; what matters here is the strangeness of the object itself. A Latin Mass of this scale fit no service Bach could attend, and no occasion for it is documented. He never heard it whole; no one did, in his lifetime. It is the summa — a lifetime of sacred music gathered, judged, and set in order by its own composer.

Start listening at the very beginning, because the opening teaches you how the whole work moves. Five bars of B-minor cry — *Kyrie* — and then a vast fugue that spends eleven minutes earning that cry. Gesture first, then the labor that justifies it: the Mass repeatedly announces its intentions in a stroke and then builds the cathedral behind the facade.

The grand design is an architecture of styles. Renaissance *stile antico* — the old church polyphony, in the Credo's opening and the *Confiteor*, where a plainchant runs in canon beneath the counterpoint — stands beside high-Baroque concerto choruses like the *Gloria* and *Et resurrexit*, which stand beside duets in the most modern galant manner. This is not stylistic inconsistency; it is curation. Bach lays out sacred music's whole history, from the ancient chant to the fashion of his sons' generation, as a deliberate museum — and demonstrates command of every room in it.

At the center sits the sequence every first-time listener should wait for. The *Crucifixus* is a chaconne over a grinding chromatic ground bass — music [born in Weimar in 1714](../weimar/bwv-12.md) as a cantata chorus, carried thirty-five years, and placed here at the heart of the Credo. At its end the music sinks into E minor, the key of burial, the voices lowering the body — and out of that lowest point *Et resurrexit* detonates, without a breath between. No transition, no preparation: burial, then blaze. It is the most theatrical single moment in a work that never once goes to the theater.

Then the ending, which is the work's thesis stated in music. The final movement, *Dona nobis pacem* — grant us peace — re-uses the fugue of the *Gratias* — we give thanks. The Mass closes by giving thanks and asking peace with the same music, and once you know it, the gesture reframes everything before it: the parody principle, Bach's lifelong practice of setting new words to existing music, elevated at the last into theology. Gratitude and supplication are one act; the music that serves one serves the other, because for Bach the notes were never bound to a single occasion.

Every craft essay on this timeline lands here eventually, because every technique does: fugue at all scales, canon in the *Confiteor*'s chant, chorale-thinking in a work with no chorales, parody as transfiguration rather than economy, and the questions of number and symmetry in its proportions — debates the essays treat soberly, without mysticism. The Mass is where the threads knot.

Its afterlife began in fragments. Carl Philipp Emanuel performed the Credo in 1786; the nineteenth century took up the whole and built it into a monument — "the greatest work of music of all ages and all peoples," announced the Swiss publisher Nägeli when offering the first edition. Extravagant as the claim was, it stuck. Today the Mass in B minor is the work by which everything else gets measured — including the rest of Bach.
