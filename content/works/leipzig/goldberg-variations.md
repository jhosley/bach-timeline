**What it is.** An Aria and thirty variations on its **bass** ([the event card](../../events/leipzig-3/goldberg-1741.md) has the publication story and the insomnia legend): the ground-bass principle scaled to an eighty-minute planetarium. The clockwork: variations in **threes** — a character piece, a virtuoso two-manual "arabesque," and a **canon**, the canons ascending by interval (unison, second, third… ninth) as the wheel turns; at the center (variation 16) a French overture restarts the machine; at 25, the great minor-key adagio (harpsichordist Wanda Landowska's "black pearl") — chromatic grief at the geometric heart; at 30, [the quodlibet's](../../events/eisenach/the-bach-clan.md) folk-song jokes; then the Aria again, unchanged and utterly changed.

**Listen for, first pass.** Don't track the schema — track the *bass*: hum the Aria's bassline until you own it, then hear it wearing thirty costumes. Second pass: the canons only, in order — strictness tightening while sounding ever freer. Third: 15, 21, 25 — the three minor-key variations as the work's hidden tragedy.

**Craft.** Every R4 thread converges: ground bass ([Chaconne](../cothen/bwv-1004.md), [Passacaglia](../weimar/bwv-582.md)), canon ([BWV 769](bwv-769.md)), dance, variation-as-world-building.

**Reception thread.** Beethoven's Diabelli answers it; and in 1955 a 22-year-old Canadian's debut recording ([Gould — Arc III's centerpiece](../../eras/living-bach.md)) made it, improbably, a bestseller and the modern piano's sacred mountain.
