## The strangest assignment in music

Every tradition of teaching harmony on earth — conservatory, university, YouTube — eventually hands the student the same assignment: **harmonize a chorale in four parts, in the style of Bach.** It's worth pausing on how strange that is. Music theory is supposedly a general discipline, describing how all tonal music works, from Monteverdi to Motown. Yet its universal entrance exam is astonishingly specific: take a German hymn tune from the sixteenth or seventeenth century, put it in the top voice, and write three lines beneath it — the way one particular Lutheran cantor did it, for one particular congregation, in one particular corner of Saxony, three hundred years ago. No other composer's habits have been promoted into a grammar this way. Nobody harmonizes "in the style of Haydn" as a rite of passage; nobody's first counterpoint exercise is "in the style of Josquin" outside specialist courses. Bach's chorales are the Latin of tonal music: the dead-language drill through which every living speaker still passes.

This essay is about why that happened — and why, for Bach himself, the chorale was even more than a teaching vehicle: raw material, structural steel, shared language, and classroom, all in one. The word *chorale* itself means two things, and the double meaning is the essay's map. First, a chorale is **a hymn**: a melody with a German sacred text, made for an entire congregation to sing together. Second, a chorale is **a setting of that hymn**: most famously Bach's four-part harmonizations, but also organ pieces, variation sets, and vast choral movements built on the same tunes. The first meaning belongs to history and worship; the second belongs to craft. Bach's achievement was to fuse them — to take the most public, most communal melodies in his culture and make them the load-bearing structure of the most sophisticated music in Europe. To understand how, start with where the tunes came from.

## Luther's singing church

The chorale is a child of the Reformation, and specifically of Martin Luther's conviction that the congregation should not merely *hear* worship but *perform* it. Medieval Catholic practice had concentrated liturgical music in the hands of trained clergy and choirs, singing largely in Latin — a language most worshippers could not understand. Luther, himself a capable musician who loved and defended the art, wanted the whole assembly singing the faith in its own tongue. Beginning in 1524, he and his collaborators — above all the composer Johann Walter — issued the first Lutheran **hymnals**: printed collections of vernacular sacred songs, some with simple polyphonic settings for choirs to lead the people. (A *hymn* is a strophic sacred song — the same music repeated for each stanza of text; a *hymnal* is the book that collects them.) Luther wrote texts and, by strong tradition, melodies of his own, including the movement's anthem, *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott* — "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God" — [which Bach would one day monumentalize](../works/leipzig/bwv-80.md).

Where did the melodies come from? From everywhere, which is part of their strength. Some were adapted from Latin plainchant, the unaccompanied liturgical melody of the medieval church: *Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland* ("Now come, Savior of the Gentiles") is a German reworking of the ancient Advent hymn *Veni redemptor gentium*, and it kept the chant's austere, archaic contour — you can hear the pre-tonal world in it. Some were **contrafacta**: existing secular songs given new sacred words. Heinrich Isaac's beloved farewell song *Innsbruck, ich muss dich lassen* ("Innsbruck, I must leave you") became *O Welt, ich muss dich lassen* ("O world, I must leave you") — the leave-taking transferred from a city to life itself. Most famously, a love song published by Hans Leo Hassler in 1601 became the melody Lutherans knew as *Herzlich tut mich verlangen* and later wedded to the text *O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden* ("O sacred head, now wounded") — the tune we now call the **Passion chorale**, which will matter enormously below. And some melodies were newly composed for the church, like Philipp Nicolai's two great tunes of 1599, *Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme* and *Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern*, traditionally nicknamed the king and queen of chorales.

Many of these tunes share a distinctive architecture inherited from the German song tradition of the Minnesingers and Meistersingers: **bar form**, a pattern the theorists label AAB. The opening musical phrase-group (the *Stollen*) is sung, then repeated with new words, and then a longer concluding section (the *Abgesang*) rounds off the stanza with new music. Sing *Ein feste Burg* or *Wachet auf* and you'll feel it: statement, restatement, and a freer conclusion. Many tunes also predate the modern major-minor system and carry traces of the older church **modes** — the medieval scale-types with names like Dorian and Phrygian, whose patterns of whole and half steps differ from our major and minor scales. When Bach harmonizes a modal melody, he must negotiate between the tune's antique logic and his own era's tonal grammar, and some of his strangest, most haunting harmonic moments come from exactly that negotiation.

Now the crucial social fact, the one to keep loaded whenever you study this music. Luther's Reformation ([this timeline's Eisenach inheritance](../events/eisenach/luthers-town.md)) put vernacular song at the center of worship, and by Bach's day — two full centuries on — Lutheran Germany owned a repertoire of hundreds of these tunes that everyone, literally everyone, knew by heart from childhood: sung weekly in church, daily in school, at home, at weddings, at deathbeds. Each melody arrived welded to its text, its season, its occasions. The chorales were the **shared memory bank of an entire civilization**, and quoting one was like quoting scripture or proverb — instantly recognized, arriving with its words and associations attached even when played without them. No composer will ever have that resource again. Bach did not invent his most powerful musical material; he inherited it, pre-installed in every listener he would ever write for.

## Anatomy of a harmonization

Now to the second meaning of *chorale*: the four-part setting. The standard texture is **SATB** — soprano, alto, tenor, bass, the four traditional voice-types from highest to lowest. The hymn melody sits in the soprano, where the congregation can follow it; the other three voices move essentially in the same rhythm beneath, a texture called **homophony** (all voices changing together, as opposed to the staggered independence of [counterpoint](craft-01-counterpoint.md)). This melody-on-top arrangement, standardized around 1600 in Lutheran publications, is sometimes called the **cantional style**. Bach wrote hundreds of such settings, mostly as the closing movements of his **cantatas** — the multi-movement works for voices and instruments he supplied for Leipzig's Sunday services: the congregation's tune, returned to them at the sermon's end, in harmony finer than they could make but singable in spirit by all.

What actually happens inside those four lines? Begin with the frame. A Bach chorale is governed by its **outer voices**: the soprano carries the given melody, and the bass is composed as a genuine second melody against it — not a plodding root-of-the-chord bass but a singing line with direction, variety, and its own small dramas. Bach's basses stride, leap purposefully at cadences, walk in smooth steps elsewhere, and frequently move in **contrary motion** to the soprano (when the tune rises, the bass falls, and vice versa), which keeps the texture open and the counterpoint honest. Generations of teachers have distilled the lesson: get the soprano-bass duet right and the harmony almost writes itself; get it wrong and no amount of inner-voice cleverness will save you.

The alto and tenor — the **inner voices** — then fill the space between, and here live the norms every harmony student drills. Voices keep to comfortable singing ranges and stay out of each other's registral territory. Each chord's notes are distributed so that some tone is **doubled** (sung by two of the four voices, since a triad has only three distinct pitches) — normally the chord's root, almost never its **leading tone**, the scale's seventh degree, whose whole character is a magnetic pull upward to the tonic that doubling would fatally blur. Voices move to the nearest available chord tone rather than leaping about. And two voices must not move in **parallel fifths or octaves** — sliding together at those intervals, which momentarily welds two independent lines into one and collapses the four-part illusion. All of this is what the term **voice-leading** means: the craft of moving each voice from chord to chord so that every line remains smooth, singable, and independent. The rules are not etiquette; each one protects the texture's core promise, that four real melodies are happening at once.

Onto this smooth chord-to-chord frame Bach then layers the small dissonant events that make the settings live. A **passing tone** fills the gap between two chord tones a third apart, creating a brief, stepwise clash; Bach's chorales are famously saturated with them, the inner voices and bass constantly streaming in eighth-notes between the beats. A **neighbor tone** steps away from a chord tone and back again, an ornamental shiver. A **suspension** — the queen of dissonances, inherited from Renaissance counterpoint — holds a voice over while the harmony changes beneath it, creating a clash that must then resolve downward by step: tension, ache, release, in three beats. An **anticipation** arrives at the next chord's note a moment early, a small eagerness especially common in soprano lines at cadences. Strip these away and a Bach chorale becomes a correct, dull chord sequence; they are the difference between harmony as bookkeeping and harmony as speech.

Every phrase of the tune then ends in a **cadence** — a formulaic harmonic arrival that functions as musical punctuation — and the chorales are a complete taxonomy of them. The **authentic cadence** (the chord on the fifth scale degree, the dominant, resolving to the tonic chord — V to I) is the full stop; in its *perfect* form, with both bass and soprano landing on the keynote, it closes the piece. The **half cadence** pauses *on* the dominant — a comma, an unanswered question. The **deceptive cadence** sets up V and then sidesteps to the sixth degree instead of home — the harmonic equivalent of a sentence that swerves before its final word, which Bach saves for moments where the text itself withholds resolution. The **plagal cadence** (IV to I, the "Amen" formula) and the antique **Phrygian cadence** (a half cadence approached by a falling half-step in the bass, common in minor and instantly archaic-sounding) round out the set. Minor-key chorales often end with a **Picardy third**: the final tonic chord turned unexpectedly major, a lightening of the last sonority that listeners since the Renaissance have heard as a ray through clouds. And over each phrase-ending sits a **fermata** — the bird's-eye sign that in most music means "hold this note," but in chorale notation usually just marks the phrase-end and the congregation's breath.

Here is the essential thing: for Bach, every one of these choices is **expressive, and usually textual**. The chorales are not neutral exercises; they are readings of words. Where the stanza speaks of sin, death, or grief, the harmony darkens with **chromaticism** — notes from outside the prevailing key, bending lines by half-steps — and with **secondary dominants**, chords borrowed to point momentarily toward other keys, so that the music seems to lean out of its frame ([Arnstadt's consistory complained about exactly these instincts](../events/arnstadt/consistory-rebukes-1706.md) when the young organist's hymn accompaniments bewildered the congregation). The extreme case is the setting of *Es ist genug* ("It is enough") in the cantata *O Ewigkeit, du Donnerwort* BWV 60, whose melody opens with three rising whole steps — an interval stack so alien to the tonal system that Alban Berg, two centuries later, lifted Bach's entire harmonization into his Violin Concerto as music for a death. And the everyday case is comparison: set Bach's several harmonizations of the *same* tune side by side — the Passion chorale's five appearances [in the St. Matthew](../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md), each darker than the last — and you are watching harmony *mean* something: same melody, different griefs.

## The accidental textbook

Bach never assembled his chorales into a book or wrote a word of theory about them. The textbook was made by his students and sons, and it was made because the settings were already legendary among connoisseurs as models of harmony. After [the estate was dispersed at his death](../events/eclipse/estate-dispersal-1750.md), his second son Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach gathered the four-part settings out of the cantata scores — settings whose parent works were in many cases already lost, so that the collection is our *only* source for dozens of them. A first, partial publication appeared in the 1760s; then, in 1784–87, the Leipzig publisher Breitkopf issued the collection in four volumes, **371 settings** in all, with C. P. E. Bach commending them as models for students of composition. His father's pupil Johann Philipp Kirnberger, who had lobbied for the publication for years, was meanwhile building Bach's practice into his own influential theory teaching in Berlin. The chorales thus entered the world already framed as pedagogy — and they never left. Through nineteenth-century conservatories, twentieth-century harmony textbooks, and the modern editions on every theory student's shelf, the same corpus has remained the common training ground, [even as the rest of Bach's music went into eclipse and returned](../events/eclipse/the-sons-era.md).

Why these pieces, though? Why did the discipline of harmony organize itself around forty-second hymn settings rather than around sonatas or symphonies? Because a Bach chorale is **maximum harmonic content at minimum scale**. Four voices, four to six phrases, under a minute — and inside: every cadence type, every standard dissonance, secondary dominants, modulations to nearby keys, modal shadows, the occasional breathtaking chromatic swerve. Each setting is a complete, graded harmony lesson that also happens to be a finished work of art. The corpus is large enough (hundreds of settings) to reveal *norms* — what Bach does ninety percent of the time, which becomes "the rules" — and rich enough in exceptions to teach *judgment*, because Bach breaks every textbook rule somewhere, always for an audible reason. That inversion is worth underlining: the rules were not a code Bach obeyed but a description later teaching distilled *from* him. When your workbook forbids parallel fifths, it is not citing an edict from 1725; it is summarizing the practice of these 371 pieces.

There is also a documented family link between the chorales and Bach's own classroom. C. P. E. Bach's account of his father's teaching describes the method: pupils began not with abstract rules but with four-part **thoroughbass** — the realization of harmony above a bass line — and moved directly to chorales, at first adding only the alto and tenor to a melody and bass Bach supplied, then graduating to composing basses themselves. That is, the universal modern assignment — fill in the inner voices, then harmonize the whole tune — *is* Bach's own assignment, preserved like an insect in amber. When a first-year student today wrestles an alto line into place, she is sitting in the Thomaskantor's parlor. It is hard to name another living pedagogical practice with an unbroken three-century pedigree — and closely related, the shorthand of **figured bass** (numerals below a bass note indicating which intervals to play above it), which Bach's continuo players read at sight, survives in the way theorists still label chord inversions.

## The chorale scaled up

Settings, though, were only the chorale's smallest job in Bach's workshop. The same tunes served him as **cantus firmus** — the old term (literally "fixed song") for a pre-existing melody used as the structural skeleton of a larger composition, often stretched into long, slow notes while faster music is woven around it. Ascend by scale and you can watch one tune-repertoire power an entire hierarchy of forms.

Smallest: the **chorale prelude**, an organ piece built on a hymn tune, originally serving to introduce the melody before the congregation sang it. [The Orgelbüchlein](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md) — Bach's Weimar "Little Organ Book," a plan of over 160 chorales of which he completed forty-six — perfects the *melody chorale* type: the tune presented once, whole and recognizable in the top voice, while beneath it a single invented accompanying figure interprets the text like a gloss in a margin — falling sevenths under the Fall of Adam, tolling pedal-strokes under mortality, wave upon wave of joy-figures under Christmas. A sibling type, the *ornamental* or **coloratura chorale**, does the opposite: it dissolves the melody itself into elaborate, aria-like embellishment, the tune present but veiled in expressive filigree — the great examples live in the [Great Eighteen](../works/leipzig/great-eighteen.md), the Leipzig-era collection in which the old Bach revised and monumentalized his Weimar chorale settings. The **chorale partita** ([Böhm's legacy](../people/georg-boehm.md) from Bach's Lüneburg years) runs the tune through variation after variation, one per stanza. The late [Schübler Chorales](../works/leipzig/schuebler-chorales.md) reverse-engineer the process, transcribing cantata movements — including the *Wachet auf* tenor setting — back onto the organ. And at the far end of learnedness, the [Canonic Variations on *Vom Himmel hoch*](../works/leipzig/bwv-769.md) subject a Christmas tune to strict canon at every interval, the chorale as proof of contrapuntal mastery for a learned society.

Scaled up to civic size: the **chorale fantasia**, the crowning invention of [the 1724–25 cantata cycle](../events/leipzig-1/cantata-machine.md). In these vast opening choruses the congregation's tune sounds as a cantus firmus in long notes — usually in the sopranos — while an independent orchestral and choral world is built around it: [BWV 20's French overture](../works/leipzig/bwv-20.md), the dread of eternity dressed as a royal procession; [BWV 78's lament-chaconne](../works/leipzig/bwv-78.md), the hymn riding a repeating ground bass of grief; [BWV 140's watchmen-march](../works/leipzig/bwv-140.md), the midnight call swinging in dotted rhythms. The chorale cantata cycle itself — roughly forty works in Bach's second Leipzig year, each built on a single hymn, its first stanza as fantasia, its inner stanzas paraphrased into arias and recitatives, its last stanza as plain four-part close — is the most systematic compositional project of his life. And the *per omnes versus* design ("through all the verses," [BWV 4, *Christ lag in Todes Banden*](../works/muehlhausen/bwv-4.md)) commits an entire work to a single hymn with *no* paraphrase at all: every movement sets one stanza verbatim, each with a different technique, the whole cantata a set of variations on one tune. One melody-repertoire, five or six structural technologies — the chorale was to Bach's vocal music what the ground bass was to his instrumental thinking: **the fixed point that makes invention legible**. However wild the surrounding music becomes, the listener always has the thread.

In the passions, the chorale takes on its most dramatic role: **the congregation's voice inside the story**. Between the Gospel narrative and the operatic arias, [the St. John](../works/leipzig/st-john-passion.md) and [St. Matthew Passions](../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md) repeatedly pause for a plain four-part chorale — familiar tune, familiar words — in which the assembled church, in effect, steps into the Passion and answers it: Peter denies Christ, and the chorale responds with the believer's own confession of weakness. Whether or not the Leipzig congregation ever sang along (scholars doubt it), the chorales *are* them — the first person plural of the whole work. And for those first audiences, all of this carried a dimension we must reconstruct deliberately: **recognition**. When [BWV 127's orchestra threads the Passion chorale under a different hymn](../works/leipzig/bwv-127.md), or the [Christmas Oratorio opens its cradle-scenes with the same melody that will bleed at Golgotha](../works/leipzig/christmas-oratorio.md), Leipzig *heard the allusion* the way you catch a film score quoting its own love-theme at the deathbed. Studying the cantatas without knowing the core chorales is studying poetry in a language whose proverbs you've never heard. The practical remedy is almost absurdly simple, and it opens the next section.

## How to work with the chorales

Everything above converges on a study method, and its first step costs nothing but singing. **Learn ten tunes.** Sing them until they're yours: *O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden*, *Wachet auf*, *Nun komm der Heiden Heiland* ([Advent's door into the cantatas](../works/weimar/bwv-61.md)), *Ein feste Burg*, *Christ lag in Todes Banden*, [*Wie schön leuchtet*](../works/leipzig/bwv-1.md), [*Jesu, meine Freude*](../works/leipzig/bwv-227.md), *Was Gott tut*, *O Ewigkeit, du Donnerwort*, *Vom Himmel hoch*. Melody first, then at least one stanza of text (a translation beside the German is fine). This is not a warm-up; it is the core act. You are installing the same memory bank Bach's listeners carried, and a third of this timeline's vocal catalog opens like a door you'd been pushing instead of pulling.

Then work with the four-part settings themselves, in a graded sequence — each pass through a chorale asking one question only:

- **Play and sing.** Play a setting slowly at the keyboard (or sing one line while a recording carries the rest). Feel where the phrases breathe at the fermatas. Before any analysis, mark each cadence by ear: full stop, comma, or swerve?
- **Name the punctuation.** Label every cadence — authentic, half, deceptive, plagal, Phrygian — and note which keys the phrases visit. A chorale's cadence-plan is its plot summary.
- **Circle the dissonances.** Find every note that doesn't belong to its chord and name it: passing tone, neighbor, suspension, anticipation. Watch how each is approached and left. This is where the settings' expressive life is hiding.
- **Extract the frame.** Copy out just the soprano and bass on two staves and study them as a duet: the contrary motion, the bass's mixture of steps and purposeful leaps. This duet is the chorale's skeleton and the single most transferable lesson in the corpus.
- **Do full harmonic analysis.** Add **Roman numeral analysis** — the standard labeling system in which chords are named by the scale degree of their root (I, IV, V, vi and so on) — including inversions and secondary dominants. Now the whole grammar is on the page.
- **Compose, then compare.** Take a tune Bach set several times, harmonize it yourself, then set your version beside two or three of his. The comparison will humble and teach you in equal measure — notice *where* your versions agree (the cadences, mostly) and where his diverge (the inner life, the bass's ambitions, the one chromatic chord placed exactly under the stanza's heaviest word).
- **Return the chorale to its context.** Finally, find your tune at work in the larger forms: in an [Orgelbüchlein](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md) prelude, a chorale fantasia, [a passion](../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md). Hear the same melody as lesson, as gloss, as architecture, and as communal speech.

And for the composer himself? The chorale was where his art and [his stated purpose](../events/muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md) — *a well-regulated church music to the glory of God* — fused most completely: the one place where the highest craft in Europe and the voice of the whole congregation were, by construction, the same music. The four-part chorale is [Bach's counterpoint](craft-01-counterpoint.md) democratized — everyone gets a real line, and the lines together make [the faith audible](craft-06-theology.md). That, more than pedagogy, is why it never left the classroom: it remains the smallest complete model of what he thought music was *for*.

## Terms of Art

*Definitions for every technical term used in this essay, assuming no prior knowledge.*

- **Anticipation** — a dissonance in which one voice arrives at its note for the *next* chord slightly early, sounding it against the current chord before the harmony catches up. Common in soprano lines at cadences.
- **Authentic cadence** — the strongest harmonic full stop: the dominant chord (built on the fifth scale degree, V) resolving to the tonic chord (I). Called *perfect* when the soprano and bass both land on the keynote, *imperfect* otherwise.
- **Bar form** — the AAB stanza design of many chorale tunes, inherited from medieval German song: an opening phrase-group (the *Stollen*) is sung twice with different words, followed by a longer, freer conclusion (the *Abgesang*).
- **Cadence** — a formulaic harmonic arrival at the end of a phrase, functioning as musical punctuation — full stop, comma, or question mark, depending on type.
- **Cantata** — a multi-movement work for singers and instruments; in Bach's Leipzig, the roughly twenty-five-minute piece performed in the Sunday service, its text tied to the day's Gospel reading.
- **Cantional style** — the standard chorale texture from around 1600: the hymn melody in the top voice with chords moving beneath it in the same rhythm, so a congregation can follow the tune.
- **Cantus firmus** — Latin for "fixed song": a pre-existing melody used as the structural skeleton of a new composition, often stretched into long notes while faster invented music is woven around it.
- **Chorale** — a Lutheran hymn — a German sacred melody-plus-text made for congregational singing — and, by extension, a musical setting of one, such as Bach's four-part harmonizations.
- **Chorale cantata** — a cantata built on a single hymn: its first stanza set as a large chorus, inner stanzas paraphrased into arias and recitatives, and the final stanza as a plain four-part chorale. The project of Bach's 1724–25 cycle.
- **Chorale fantasia** — a large-scale choral movement in which the hymn tune sounds phrase by phrase in long notes (usually in the sopranos) while chorus and orchestra build an independent musical world around it.
- **Chorale partita** — a set of keyboard variations on a hymn tune, typically one variation per stanza; a form Bach absorbed from Georg Böhm.
- **Chorale prelude** — an organ piece built on a hymn tune, originally played to introduce the melody before the congregation sang it. The Orgelbüchlein is Bach's great collection of the type.
- **Chromaticism** — the use of notes from outside the prevailing key, moving lines by half-steps; in the chorale repertoire, the standard resource for darkening the harmony under texts of sin, grief, or death.
- **Coloratura chorale** — a chorale prelude type (also called *ornamental chorale*) in which the hymn melody itself is elaborately embellished, present but veiled in expressive, aria-like decoration.
- **Contrafactum** — an existing melody fitted with a new text, most often a secular song given sacred words; several core chorale tunes, including the Passion chorale, entered the repertory this way.
- **Contrary motion** — two voices moving in opposite directions (one up, one down); prized between soprano and bass because it keeps the two lines maximally independent.
- **Deceptive cadence** — a cadence that prepares the expected dominant-to-tonic close but resolves instead to the chord on the sixth scale degree (V–vi): the harmonic sentence swerving just before its final word.
- **Doubling** — assigning the same chord tone to two of the four voices, necessary because a triad has only three distinct pitches. Convention favors doubling the chord's root and forbids doubling the leading tone.
- **Fermata** — the arc-with-dot sign over a note. In most music it means "hold longer than written"; in chorale notation it typically just marks the end of a phrase and the singers' breath.
- **Figured bass** — Baroque harmonic shorthand: numerals written under a bass line telling a keyboard player which intervals to sound above each bass note. Also called *thoroughbass*; the practical harmony skill from which Bach's composition teaching began.
- **Four-part texture** — music written in four simultaneous melodic lines, one per voice-type; the standard scoring of the harmonized chorale and the default laboratory of harmony teaching ever since.
- **Half cadence** — a phrase-ending that pauses *on* the dominant chord (V) rather than resolving home: a comma or open question rather than a full stop.
- **Homophony** — texture in which all voices move in essentially the same rhythm, forming a melody with chordal support — as opposed to polyphony or counterpoint, where the voices' rhythms are independent.
- **Hymn / hymnal** — a *hymn* is a strophic sacred song (the same music repeated for each stanza of text) meant for congregational singing; a *hymnal* is the printed book that collects a church's hymns.
- **Inner voices** — the alto and tenor, the two middle lines of a four-part texture, which fill in the harmony framed by the soprano and bass.
- **Leading tone** — the seventh degree of the scale, a half-step below the keynote, whose defining character is a strong pull upward to the tonic. Never doubled in good four-part writing, because two voices would then need the same resolution.
- **Mode** — one of the medieval scale-types (Dorian, Phrygian, Mixolydian, and others) that predate the major-minor system, each with its own pattern of whole and half steps. Many chorale tunes retain modal features that resist ordinary tonal harmonization.
- **Neighbor tone** — an ornamental dissonance that steps away from a chord tone (up or down) and immediately returns to it.
- **Outer voices** — the soprano and bass, the highest and lowest lines, which together form the structural frame of a four-part setting; teachers treat their two-voice counterpoint as the harmonization's skeleton.
- **Parallel fifths / parallel octaves** — the error of two voices moving together while standing a perfect fifth or octave apart; forbidden in four-part writing because those intervals blend so completely that the two lines momentarily fuse into one, collapsing the texture's independence.
- **Passing tone** — a dissonant note that fills the space between two chord tones a third apart by stepwise motion; the most common decoration in Bach's chorales, streaming through the inner voices and bass.
- **Per omnes versus** — Latin for "through all the verses": a cantata design that sets every stanza of a hymn verbatim, each movement using the tune with a different technique. BWV 4 is the landmark example.
- **Phrase** — a complete musical thought, analogous to a clause or sentence, ending in a cadence; in a chorale, each line of the stanza's text is one phrase, marked off by a fermata.
- **Phrygian cadence** — an antique-sounding half cadence in minor keys in which the bass falls a half-step onto the dominant (typically iv6–V); named for the church mode whose scale contains that half-step.
- **Picardy third** — the raising of the final tonic chord of a minor-key piece from minor to major, brightening the last sonority; a convention Bach uses at the close of most minor-key chorales.
- **Plagal cadence** — the subdominant chord resolving to the tonic (IV–I); the "Amen" formula, gentler than the authentic cadence because it lacks the leading tone's pull.
- **Roman numeral analysis** — the standard labeling system of harmony teaching: each chord is named with a Roman numeral for the scale degree of its root (I, ii, IV, V, and so on), with added symbols for inversions and borrowed chords.
- **SATB** — soprano, alto, tenor, bass: the four traditional voice-types from highest to lowest, and shorthand for the standard four-part vocal texture.
- **Secondary dominant** — a chord borrowed from another key to act as a temporary dominant of some chord other than the tonic (for example, V of V), making the harmony lean momentarily toward that chord; a basic engine of color and motion in Bach's harmonizations.
- **Suspension** — the classic prepared dissonance: a voice holds its note while the chord changes beneath it, producing a clash that then resolves downward by step. The three-stage pattern — preparation, dissonance, resolution — is inherited from Renaissance counterpoint.
- **Voice-leading** — the craft of moving each voice from one chord to the next so that every line remains smooth, singable, and independent; the real subject of chorale study, of which "the rules" are the summary.
