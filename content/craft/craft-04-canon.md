A **canon** is counterpoint with the safety catch removed. In a fugue, imitation is a resource — voices echo the subject when the music wants them to, and the composer adjusts freely everywhere else. In a canon, imitation is **law**: one voice states a melody, and another must reproduce it *exactly*, at a fixed delay, for the entire piece — while the combination remains correct harmony throughout. The composer writes one line and a rule; the rule writes the rest. It is the closest music comes to proving a theorem.

This essay unpacks that sentence slowly, because every word of it is load-bearing. It defines the game and its rules, walks the full taxonomy of transformations a canon can impose, and then visits Bach's three canonic monuments — the [Goldberg Variations](../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md), the [Musical Offering](../works/leipzig/musical-offering.md), and the [Canonic Variations on *Vom Himmel hoch*](../works/leipzig/bwv-769.md) — asking of each the same question: why did the most expressive composer of his age keep returning, all his life, to the least free procedure in the art?

## The rule that writes the piece

Start with the word. *Canon* is Greek — *kanōn*, a measuring rod, hence a rule or law (the same word gives us the "canon" of scripture and the "canonical" in mathematics). In music before Bach's time the word named not a kind of piece but literally the **rule** written above a piece: composers would notate a single melodic line and add one sentence of instruction — *the second voice enters a fifth higher, two bars later* — from which the performer was expected to derive the missing voice or voices. One line, one law, and the whole polyphonic fabric unfolds. That economy is the genre's founding gesture, and it tells you what a canon fundamentally is: not a texture but a **compressed instruction**, music stored as a seed plus a rule for growing it.

The vocabulary, defined once. **Counterpoint** — the subject of [this section's first essay](craft-01-counterpoint.md) — is the craft of combining simultaneous independent melodies, each a real tune on its own, all correct together. A **voice** is one such melodic strand (whether sung by a person or played by two fingers of one hand). **Imitation** is the device of one voice restating what another voice just said — a melodic echo at a delay. In a canon, the voice that leads is traditionally called the **dux** (Latin for "leader") and the voice that follows the **comes** ("companion" or "follower"). The dux is the only line the composer freely writes; the comes is generated from it by the rule.

Everyone already knows the folk-level version of this. A **round** — *Frère Jacques*, *Row, Row, Row Your Boat* — is a canon at the unison: each singer performs the identical melody, starting a phrase apart, and the tune has been engineered so that it harmonizes with itself. The round also demonstrates the genre's oldest trick, the **perpetual canon**: because the end of the melody loops back to harmonize with its own beginning, the piece can circle forever, ending only by agreement. The English rota *Sumer is icumen in*, from around 1250, was already doing this four centuries before Bach — the game is nearly as old as written polyphony itself, and Bach inherited a long learned tradition (the Flemish masters of the fifteenth century, above all) of treating it as counterpoint's proving ground.

Now feel the difficulty from the composer's chair, because it is the whole point. When you write bar five of the dux, that bar will be replayed, unaltered, by the comes in bar seven — where it must make correct harmony against whatever you write in the dux at bar seven. Which will itself return in bar nine. Every note is therefore written twice: once as melody now, once as accompaniment later. You compose forward while auditing backward, and there is no escape hatch — no adjusting the echo, no dropping the imitation when it becomes inconvenient. Compare the fugue, [the previous essay's subject](craft-03-fugue.md): a fugue imitates *at will*, restating its subject when the design wants it and inventing freely everywhere between; its strictness is episodic, a discipline of thought rather than a binding contract. A canon's strictness is total. The one mercy the genre allows is the **accompanied canon**: free, non-canonic voices may be added around the strict pair — most famously the independent bass line that walks beneath nearly all the Goldberg canons — giving the harmony room to breathe while the two upper voices remain locked in law.

## A taxonomy of transformations

The variables make the game, and there are exactly three: at what *pitch* the follower enters, after what *delay*, and under what *transformation*. Each can be tightened independently, and the catalog of options below is not pedantry — it is the actual space Bach systematically explored, so learning the names is learning the map.

The **interval of imitation** is the pitch relationship between leader and follower. In a **canon at the unison** the comes repeats the dux at the identical pitch (every round works this way); in a **canon at the fifth** it repeats the melody transposed five scale-steps higher; and so on for any interval — second, third, fourth, up to the ninth and beyond. An **interval**, for the newcomer, is simply the distance between two pitches, counted in steps of the scale (C up to G is a fifth: count C-D-E-F-G). The choice of interval changes the character of the constraint entirely. Canon at the unison is *hardest to keep interesting* — the voices shadow each other so closely that the texture risks monotony. Canon at the second is *hardest to keep correct* — a melody transposed up one step against itself generates near-constant dissonance, and steering it is like writing a sentence that must remain grammatical when every word is shifted one entry ahead in the dictionary.

The **distance of imitation** is the delay before the follower enters, and it sets the music's breathing room. A two-bar gap — as in the C minor Invention discussed below — gives each idea time to speak before its echo arrives. Shrink the distance and the canon tightens toward **stretto** (the fugue-world's word for entries crowding in before the theme finishes): the Sixth Brandenburg Concerto opens with the two violas in strict canon at [half a beat's distance](../works/cothen/bwv-1051.md), so close that the ear hears not melody-and-echo but a single shimmering, seasick pursuit — strict imitation experienced purely as *texture*.

Then the transformations, where the game becomes high sport. In a **canon by inversion** (also called *contrary motion*), the comes plays the dux upside down: every rising interval becomes an equal falling one, the melody mirrored around a horizontal axis, ascent answered by descent. In a **retrograde canon** — universally nicknamed the **crab canon**, *canon cancrizans*, after the crab's supposed backward walk — the second voice plays the melody *backwards*, so the line must harmonize with its own reversal; such a piece is a musical palindrome, identical whether read left to right or right to left. In an **augmentation canon** the follower plays the melody at doubled note-values — half speed, falling ever further behind the leader like a long memory — while a **diminution canon** does the reverse, the follower quickening at halved values and threatening to overtake. A **mirror canon** compounds strictness with strictness: leader and follower in inversion so exact that the two parts stand to each other as an object to its reflection. A **double canon** runs two canons simultaneously — four voices, two interlocking laws. And the **canon per tonos**, the *spiral* or endlessly modulating canon, is engineered so that each repetition ends a whole step higher than it began, the music climbing key by key — a perpetual canon that corkscrews upward instead of circling in place. (**Modulation**, one more definition, is the act of changing key — moving the music's home base from one tonal center to another.)

Finally there is a category of *presentation* rather than construction: the **puzzle canon** or **riddle canon**, the old single-line notation weaponized as a game. The composer supplies only the dux, withholds the rule, and hints at it with a motto — often cryptic, often Latin — leaving the solver to deduce the interval, the distance, the transformation, even the number of voices. Deriving the missing voices is called **realization**, and in Bach's circle it was a social currency: learned musicians exchanged puzzle canons as greetings, credentials, and album-leaf souvenirs, the eighteenth-century equivalent of mathematicians trading elegant problems. Bach wrote them all his life; one of them, as we'll see, he chose to hold in his hand for his official portrait.

The [Art of Fugue](../works/leipzig/art-of-fugue.md) contains, alongside its fourteen fugues, four canons on its single D minor subject — at the octave, at the tenth, at the twelfth, and one in augmentation and contrary motion at once — a compact demonstration that the taxonomy above is not a museum catalog but a set of live options, each yielding utterly different music from the same theme.

## The Goldberg ladder

Bach's canonic masterwork hides in plain sight inside [the Goldberg Variations](../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md): **every third variation is a canon, and the interval grows each time** — variation 3 at the unison, 6 at the second, 9 at the third, 12 at the fourth, on up to 27 at the ninth — a systematic ladder through the whole space of the game, disguised as entertainment. The scaffolding underneath is the work's famous **ground bass**: a repeating bass line (the general name for any such obstinately repeating figure is **ostinato**) over which all thirty variations are built. So the nine canons are accompanied canons of the strictest kind — two upper voices locked in imitation *while also* conforming to a preordained harmonic foundation. Two laws at once, and the music comes out sounding like dances and duets.

Climb the ladder and the constraint visibly tightens. Variations 12 and 15 are canons **in inversion** — at the fourth and fifth respectively, the follower answering the leader upside down. Variation 27, at the ninth, is the only canon in the set with *no* free bass at all: two voices, nothing else, the pure game with the training wheels removed — and it is, mischievously, one of the lightest movements in the work. And where the wheel's logic demands a canon at the tenth for variation 30, Bach instead writes a **quodlibet** — literally "what you please," a comic genre in which pre-existing popular tunes are stacked in counterpoint — folding two folk songs into the texture as a family joke where the summit of learning should be. Having proved he could ascend forever, he ends the ladder with a laugh.

Here is the point this essay most wants to make: *listen to them cold and you will not notice they are canons.* Variation 9 sounds like a tender duet; variation 15, canon at the fifth in inversion, sounds like the work's first grief; variation 21 like a dark aria. The strictness is complete and completely inaudible — law experienced as inevitability, which was, one suspects, precisely the theology of it ([essay six](craft-06-theology.md) again).

Then, in 1974, the story acquired an epilogue. Bach's personal copy of the original print — his *Handexemplar*, the composer's own working copy, [published in 1741](../events/leipzig-3/goldberg-1741.md) — surfaced with **fourteen further canons** (BWV 1087) penciled in the back, all built on the same material: the first eight bass notes of the [Aria's ground](../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md). Fourteen canons on eight notes — simple, retrograde, inverted, augmented, multiplied into double and triple combinations — a private appendix showing the published ladder was only the visible portion of the game. One of the fourteen, a triple canon in six voices catalogued separately as BWV 1076, is the very sheet Bach holds in the 1746 Haussmann portrait, the one authentic painted likeness we have: asked to present a face to posterity, he presented a puzzle canon. Admirers of number symbolism note that in the alphabet-number game of the day B+A+C+H yields 14, and that the set numbers exactly fourteen; take that as seriously as you like — the sturdier fact is that this game, for him, had no bottom.

## Riddles for a king

The [Musical Offering](../works/leipzig/musical-offering.md) adds the register the Goldbergs deliberately conceal: **wit**, publicly performed. The volume answers [the famous evening of May 1747](../events/leipzig-3/potsdam-1747.md), when Frederick the Great handed Bach a long chromatic theme at the keyboard; within two months Bach had engraved and dedicated to the king a compendium of everything that theme could become — two fugues (he used the deliberately archaic word *ricercar*), a trio sonata, and **ten canons** on the royal subject.

Several of the canons were engraved as riddles: a single melodic line plus a cryptic clue, the realization left to the reader. One pair carries the motto *Quaerendo invenietis* — "seek and ye shall find," scripture repurposed as a puzzle-setter's taunt. The print includes a crab canon, the royal theme harmonized against its own retrograde. And the most quoted page is the canon *per tonos*, the spiral canon, engineered to modulate up a whole tone with each repetition and inscribed *"as the notes ascend, so may the King's glory"*: a canon that is also a courtier's compliment that is also, played long enough, a joke — it never stops climbing, and after six revolutions it arrives back home an octave higher, glory apparently orbital. A companion canon in augmentation and contrary motion bears the matching flattery: as the note-values grow, may the king's fortune grow.

Read the rhetoric of all this carefully, because the courtliness has an edge. Frederick's court was the headquarters of the *galant* style — the light, tuneful, accompaniment-and-melody fashion that regarded dense counterpoint as their fathers' pedantry. Into that room Bach delivered ten canons and a six-voice fugue: genuflection engraved in the one medium where the visitor from Leipzig was untouchable. A riddle canon addressed to a king says, in perfect etiquette, *solve it yourself, Majesty* — and the deeper message of the whole volume is that the monarch's casually cruel test-theme contained, for anyone with eyes to see, a universe the fashionable style could not even notate. The canons are simultaneously gift, homage, demonstration, and quiet verdict; no other genre could hold all four at once, which is exactly why Bach reached for it.

## The carol and the credential

The same year, 1747, Bach joined [Mizler's Society of the Musical Sciences](../events/leipzig-3/mizler-1747.md) — a learned correspondence society of composer-theorists, admission by demonstrated science — becoming its fourteenth member. His membership exercise was [the Canonic Variations on *Vom Himmel hoch*](../works/leipzig/bwv-769.md): Luther's Christmas hymn run through five variations of escalating canonic strictness for organ — canon at the octave, at the fifth, at the seventh, canon in augmentation, and a final movement that combines the phrases of the carol *against themselves* in stretto, augmentation, and inversion at once, before a B–A–C–H figure (the composer's name spelled in German note-letters) enters in the closing bars like a signature on a finished proof.

Two things make the work more than a diploma. First, its **cantus firmus** discipline — the old term for a pre-existing melody held plainly in long notes while counterpoint is woven around it: through all five movements the carol itself stays *audible*, singable, childlike in pedal or soprano, while the canons coil about it. The strictness never buries the tune it serves; Christmas never stops being Christmas. That is a compositional ethic disguised as a texture — the learning exists *for* the song, not instead of it. Second, its address: this is what Bach chose to prove himself with, at sixty-two, to a society of scientists — not brilliance, not fashion, but strict canon on a children's carol, devotion and rigor fused past separating. (He circulated the portrait canon, BWV 1076, to the same society; the credential and the likeness were the same kind of object.)

Set the three monuments side by side and each answers a different question about the genre. The Goldberg canons prove strictness can be *inaudible* — pure music first, law only on inspection. The Offering's canons prove strictness can *speak* — carry courtesy, irony, and power in a public transaction. The Canonic Variations prove strictness can *pray*. One discipline, three registers; and behind all three, the notebook page of the Fourteen Canons shows the discipline with no audience at all — Bach solving Bach, for nobody.

## Game and creed

Why did he keep at it? The frivolous answer is temperamental: canons are puzzles, Bach was a lifelong solver, and the delight is legible on every page — the quodlibet punchline, the spiral canon's cosmic shaggy-dog joke, the taunting mottos. The genre was his chess, his correspondence game, his species of play. Any account that skips the fun falsifies the man.

But the play was load-bearing. For Bach's tradition — [essay six](craft-06-theology.md) makes the larger case — musical order was not a metaphor for the created world's order; it was a local instance of it, harmony as audible law. A canon is that conviction in its most concentrated form: a piece whose every note follows necessarily from a single line and a single rule, yet which arrives as song — grief in variation 15, Christmas in the pedal, tenderness in a two-voice duet. Law experienced as freedom; necessity indistinguishable from grace. If the fugue was Bach's essay form, the canon was his creed said in one sentence. That is why canons keep appearing at the summits — inside the Goldbergs, around the royal theme, in [the late encyclopedic shelf](craft-07-summa.md), among the last pages of the Art of Fugue — and why he put one in his portrait: it is the smallest object that says the whole thing.

And it is why the genre carries the aesthetic claim this whole Craft section keeps circling: **maximum constraint and maximum expression are not enemies.** Every essay on this timeline argues that claim somewhere; the canons do not argue it, they *demonstrate* it, QED, over and over, in music you'd love before you knew it was a proof.

## How to study a canon

A practical liturgy, in five moves, none requiring advanced theory.

**(1) Start where Bach started his students.** [Invention No. 2 in C minor](../works/cothen/bwv-773.md) is a strict canon at the octave, two bars apart, that most players never notice. Play or listen to the right hand alone; then bring in the left, two bars behind, and hear the law bare. Then ask the question the piece exists to pose: how must a melody be *built* so that it can accompany itself? (The whole [Inventions collection](../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) is the gateway to this thinking.)

**(2) Climb the Goldberg ladder in order.** Listen to variations 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27 straight through, nothing between — first pass purely for pleasure, refusing to analyze; second pass tracking only the follower, hearing it enter, shadow, invert. The point of the exercise is the gap between the two passes: how little the strictness announced itself the first time.

**(3) Solve one riddle.** Take the crab canon from the Musical Offering — the single line is easy to find in any score of the work — and try to work out, on paper or at a keyboard, how the melody fits against its own reversal. You will fail informatively for an hour, and afterward you will never again hear the word "craft" as a vague compliment.

**(4) Hear strictness as texture.** Play the opening of the [Sixth Brandenburg](../works/cothen/bwv-1051.md) and the five movements of [BWV 769](../works/leipzig/bwv-769.md) back to back: the same device — strict canon — as sensuous surface in one and as devotion in the other. This calibrates the essential lesson that a technique has no fixed expressive meaning.

**(5) Write one.** Eight bars, canon at the unison, two voices, one bar apart — a round, essentially, in the style of *Frère Jacques*. The rules of [species counterpoint](craft-01-counterpoint.md) are enough. It will be the hardest easy assignment you have ever attempted, and it will teach you, from inside, what this essay could only describe: that a good melody is not a pretty surface but a structure with load-bearing walls, and that in this game every wall must hold twice.

## Terms of Art

*A working glossary for this essay — each term defined from zero, assuming no prior training.*

- **Augmentation** — restating a melody with all its note-values lengthened, usually doubled, so the tune unfolds at half speed. In an *augmentation canon* the follower plays the leader's melody in this slowed form, falling progressively further behind.
- **Canon** — a composition governed by exact imitation: one voice states a melody and one or more others must reproduce it precisely, at a fixed delay and pitch relationship, for the whole piece, while everything sounds as correct harmony. From the Greek *kanōn*, "rule."
- **Canon per tonos** — a canon engineered so that each repetition ends a whole step higher than it began, causing the music to climb key by key; also called a spiral or endlessly modulating canon. The Musical Offering's most famous canon is one.
- **Cantus firmus** — Latin for "fixed song": a pre-existing melody (often a hymn) held in long, plain notes in one voice while freer counterpoint is woven around it. The carol in BWV 769 is a cantus firmus.
- **Chorale** — a German Lutheran hymn tune, the congregational melody sung in church; the raw material of an enormous share of Bach's music, including the Canonic Variations.
- **Counterpoint** — the craft of combining two or more simultaneous melodies, each independent and singable on its own, all correct together. The umbrella discipline of which canon and fugue are branches.
- **Crab canon (canon cancrizans)** — a canon in which the second voice plays the melody backwards, so the line harmonizes with its own reversal; the result is a musical palindrome. Named for the crab's supposed backward walk.
- **Diminution** — the opposite of augmentation: restating a melody with its note-values shortened, usually halved, so it moves at double speed.
- **Distance of imitation** — the time delay between the leader's start and the follower's entry, measured in beats or bars. Two bars in Invention No. 2; half a beat in the Sixth Brandenburg's opening.
- **Dominant** — the fifth degree of the scale, and the key built on it: harmony's natural counterweight to the home key. Imitation at the fifth gravitates toward it.
- **Double canon** — two canons running simultaneously: four voices bound by two independent laws of imitation at once.
- **Dux and comes** — the traditional Latin names for a canon's two roles: the *dux* ("leader") states the melody; the *comes* ("companion," follower) reproduces it under the canon's rule. Only the dux is freely composed.
- **Fugue** — a composition in which several voices develop one theme (the subject) through flexible, non-binding imitation; the composer imitates at will and writes freely between entries. See [the previous essay](craft-03-fugue.md) for the full machinery.
- **Ground bass** — a bass line repeated over and over, unchanged, as the foundation over which upper voices build continuous variations. The Goldberg Variations rest on one.
- **Imitation** — the device of one voice restating what another has just stated: a melodic echo at a delay. In fugue it is a resource; in canon it is a binding rule.
- **Interval** — the distance between two pitches, counted in scale steps and named by number: C up to G is a fifth (C–D–E–F–G), C up to D a second, C to the same C an octave.
- **Interval of imitation** — the pitch relationship at which the follower reproduces the leader: at the unison (same pitch), at the fifth (five steps higher), at the ninth, and so on. Each interval imposes a distinct compositional constraint.
- **Inversion** — turning a melody upside down: every rising interval becomes an equal falling one, and vice versa, as if the tune were reflected around a horizontal mirror. Also called contrary motion.
- **Invertible counterpoint** — counterpoint written so that its voices can trade registers — the top line moved below the bottom line — and still form correct harmony. The workhorse skill behind canons and fugues alike.
- **Mirror canon** — a canon whose follower is the leader's exact inversion, the two parts standing to each other as an object to its reflection.
- **Modulation** — changing key: moving the music's home base from one tonal center to another. A spiral canon modulates upward with every repetition.
- **Ostinato** — any short musical figure repeated obstinately throughout a passage or piece (Italian for "stubborn"). A ground bass is an ostinato in the bass.
- **Perpetual canon** — a canon whose ending loops seamlessly back into its beginning, allowing it to circle indefinitely; every round is one. Also called a circular or infinite canon.
- **Puzzle canon (riddle canon)** — a canon presented as a challenge: only the single leader line is written out, and the rule for deriving the other voices is withheld or hinted at by a cryptic motto. The solver must deduce interval, distance, and transformation.
- **Quodlibet** — Latin for "what you please": a comic piece stacking several pre-existing popular tunes in simultaneous counterpoint. Variation 30 of the Goldbergs is the most famous example.
- **Realization** — working out the full polyphonic texture from a canon's compressed notation: deriving the follower voices that the single written line and its rule imply.
- **Retrograde** — a melody played backwards, last note first. The transformation behind the crab canon.
- **Ricercar** — an old, learned species of fugue, and the deliberately archaic word Bach chose for the two great fugues of the Musical Offering; by his day the term already signaled venerable strictness.
- **Round** — the folk form of the perpetual canon at the unison: every singer performs the identical melody, entering a phrase apart (*Frère Jacques*, *Sumer is icumen in*).
- **Species counterpoint** — the graded classroom method for learning counterpoint, adding rhythmic complexity stage by stage against a given melody; the traditional gateway to writing canon and fugue. See [the first essay](craft-01-counterpoint.md).
- **Stretto** — entries of a theme crowding in before the previous statement has finished, overlapping voices in close pursuit. A canon at a very short distance of imitation is, in effect, a piece-long stretto.
- **Subject** — the principal theme of a fugue (or, loosely, of any imitative piece): the melody everything else develops and answers.
- **Voice** — one independent melodic strand in a polyphonic texture, whether or not anyone literally sings it; a keyboard fugue "in four voices" weaves four such strands under two hands.
