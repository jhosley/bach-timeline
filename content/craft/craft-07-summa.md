Every thread in this Craft section — invention, fugue, canon, chorale, theology — converges on the same last decade, and on a single strange fact: **in his final ten years, with no commission, no deadline, and a musical culture that had moved on, Bach systematically wrote the complete book on his own art.** Not memoirs; *demonstrations*. One per domain, each exhaustive: variation ([the Goldbergs](../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md), 1741), the tonal universe revisited ([WTC II](../works/leipzig/wtc-2.md), ~1742), fugue and canon on a given theme ([the Musical Offering](../works/leipzig/musical-offering.md), 1747), strict canon on a chorale ([the Canonic Variations](../works/leipzig/bwv-769.md), 1747), the church's whole musical history ([the B minor Mass](../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md), 1748–49), and fugue as such ([the Art of Fugue](../works/leipzig/art-of-fugue.md), left one page short). This essay asks the question the shelf itself poses: *what was he doing?* The answer this essay will defend has a name, and the name is the essay's title. A **summa** — the word is medieval Latin for "summary" or "sum total" — was a genre before it was a metaphor: a comprehensive, systematically ordered treatise covering an entire discipline, the form in which a scholar like Thomas Aquinas set out to state *everything* theology knew, in order, from first principles. Bach's last decade produced, work by work, the summa of counterpoint — written not in prose but in sound.

## The encyclopedic turn

First, notice the shape these works share, because it's a **method**, and it had been his since childhood: take one small thing and exhaust it. A thirty-two-note bass; twenty-four keys; one royal theme; one carol; one D-minor subject. The [Inventions' title-page promise](craft-02-invention.md) — find a good idea and develop it *well* — executed at terminal scale. The late works add only totality: not "develop it well" but *develop it completely*, so that the piece becomes a survey of its own possibility-space. Encyclopedism was the family temperament ([the planned-164 Orgelbüchlein grid](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md) at thirty; [six-of-everything at Cöthen](../eras/cothen.md)); the late decade is that temperament with nothing left to interrupt it.

But temperament alone doesn't explain the *timing*, and three documented currents of the 1740s do. The first is [Mizler's Society of Musical Sciences](../events/leipzig-3/mizler-1747.md), the corresponding society of learned musicians — Telemann and Handel among its members — founded by Bach's former student Lorenz Mizler on the Enlightenment premise that music was a science: a body of demonstrable knowledge, like optics or geometry, that could be stated in propositions and proved. Bach joined in June 1747, submitted the Canonic Variations as his credential, and sat for the portrait in which he holds out a puzzle canon toward the viewer. A composer who thinks of his art as a *science* thinks naturally of writing its textbook; the society gave the encyclopedic instinct an intellectual frame and an audience of peers.

The second current is the **stile antico revival** — the mid-century's antiquarian rediscovery of the "old style," the severe vocal polyphony of the sixteenth century whose model was Palestrina. **Counterpoint**, to define the discipline's own name once more, is the art of combining simultaneous independent melodic lines; the stile antico is its oldest surviving dialect — long, even note-values, smooth voice-leading, the sound of Renaissance church music. In the 1740s Bach studied, copied, and performed this repertoire, Palestrina included, and Mizler published a German translation of Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum*, the counterpoint treatise that codified the old style into teachable rules. A musician steeped in that revival stops seeing his art as a fashion and starts seeing it as a **tradition** — centuries deep, with himself as its current custodian. The late works keep making exactly that gesture: reaching back past the taste of the decade to the whole history of the discipline, and claiming all of it.

The third current is polemical. [Scheibe's 1737 verdict](../events/leipzig-2/scheibe-1737.md) — turgid, unnatural, obsolete — was the fashionable consensus, and Bach never rebutted it in prose after Birnbaum's pamphlets. The late shelf *is* the rebuttal: counterpoint's case stated not defensively but exhaustively, in engraved publications ([mostly self-financed](../events/leipzig-2/clavier-ubung.md)) addressed to the only jury that could overrule his century — practitioners not yet born. This is not romantic projection; the *format* says it. These are teaching editions, systematically ordered, of precisely the disciplines his era called dead. You don't publish the complete grammar of a language you believe is dying unless you believe in resurrection — or at least in libraries.

## The Art of Fugue: a treatise in sound

If the summa has a central volume, it is the Art of Fugue, because its organization is not a metaphor for a treatise — it *is* one. Fourteen fugues and four canons, every one grown from a single plain D-minor **subject** (a fugue's subject is its theme, the one melodic idea every voice will discuss — [the fugue essay](craft-03-fugue.md) defines the machinery in full). Bach headed each movement with the austere Latin term **contrapunctus** — "counterpoint," used as a chapter title — and arranged the chapters in ascending order of rigor, exactly as a treatise proceeds from axioms to hard theorems.

The curriculum runs like this. First, **simple fugues**: the subject treated straightforwardly, the rules of the world laid out. Then **counter-fugues**, in which the subject does battle with its own **inversion** — the same melody turned upside down, every rising interval now falling, every fall now a rise — so that the theme generates its own opposition. Then the multiple fugues: **double** and **triple fugues**, which introduce one and then two entirely new subjects, give each its own exposition, and then combine them all in simultaneous counterpoint — several themes sounding at once, each still itself. Then the **mirror fugues**, the cycle's most vertiginous exhibit: complete fugues written so that the *entire texture* can be inverted — every voice, every interval, top-to-bottom — and remain correct, two whole pieces standing to each other as a body to its reflection in still water. And finally the summit: a culminating fugue on three subjects, the third of which spells, in German note-names (where B means B-flat and H means B-natural), B-flat–A–C–B-natural — **B–A–C–H**. After a lifetime of counterpoint, the signature enters the cathedral as a subject. The design implies a **quadruple fugue** — in the 1880s the scholar Gustav Nottebohm demonstrated that the three subjects combine in correct counterpoint with the cycle's principal D-minor theme, the binding stroke the plan seems to promise — and the manuscript breaks off before delivering it.

Here the site's provenance discipline matters, because the most famous story about this work is a curated legend. On the final page, in Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach's hand, stands the note: "at this point... the composer died." Taken literally, it is not true. The surviving autograph of the cycle dates from about 1742 — most of the work was composed while Bach was healthy and busy — and the handwriting of the final fragment predates the last illness; the missing ending may even have existed on pages now lost. [The event card](../events/leipzig-3/art-of-fugue-begun.md) walks the dating; the honest summary is that Bach spent his last years revising and expanding a long-finished conception for engraved publication, and died with the edition incomplete. The same honesty applies to the deathbed chorale — the tradition that he dictated *Vor deinen Thron* from his bed is [flagged tradition on the death card](../events/leipzig-3/death-1750.md); the documented reality is a craftsman revising an earlier setting. And yet the legend's power is itself a fact about the work: generations have heard those empty staves as the most eloquent silence in music, and the timeline flags the legend as legend without pretending it has no force.

## The Musical Offering: the summa goes to court

The Musical Offering is the summa in miniature and in public — the one volume of the shelf provoked by an occasion, and therefore the one that shows the method under a spotlight. In May 1747 Frederick the Great handed Bach a theme at the keyboard — the *thema regium*, the "royal theme," a long, chromatic, deliberately awkward subject — and [the Potsdam evening](../events/leipzig-3/potsdam-1747.md) became a challenge: what can be made of this? Within two months Bach had composed, engraved, and dedicated to the king an entire anthology answering the question exhaustively: two ricercars, ten canons, and a trio sonata, every movement built on the royal theme.

The vocabulary of the volume is itself an argument. A **ricercar** (Italian, "to seek out") is an archaic word for a learned imitative keyboard piece — the sixteenth-century ancestor of the fugue — and Bach's choice of the antique term over the modern one was pointed: a deliberate reach back past the galant fashion of Frederick's court to the old learned tradition. He even printed the word as an acrostic, its letters unfolding into a Latin sentence meaning "at the king's command, the song and the remainder resolved with canonic art." The three-voice ricercar approximates what he had improvised on the spot at Potsdam; the six-voice ricercar is the feat he had *declined* to improvise — six independent voices under two hands — and delivered instead in print, as if to say: the evening found the limit of improvisation, not of the theme. The ten canons add wit to rigor. A **canon** is counterpoint's strictest game — one voice states a melody and another must reproduce it exactly, at a fixed delay, while the combination stays correct ([the canon essay](craft-04-canon.md) lives here) — and several of the Offering's canons are engraved as **riddle canons**: a single line plus a cryptic Latin motto, the solution left to the reader. One bears the motto *Quaerendo invenietis* — "seek and you shall find." Another spirals upward, modulating a whole tone with each repetition, inscribed "as the notes ascend, so may the King's glory." And the closing trio sonata dresses the severe theme in the king's own beloved galant flute style — the **galant** being the mid-century's fashionable idiom of light, tuneful, accompanied melody, precisely the taste that had declared counterpoint obsolete. The learned style, in other words, absorbs the fashion that was supposed to bury it. Courtesy and demonstration are fused on every page: a gift a monarch could enjoy, framing a claim no monarch could refute.

## The vocal summa: the Mass in B minor

The instrumental volumes survey counterpoint; the Mass surveys everything else — and it does so by a method that needs a name and a defense. **Parody technique**, in eighteenth-century usage, has nothing to do with mockery: it means fitting new words to existing music, recomposing as much as the new text requires. Bach practiced it all his life as working economy; in the Mass he elevated it into curation. Assembled in 1748–49, the work sets the complete Latin **Mass Ordinary** — the five unchanging texts of the Mass (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) — at a scale no service could use, and most of it was not newly composed but *chosen*: the **Missa** (in Lutheran usage, a Kyrie–Gloria pair) he had presented to the Dresden court in 1733 [with his petition for a royal title](../events/leipzig-2/dresden-title-1733-36.md); the Sanctus of 1724; movements parodied from cantatas across three decades, the oldest — the *Crucifixus*, a chaconne over a grinding chromatic ground bass — [born in Weimar in 1714](../works/weimar/bwv-12.md) and carried thirty-five years to its place at the Credo's heart. A composer at the end of his life went back through a lifetime of sacred music, judged it, perfected the best of it, and set it in order. That is not recycling; it is an author compiling his collected works.

The Credo — headed **Symbolum Nicenum**, "the Nicene Creed," in Bach's autograph — is where the summa's historical reach shows plainest. Its opening chorus and the *Confiteor* are written in strict stile antico, the old Palestrina style, each built over a **cantus firmus** — a pre-existing melody, here the ancient plainchant of the Creed itself, running in long notes through the texture while the counterpoint works around it. **Plainchant** (or Gregorian chant) is the unaccompanied, unmeasured liturgical song of the medieval church: the oldest layer of Western music, quoted here as a foundation stone. Beside these antique movements stand high-Baroque concerto choruses and duets in the modern galant manner — sacred music's whole history, from chant to the style of his sons' generation, laid out in one architecture. And the ending states the thesis: the final *Dona nobis pacem* ("grant us peace") re-uses the music of the earlier *Gratias* ("we give thanks"), parody raised to theology — gratitude and supplication revealed as one act. No Leipzig liturgy [could seat the whole](../events/leipzig-3/b-minor-mass-1749.md); he never heard it complete, and no one did in his lifetime. It is the vocal summa exactly as the Art of Fugue is the contrapuntal one: comprehensive, ordered, addressed past the occasion to the discipline's whole memory.

## The variation summa: the Goldbergs and the Christmas canons

**Variation** — the art of restating one musical foundation in successive transformations — gets two volumes, one public and encyclopedic, one concentrated and scientific. The Goldbergs, [published in 1741 as Clavier-Übung IV](../events/leipzig-3/goldberg-1741.md), are an Aria and thirty variations built not on the Aria's melody but on its *bass*: a **ground bass**, a repeating foundation line over which each new structure is raised, here sustained for some eighty minutes. (The Aria itself moves in the slow triple sway of a **sarabande**, the gravest of the Baroque dances.) The clockwork is total: the variations run in threes — character piece, virtuoso showpiece, canon — and the canons ascend by interval as the wheel turns, at the unison, the second, the third, up to the ninth: a systematic ladder through the entire space of the canonic game, disguised as entertainment. A French overture restarts the machine at the exact center; the great minor-key adagio of variation 25 plants grief at the geometric heart; and the last variation is a **quodlibet** — a counterpoint of quoted folk songs, learned technique wearing a grin — before the Aria returns unchanged, and utterly changed. When Bach's personal copy surfaced in 1974 with fourteen further canons on the same bass penciled in the back, it confirmed the design's character: this was a possibility-space he did not consider closed at thirty.

The Canonic Variations on *Vom Himmel hoch* are the same instinct at maximum concentration: Luther's Christmas hymn run through five movements of escalating canonic strictness — the credential Bach submitted [to Mizler's society](../events/leipzig-3/mizler-1747.md) in 1747, counterpoint's doctoral defense conducted on a children's carol. Two features make it the summa's epitome in eleven minutes. The carol stays *audible* throughout — plain, singable, never buried by the mathematics — which is the whole late-style credo: law experienced as song. And in the final bars, as every phrase of the tune piles against itself in **stretto** (entries overlapping before the theme finishes), **augmentation** (the theme at doubled note-values, moving at half speed), and inversion at once, a B–A–C–H figure enters the texture — the same signature that crowns the Art of Fugue, stitched here into a finished cathedral rather than an unfinished one.

## What "summa" finally means

Now the word can carry its full weight. A medieval summa was not a collection of essays but a *complete, ordered account of a discipline* — everything the field knew, arranged from elements to summits, written so that the tradition could be transmitted whole. Measure the late shelf against that definition and the fit is exact: each work takes one domain of the art, orders its contents by ascending difficulty, and demonstrates the domain's entire range on a deliberately minimal premise. The treatise format without the prose; Aquinas's ambition with a keyboard instead of a lectern.

And notice who the volumes are *for*, because the answer keeps widening. The Goldbergs address connoisseurs ("for the soul's delight of music-lovers," says the title page); the Offering, a king; the Canonic Variations, a scientific society; the Mass — no one alive: a Latin summa no Leipzig liturgy could seat, assembled from his life's best sacred music as if for an archive of the art itself. And the Art of Fugue's audience is, finally, **the discipline as such** — one theme interrogated until the interrogator's own name enters as a subject and the manuscript stops. Whether or not the deathbed framing is legend (it partly is), the trajectory is documentary: each summa is addressed one step further past the market, until the addressee is posterity outright.

Resist, one last time, the two available sentimentalities. The bitter version — neglected genius, scorned by his time, writing for the drawer — founders on the facts: these were *publications*, sold, engraved at real cost, by a man with a royal title, famous sons, and full employment. The serene version — the craftsman above it all — founders on [the Erdmann letter](../people/georg-erdmann.md) and the pamphlet war: he felt the era's verdict, and it stung. The truer picture is stranger: a working professional who had answered every occasion for forty years — [wedding, funeral](../events/leipzig-1/cantors-household.md), [council election](../works/muehlhausen/bwv-71.md), [coffee house](../events/leipzig-2/collegium-1729.md), king's parlor — spending his last strength answering **the occasion behind the occasions**: the art itself, treated as the final patron, with the same punctual thoroughness he'd given every other client. *Soli Deo Gloria*, he still wrote at the ends — and if [essay six](craft-06-theology.md) is right that order cohering into harmony was his experience of the created world, then the late summa is simply the largest S.D.G. he could construct.

## How a student should enter the summa

The late works have a forbidding reputation, and the single most useful correction is practical: **do not start where the rigor is thickest.** The shelf has a friendly door and a steep interior, and the order below climbs it.

- **Start with the Goldbergs**, because they are the summa wearing its most charming clothes. First pass: learn the Aria's bass line by humming it, then hear the variations as one line wearing thirty costumes. Second pass: the canons only, variations 3, 6, 9 and every third thereafter, listening for strictness you *cannot hear* — the essay's central lesson delivered as pleasure.
- **Take the Canonic Variations as one afternoon.** Eleven minutes, five movements, a tune you already know. Track the carol through each movement and let the canons blur around it; then read [the canon essay](craft-04-canon.md) and listen again for the machinery.
- **Then the Musical Offering**, in its dramatic frame: read [the Potsdam card](../events/leipzig-3/potsdam-1747.md) first, so the three-voice ricercar sounds like the improvisation it approximates and the six-voice like the answer it is. Save the six-voice ricercar for last and hear it twice — once at a keyboard, once in an ensemble arrangement — because deciding what it should sound like is part of studying it.
- **The Mass wants a map, not stamina.** Don't attempt two hours cold. Begin with the sequence at the Credo's heart — *Crucifixus* into *Et resurrexit*, burial then blaze — then the opening Kyrie, then the *Gratias* and the final *Dona nobis pacem* back to back, to hear the parody thesis with your own ears. Only then play it whole.
- **End with the Art of Fugue**, and study it in score. It stands in **open score** — each voice on its own staff, no instrument named — and that format is an invitation: follow one voice at a time with your finger, through Contrapunctus 1, then 4, then 9, then 11, then the mirror pair heard back to back, then 14. Free scores are a search away (the work cards link IMSLP); a pencil and four colored lines will teach you more counterpoint than a semester of adjectives.

What happened to the completed book is [the next arc's story](../eras/eclipse.md) — the plates nearly sold for copper, the thirty subscribers, the long silence. But hold the image the summa leaves: the last page of the Art of Fugue, three subjects sounding, the fourth — the one that would bind them — implied by the design and absent from the paper. Every student who has ever traced that final page has felt the same thing: not incompleteness, but *assignment*. The complete book ends, deliberately or not, with an exercise left for the reader. This timeline is one very long attempt at it.

## Terms of Art

*The working vocabulary of this essay, defined from zero — no prior knowledge assumed.*

- **Augmentation** — Restating a theme in longer note-values (typically doubled), so it moves at half speed. The opposite, halved values, is **diminution**.
- **B–A–C–H motive** — In German note-spelling, B means B-flat and H means B-natural, so the letters of Bach's name form a playable four-note melody: B-flat, A, C, B-natural. Bach wove it into the final fugue of the Art of Fugue and the last bars of the Canonic Variations as a musical signature.
- **Canon** — The strictest form of imitative counterpoint: one voice states a melody and another must reproduce it exactly, at a fixed delay (and possibly transposed, inverted, or slowed), while the combination remains correct harmony throughout.
- **Cantus firmus** — Latin for "fixed song": a pre-existing melody, often a chant or hymn, laid through a composition in long notes while newly composed voices weave around it.
- **Chaconne** — A Baroque form built from continuous variations over a short repeating bass line or harmonic pattern; the *Crucifixus* of the B minor Mass is a chaconne over a chromatic ground.
- **Clavier-Übung** — "Keyboard practice": the modest series title Bach gave his major engraved keyboard publications; the Goldberg Variations appeared as its fourth installment in 1741.
- **Contrapunctus** — Latin for "counterpoint"; the austere chapter-heading Bach gave each fugue of the Art of Fugue, numbering them like propositions in a treatise.
- **Counter-fugue** — A fugue in which the answering entries present the subject in inversion, so the theme generates its own upside-down opposition; the middle chapters of the Art of Fugue.
- **Counterpoint** — The art of combining two or more simultaneous melodic lines, each independent and each correct against the others; the discipline the whole late summa summarizes.
- **Double and triple fugue** — A fugue with two (or three) distinct subjects, each usually given its own exposition, then combined so that all sound at once in correct counterpoint.
- **Galant style** — The fashionable mid-eighteenth-century idiom of light, tuneful melody over simple accompaniment — the "natural" modern taste, championed by critics like Scheibe, that regarded dense counterpoint as obsolete.
- **Ground bass** — A short bass line repeated over and over as the fixed foundation for continuous variation above it; the Goldberg Variations are built on one thirty-two-note ground.
- **Inversion** — Turning a melody upside down: every interval keeps its size but reverses direction, rises becoming falls. A fugue subject, a canon, or (in the mirror fugues) an entire texture can be inverted.
- **Mass Ordinary** — The five unchanging texts of the Latin Mass — Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei — the standard frame composers set to music; the B minor Mass sets all five at maximal scale.
- **Mirror fugue** — A fugue composed so that the complete texture can be inverted — every voice, every interval, top to bottom — yielding a second, equally correct piece; the Art of Fugue contains two such pairs.
- **Missa** — In Bach's Lutheran usage, a setting of just the Kyrie and Gloria; the Missa he presented at Dresden in 1733 became the opening third of the B minor Mass.
- **Number alphabet** — The cipher where A=1, B=2, and so on, by which B+A+C+H sums to 14. Bach *was* the fourteenth member of Mizler's society; the claim that he waited for that number on purpose is undocumented tradition.
- **Open score** — Notation with each voice on its own staff and no instrument specified — the format of the Art of Fugue; scholarly consensus holds it was nonetheless meant for keyboard.
- **Parody technique** — Fitting new words to existing music, recomposing as needed — no mockery implied. Bach's lifelong practice, elevated in the B minor Mass into the method of a lifetime's self-anthology.
- **Plainchant** — The unaccompanied, unmeasured liturgical melody of the medieval church (Gregorian chant); the ancient Credo chant runs as a cantus firmus through the Mass's stile antico movements.
- **Quadruple fugue** — A fugue combining four subjects at once. The Art of Fugue's unfinished finale presents three; Nottebohm showed in the 1880s that they combine with the cycle's main theme, implying the quadruple summit the manuscript never reaches.
- **Quodlibet** — Latin for "what you please": a piece weaving several familiar tunes together in counterpoint, usually humorously; the Goldbergs' thirtieth variation stitches folk songs over the ground bass.
- **Ricercar** — An archaic term (Italian, "to seek out") for a learned imitative piece, ancestor of the fugue; Bach revived the antique word for the two great fugues of the Musical Offering.
- **Riddle canon** — A canon notated as a single line plus a cryptic clue, the reader left to deduce where, at what interval, and in what transformation the other voices enter. Several of the Musical Offering's canons are engraved this way.
- **Sarabande** — A slow, grave Baroque dance in triple meter with weight on the second beat; the Goldberg Aria moves in sarabande rhythm.
- **Stile antico** — "Old style": the severe sixteenth-century vocal polyphony modeled on Palestrina — long note-values, smooth voice-leading — revived by eighteenth-century learned musicians and deployed in the B minor Mass's Credo and *Confiteor*.
- **Stretto** — Overlapping entries of a fugue subject, each voice beginning before the previous has finished — the texture tightening like a fist; climactic in the Canonic Variations' finale.
- **Subject** — A fugue's theme: the single melodic idea stated alone at the outset and then discussed by every voice; the Art of Fugue grows entirely from one D-minor subject.
- **Summa** — Medieval Latin for "sum total": a genre of comprehensive treatise setting out an entire discipline systematically, as in Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae*. This essay's claim: Bach's late works are the summa of counterpoint, written in sound.
- **Symbolum Nicenum** — "The Nicene Creed": Bach's own heading for the Credo section of the B minor Mass in his autograph score.
- **Thema regium** — "The royal theme": the long chromatic subject Frederick the Great gave Bach at Potsdam in May 1747, on which every movement of the Musical Offering is built.
- **Variation** — The art of restating one musical foundation — a melody, a bass, a harmonic frame — in successive transformations, each preserving the foundation's identity; the Goldbergs and the Canonic Variations are its late summa.
