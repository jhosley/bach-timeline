Start with the word. *Contrapunctus* — from the medieval Latin *punctus contra punctum*, "point against point," note against note: the craft of combining **two or more melodies that sound at the same time**, each one a real tune you could sing alone, which together also make correct, expressive harmony. That double requirement is the whole discipline, and it is worth sitting with before any technicality arrives. Anyone can write one good melody. Anyone can write a chord progression — a planned series of harmonies — and float a tune over it; that is what most music, from opera arias to pop songs, actually is: melody plus accompaniment. Counterpoint refuses the division of labor. In a contrapuntal texture there *is* no accompaniment; everyone is a soloist, and the harmony is what their agreements and frictions add up to. Each note is doing two jobs at once — a step in its own melody's story, and a vote in the harmony of the moment — and the composer must satisfy both masters in every voice, on every beat.

This essay is the foundation of the Craft section. Everything the later essays examine — [the Inventions as a teaching method](craft-02-invention.md), [fugue](craft-03-fugue.md), [canon](craft-04-canon.md), [the chorale workshop](craft-05-chorale.md), [the theology of order](craft-06-theology.md), and [the encyclopedic last decade](craft-07-summa.md) — is a specialization of the one discipline defined here. So the questions this essay owes you are the basic ones: what counterpoint actually is, what its rules are and why they exist, where Bach got it, why his century was abandoning it, and why he bet his life on it anyway.

## Two ways for music to be many

Musicians name the difference with a pair of terms. A **texture** — the word for how the simultaneous layers of a piece relate to each other — is **homophonic** when one melody leads and everything else supports it: tune on top, chords beneath, a clear hierarchy of figure and ground. It is **polyphonic** ("many-voiced") when several melodies of equal citizenship unfold at once. Counterpoint is the craft that makes polyphony possible; polyphony is what counterpoint sounds like. Neither texture is better — Bach wrote superb homophony all his life, and the famous C major prelude that opens [the Well-Tempered Clavier](../works/cothen/bwv-846.md) is nothing but harmony, one arpeggiated chord per bar, melody renounced entirely. But the two textures ask fundamentally different things of a listener, and learning to notice which one you are inside is the first analytical skill this whole site tries to teach.

Hear the difference in one comparison from this timeline. In a Vivaldi concerto — the music [young Bach transcribed by the crate](../events/weimar/vivaldi-transcriptions.md) — the violin sings and the orchestra mostly pulses beneath: brilliant, clear, one voice sovereign. In the slow movement of [Bach's Double Concerto](../works/cothen/bwv-1043.md), two violins sing *the same melody at different times*, overlapping so that each one's phrase-ending becomes the other's accompaniment. Nobody is sovereign; the beauty *is* the relationship. That is counterpoint doing emotional work, and it's why the movement feels like a portrait of friendship rather than a serenade.

One more piece of vocabulary and the ground is laid. In contrapuntal writing each melodic strand is called a **voice**, whether or not anyone sings it — a fugue for solo harpsichord has three or four "voices," meaning three or four simultaneous melodic lines the player's two hands must keep distinct. The number of voices is the first fact to establish about any contrapuntal piece, the way the number of characters is the first fact about a play: it tells you how many speakers to follow, and everything else in the analysis hangs on it.

## The grammar of two lines at once

Now the machinery — because the miracle of independent voices rests on a grammar as learnable as any language's, and Bach would have insisted on exactly that word. Its atoms are **intervals**: the distance between two pitches, counted in steps of the scale, so that C up to D is a second, C to E a third, C to G a fifth, C to the next C an octave. When two voices sound together, they form an interval at every instant, and the entire tradition rests on sorting those intervals into two families. **Consonances** are the stable ones — the intervals that sound settled, at rest, needing nothing: the octave and fifth (called *perfect* consonances — pure, open, slightly hollow) and the thirds and sixths (called *imperfect* — sweeter, warmer, the sound of voices agreeing). **Dissonances** are the unstable ones — seconds, sevenths, the tritone, and (in this style, against the bass) the fourth: intervals that sound tense, leaning, *in need of somewhere to go*. The terms are not insults and compliments. Dissonance is not a flaw; it is the engine. A music of pure consonance would be a music without desire. The craft lies entirely in how tension is entered and left.

The second layer of the grammar governs how voices move from one interval to the next — what theorists call **voice-leading**, the art of connecting each voice's present note to its next note so that every line stays smooth and singable while the combination stays correct. Two voices can move in exactly four ways, and the four terms are worth memorizing because every counterpoint text for five centuries uses them. In **parallel motion** the voices move in the same direction by the same distance, locked together like cyclists drafting. In **similar motion** they move the same direction by different distances. In **oblique motion** one voice moves while the other holds still. In **contrary motion** — the gold standard — they move in opposite directions, each asserting its own will, maximally distinct to the ear.

From these materials come the famous prohibitions, and here is the point most students miss: the rules are not etiquette, they are *physics of attention*. The most notorious — no **parallel fifths or octaves**, meaning two voices may not move in parallel while standing a perfect fifth or octave apart — exists because those intervals blend so completely that the two voices momentarily fuse into one thicker voice. Nothing sounds "wrong"; something worse happens — a voice *dies*, and the texture's whole premise, real independence, is silently betrayed. Nearly every rule in the book unpacks the same way. Prefer contrary motion: it keeps the voices audibly distinct. Let each line move mostly by step, saving leaps for expressive moments and recovering from them by stepping back: that keeps every voice singable, and *singable* is the tradition's root criterion, because the rules were worked out for unaccompanied choirs. The whole rulebook is one sentence wearing fifty costumes: **every voice must remain a voice.**

## Species thinking: dissonance on a leash

The rules that make it all possible are older than Bach by centuries. The Renaissance church — Palestrina is the name the textbooks keep, the sixteenth-century Roman master whose smooth choral style became the tradition's benchmark — worked out how independent voices can move without chaos: which intervals are stable, which are tense, and above all how dissonance must be **prepared and resolved** — approached smoothly, left by step — so that friction reads as *feeling* rather than error. And the pedagogical genius of the tradition was to make that treatment drillable, one difficulty at a time, through the regimen called **species counterpoint**.

The method, codified most influentially in Johann Joseph Fux's 1725 treatise *Gradus ad Parnassum* ("Steps to Parnassus" — written as a dialogue between a master and pupil, with Palestrina's style as its model, and still the skeleton of counterpoint courses today), works like this. The student receives a **cantus firmus** — "fixed song": a given melody in long, even notes that cannot be altered — and writes one new voice against it under progressively loosening constraints, each constraint level a *species*. First species: one note against each note of the cantus, consonances only — pure interval-choice, no rhythm, no dissonance, the discipline at its most naked. Second species: two notes against one, and with rhythm comes the first legal dissonance, the **passing tone** — a dissonant note allowed *because it is in motion*, stepping between two consonances on a weak beat, tension as a corridor rather than a room. (Its cousin the **neighbor tone** steps off a consonance and back again.) Third species: four notes against one, faster figuration, more ways through. Fourth species: the profoundest lesson in Western music, the **suspension** — one voice *holds* its consonant note while the other voice moves beneath it, so that the held note becomes dissonant through no motion of its own, clashes on the strong beat, then resolves down by step into consonance. Prepare, suspend, resolve: tension created by loyalty to a note the world has moved past, released by yielding one step. Fifth species combines everything — "florid" counterpoint, the real thing.

If you've done these exercises, you've drilled exactly this; if you haven't, play [Invention No. 11](../works/cothen/bwv-782.md) slowly and listen for the holds and releases. Every "expressive" moment in Bach is, at the level of machinery, a prepared and resolved tension — the appoggiaturas that ache in the slow movements, the chains of suspensions that make the [Double Concerto's](../works/cothen/bwv-1043.md) Largo lean forward, link by link. The rules aren't the opposite of the feeling; they're its delivery system. And species thinking — isolate one difficulty, master it, add the next — is not just counterpoint's method but the pedagogical DNA of everything Bach built for students: the [Inventions'](../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) two voices before the Sinfonias' three, [the Well-Tempered Clavier's](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md) graded ascent. The connection is direct even institutionally: Lorenz Mizler, founder of [the learned society Bach joined in 1747](../events/leipzig-3/mizler-1747.md), translated Fux's treatise into German for Bach's own generation of students.

One more concept belongs in the toolkit, because the later essays lean on it constantly. **Imitation** is the device of one voice echoing another's melody moments later — the simplest and most audible way to make independent voices sound *related*, cooperation dramatized as call and answer. Made strict, so that the following voice must copy the leader exactly for the whole piece, imitation becomes **canon** — [essay four's subject](craft-04-canon.md). Made flexible and architectural, organized around a single theme that every voice must state and discuss, it becomes **fugue** — [essay three's](craft-03-fugue.md). And when counterpoint is engineered so that its voices can trade places — the top line becoming the bottom line and the harmony still working, a technique called **invertible counterpoint** — the same material yields multiple pieces of music, a thrift that Bach exploits everywhere. All of that machinery is downstream of this essay: every one of those forms is just counterpoint with a particular promise attached.

## The old style, the new style, and the thoroughbass classroom

Bach stands at a peculiar junction in this tradition's history, and his position there explains both his mastery and his isolation. By his lifetime the strict vocal counterpoint of Palestrina's age had been consciously preserved under the name **stile antico** — the "old style" — kept alive in church music as a deliberate archaism, the musical equivalent of Latin: no one's native tongue anymore, everyone's mark of learning. Bach studied it, performed it (he adapted Palestrina masses for his Leipzig choir in his last decade), and wrote in it when gravity demanded — the opening of the [B minor Mass's](../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md) Credo sets the ancient creed in deliberately ancient polyphony, and the E major fugue of [the WTC's second book](../works/leipzig/bwv-878.md) is a loving essay in the old vocal manner, its subject practically a piece of Gregorian chant. But Bach was not a stile antico composer. His native language was the *modern* counterpoint of his own era — chromatic where the old style was pure, instrumental where it was vocal, and above all **harmonically driven**: organized around keys, around the gravitational pull of **tonic** (a key's home chord, where music rests) and **dominant** (the chord a fifth above, the home chord's natural counterweight, whose tension demands return), around directed progressions and **cadences** — the settling formulas that end phrases the way punctuation ends sentences.

That is why the way Bach actually *taught* this material matters so much, and we know how he taught it: his students and his sons left concordant accounts. He did not begin students with species exercises in the abstract. He began them with **thoroughbass** — also called **figured bass**: the era's working shorthand, in which a keyboard player reads a bass line annotated with small numbers (*figures*) indicating the intervals to sound above each note, and realizes full harmony from it in real time, the way a jazz pianist reads chord symbols. Thoroughbass was the period's harmonic literacy — every professional had it in the fingers — and in Bach's classroom it came first: pure four-part harmony at the keyboard, every voice's conduct attended to, before any freer composition. Then came [chorales](craft-05-chorale.md) — the four-part hymn settings that are, as essay five argues, complete harmony lessons forty seconds long. Only inside that harmonic discipline did fugue and free counterpoint follow. In the rules of thoroughbass he dictated to pupils, Bach defined the practice in terms borrowed from an older theorist, Friedrich Erhard Niedt, and ended with the claim this whole section keeps meeting: the final purpose of all music is the glory of God and the lawful refreshment of the mind.

Understand this and you hold the key to what makes Bach's counterpoint *his*. The textbook opposition — counterpoint versus harmony, lines versus chords — is precisely what Bach dissolves. In his music the two systems are one system heard from two sides: every chord is the meeting of real melodies, and every melody implies the harmony it travels through. A Bach bass line is simultaneously a tune, a harmonic argument, and a scaffold a keyboardist could realize figures over. This is why his counterpoint sounds *inevitable* where even fine strict counterpoint of the old school can sound merely correct: the lines are riding harmonic currents — sequences that travel, cadences that promise and defer — so the ear always knows not just that the voices agree, but *where they are all going together*.

## The wager

Bach inherited this craft from every direction at once — [the family workshop](../events/eisenach/the-bach-clan.md), [Pachelbel via his brother](../events/ohrdruf/ohrdruf-brother-1695.md), [the northern organists](../events/arnstadt/lubeck-buxtehude-1705.md), [the choir library at Lüneburg](../events/lueneburg/michaelis-library.md) — and then made the strange, career-defining choice this timeline keeps circling: **as his century abandoned counterpoint, he doubled down.** The fashionable music of his mature years — the *galant* style his own sons wrote, the style whose very name meant "elegant" and "agreeable" — deliberately simplified: one lovely melody, light chords, easy pleasure, the tune's charm now the whole transaction. The galant composers were not fools; they had a genuine aesthetic case, the case of clarity and nature against learned artifice, and history sided with them all the way to Mozart. Critics like [Scheibe said the quiet part loudly](../events/leipzig-2/scheibe-1737.md): all that artifice is against nature — Bach's insistence that every voice be fully worked out "darkens the beauty" of his music with excessive art. Bach's answer, in words (through his defender Birnbaum) and in works, was that art *is* nature brought to its perfection — that reality is many true voices sounding at once, and music that renders only one voice is the simplification.

Why did it matter so much to him? Three answers braid through this timeline, and you don't have to choose among them. **Craft-pride:** counterpoint is the hardest thing music does, and Bach was [a guildsman's son](../events/eisenach/the-bach-clan.md) who measured himself by mastery — the wager of a man who would rather be the last master of the deepest craft than a fluent speaker of an easier one. **Pedagogy:** independence of voices is *teachable* — it's a discipline of constraints, which is why [his teaching books](craft-02-invention.md) could promise composition to beginners; you cannot teach inspiration, but you can teach voice-leading, and Bach's whole classroom was built on that distinction. **Theology:** for a Lutheran of Bach's stamp, many free voices cohering into one harmony was not a metaphor he invented but a picture of created order he inherited — [essay six](craft-06-theology.md) takes that up in full. And the wager's last act is the plainest evidence of how much he meant it: the final decade spent writing counterpoint's complete book, from [a king's parlor challenge](../events/leipzig-3/potsdam-1747.md) to [the unfinished summit of the Art of Fugue](../works/leipzig/art-of-fugue.md) — [essay seven's](craft-07-summa.md) subject, and the reason this section ends where it does.

## How to study this, how to hear it

For your own study, one reorientation matters more than any definition: **stop listening for the tune and start listening for the conversation.** Bach rewards — really, requires — a kind of listening most music never asks of us: tracking two, three, four simultaneous speakers, the way you follow a good dinner-table argument. Some concrete disciplines, in rising order of effort. Pick any two-voice piece and listen through once attending *only* to the lower voice — sing along with it, badly, out loud; most listeners discover they have never actually heard a Bach bass line, only felt it. Then listen again for the moments of friction: every time the texture stings, ask the species question — *who held on, and who moved?* — and listen for the downward step that releases it; you will be hearing suspensions, and once heard they cannot be unheard. Watch for the four motions, especially contrary motion at climaxes, where Bach's voices pull apart like wings opening. And notice imitation everywhere: when a phrase you just heard returns in another register, in another voice, you are watching the conversation's logic — statement, reply, development.

The skill is buildable, and this timeline's work cards build it deliberately: the [Inventions](../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) teach two-voice hearing, the Sinfonias three, [the WTC fugues](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md) three to five — and when you reach them, [the fugue essay's](craft-03-fugue.md) five-step study liturgy takes over where this paragraph stops. Six months of that ladder changes what your ears can do — and every other repertoire you study, from Renaissance masses to Brahms, comes into focus with it, because the grammar you will have internalized is the grammar all of them assume. That is the practical case for counterpoint, and it was Bach's own: he taught it not as history's strictest game but as the fullest way of hearing what music can be.

## Terms of Art

*The working vocabulary of this essay — defined from zero, and alphabetized for reference.*

- **Cadence** — a settling formula of harmony and melody that closes a musical phrase, the way punctuation closes a sentence. Cadences range from full stops (a firm arrival on the home chord) to commas (a pause on a tense chord that promises continuation).
- **Canon** — the strictest form of imitation: one voice states a melody and another must reproduce it exactly, at a fixed delay, for the whole piece, while the combination remains correct harmony throughout.
- **Cantus firmus** — Latin for "fixed song": a given melody, usually in long even notes, that cannot be altered, against which a student (or composer) writes new voices. The anchor of species-counterpoint training.
- **Chorale** — a Lutheran hymn: a melody with German text that entire congregations knew by heart, and, by extension, Bach's four-part harmonized settings of such hymns — still the universal model for teaching harmony.
- **Consonance** — an interval that sounds stable and at rest, needing no continuation. The octave and perfect fifth are the "perfect" consonances (pure and open); thirds and sixths are "imperfect" (warmer, sweeter).
- **Contrary motion** — two voices moving in opposite directions at once, one rising while the other falls. The most prized kind of motion in counterpoint, because it keeps the voices maximally distinct to the ear.
- **Counterpoint** — the craft of combining two or more simultaneous melodies, each independently singable, so that together they also make correct and expressive harmony. From the Latin *punctus contra punctum*, "point against point."
- **Dissonance** — an interval that sounds tense and unstable, demanding motion toward a consonance: seconds, sevenths, the tritone, and (against the bass, in this style) the fourth. In tonal counterpoint dissonance is not an error but the engine of expression — provided it is prepared and resolved.
- **Dominant** — the chord (or key) built a fifth above the tonic; harmony's natural counterweight to home. Its characteristic tension pulls music back toward the tonic, and that pull powers most cadences.
- **Fugue** — a contrapuntal procedure (not a fixed form) in which several voices state and then discuss a single theme, the subject, entering one at a time in imitation. The subject of [the third essay](craft-03-fugue.md).
- **Galant style** — the fashionable idiom of the mid-eighteenth century, cultivated by Bach's sons among others: one graceful melody over light, simple accompaniment, prizing naturalness and charm over contrapuntal elaboration.
- **Harmony** — the vertical dimension of music: the chords formed when pitches sound together, and the grammar governing how one chord follows another. In Bach, harmony and counterpoint are two descriptions of the same fabric.
- **Homophony** — texture in which one melody leads and all other parts accompany it (tune plus chords). The complement of polyphony.
- **Imitation** — the device of one voice echoing another voice's melody shortly after it is heard, making the voices audibly related. Flexible imitation underlies fugue; exact, sustained imitation is canon.
- **Interval** — the distance between two pitches, counted in scale steps: C to D is a second, C to E a third, C to G a fifth, C to the next C an octave. Counterpoint's basic unit of measurement.
- **Inversion (melodic)** — turning a melody upside down: every rising interval becomes a falling one of the same size, and vice versa, so the shape is mirrored.
- **Invertible counterpoint** — counterpoint engineered so that its voices can exchange registers — the top line moved below the bottom line — and still produce correct harmony. It lets one worked-out combination serve a piece in multiple arrangements.
- **Key** — the gravitational system of a piece of tonal music: a home pitch (the tonic), its scale, and the network of chords built on that scale, within which every note has a felt degree of stability or tension.
- **Mode** — one of the scale-systems of medieval and Renaissance music (Dorian, Phrygian, and so on), each with its own pattern of steps and its own character; the ancestors of the modern major and minor keys. Stile antico counterpoint was worked out in the modes.
- **Neighbor tone** — a brief dissonance made by stepping from a consonant note to the note directly above or below it and back again. Like the passing tone, it is legal because it moves by step on a weak beat.
- **Oblique motion** — one voice moving while another holds its note. The motion that produces suspensions.
- **Parallel motion** — two voices moving in the same direction by the same distance. Forbidden at the perfect fifth and octave, because at those intervals the voices blend so completely that they fuse into one — and a voice's independence dies.
- **Passing tone** — a dissonant note permitted because it is in transit: it steps between two consonant notes, on a weak beat, tension used as a corridor rather than a destination. The first legal dissonance a species-counterpoint student meets.
- **Polyphony** — texture made of several simultaneous melodies of equal importance ("many voices"). What counterpoint produces; the complement of homophony.
- **Sequence** — the immediate restatement of a musical idea at a higher or lower pitch level, often several times in a row, so the harmony travels stepwise through the key. A basic engine of motion in Baroque music.
- **Species counterpoint** — the graded training method, codified in Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725), in which a student writes a new voice against a cantus firmus under progressively loosening constraints — five stages ("species") running from note-against-note to free, florid writing.
- **Stile antico** — Italian for "old style": the deliberately preserved strict vocal counterpoint of the Palestrina era, used in the eighteenth century as a learned, archaic church idiom — music's equivalent of writing in Latin.
- **Subject** — the theme of a fugue: the melody stated alone at the outset, which every voice must take up and which the whole piece examines.
- **Suspension** — the classic prepared dissonance: a voice holds a consonant note while the other voices move, so the held note becomes dissonant on a strong beat, then resolves by falling one step. The prepare–suspend–resolve cycle is the machinery of "leaning" expression throughout Bach.
- **Texture** — the way a piece's simultaneous layers relate to one another: one leading melody with accompaniment (homophony), several equal melodies (polyphony), or anything between.
- **Thoroughbass** — also **figured bass**: the Baroque practice of writing only a bass line with small numbers ("figures") indicating the intervals to be sounded above it, from which a keyboard player improvises full harmony in real time. The era's working harmonic literacy, and the foundation of Bach's own teaching sequence.
- **Tonic** — the home pitch and home chord of a key: the point of maximum rest, where a tonal piece's tensions ultimately resolve.
- **Triad** — the basic chord of tonal music: three pitches stacked in thirds (such as C–E–G), the unit from which the era's harmony is built.
- **Voice** — a single melodic strand within a contrapuntal texture, whether sung or played; a keyboard fugue "in four voices" weaves four simultaneous melodic lines. Counting the voices is the first step in studying any contrapuntal piece.
- **Voice-leading** — the art of moving each voice from its current note to its next: keeping lines stepwise and singable, managing the four types of motion, and treating dissonance correctly, so that every voice remains a coherent melody while the harmony stays sound.
