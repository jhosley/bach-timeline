Bach left almost no words about how music should be made — no treatise, no essays, a handful of terse letters. The great exception is one page long: the **title page of the Inventions and Sinfonias** (1723), the closest thing we have to his teaching philosophy in his own hand. It deserves reading slowly, because every clause is a claim — and because the thirty small pieces behind it constitute, when read as a single designed object, the most complete course in musical thinking any composer has ever left. This essay reads the page clause by clause, then walks through the method it announces: what *invention* meant in Bach's world, what the two-voice and three-voice disciplines actually train, what the famous word *cantabile* is doing in a beginners' book, and how a student today should put the set to work.

## One page, three promises

An *upright guide*, the title page begins, by which lovers of the keyboard are shown a plain way **"(1) to learn to play cleanly in two voices, but also, after further progress, (2) to deal correctly and well with three obbligato parts"** — obbligato meaning *obligatory*: three real voices, none of them filler — and along the way **"to acquire good inventiones, and to develop the same well,"** and **"most of all to achieve a cantabile style of playing, and alongside all this to gain a strong foretaste of composition."**

Three promises, ascending. The first is mechanical: hand-independence, two voices then three — a **voice**, throughout this essay, meaning one continuous melodic line in the texture, as if a singer were assigned to it, even when all the lines come from one player's two hands. The third promise is the one every pianist quotes: *cantabile* — the singing touch. But the middle promise is the radical one, and the rest of this essay lives inside it: that a beginner, by playing these pieces, will learn *to have good musical ideas and to develop them well*. No method book before or since has promised that so flatly, and none has delivered it so reliably.

The object built to keep these promises is exactly symmetrical: fifteen **Inventions** in two voices, fifteen **Sinfonias** in three — *sinfonia* here meaning simply a three-voice keyboard piece, not a symphony — each set arranged in ascending order of **key**, the home pitch and scale a piece is built on, from C major up to B minor. The fifteen keys are the usable center of the tonal map, nothing with a forest of sharps or flats; the exotic corners are deliberately reserved for [the Well-Tempered Clavier](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md), the next book up the staircase. And the set had a domestic prehistory: earlier versions of these pieces, [begun in Wilhelm Friedemann's notebook in 1720](../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md) for a nine-year-old, carried the older titles *Praeambula* and *Fantasias*. When Bach made the 1723 fair copy he reordered them and — the telling act — *renamed* them. A praeambulum is named for its function: a thing you play before something else. An *inventio* is named for a mental act. The new title announces that these pieces are not warm-ups; they are demonstrations of how a musical idea is found and grown.

## Inventio: composing as rhetoric

The word hides in plain sight, borrowed from an unexpected discipline. ***Inventio*** is a term of classical **rhetoric** — the art of constructing persuasive discourse, the backbone of every Latin school curriculum in Bach's Germany. In the rhetoric of Cicero and his heirs, *inventio* is the orator's first task: not "making something up," but ***finding* the core idea of your argument** — surveying the subject until you locate the thesis rich enough to carry a whole oration. Only then come the later stages: development of the idea (*elaboratio*) and its arrangement into an effective order (*dispositio*). Bach and his contemporaries — schooled, [as he was](../events/ohrdruf/lyceum-scholar.md), on Cicero — thought about composing in exactly these terms. German music theory of his era spoke openly of *musica poetica*, music-making as a branch of rhetoric; Laurence Dreyfus's modern study of Bach's craft takes its title, *Bach and the Patterns of Invention*, from precisely this tradition. A piece of music was an argument; its opening idea was its thesis; the composer's craft was to find a thesis *rich enough to sustain development*, then develop it exhaustively.

This reframing matters because it dissolves the Romantic picture of composition — the lightning-strike of inspiration, the melody received whole from the muse — that still quietly shapes how students think. In Bach's workshop, the quality of an idea is not how pretty it sounds but *how much it implies*. The technical name for such an idea is a **motive**: a short, distinctive melodic-rhythmic figure, usually just a handful of notes, that a piece treats as its basic building block. A good motive, in this school, must pass engineering tests. It must outline harmony clearly enough that a second voice can converse with it. It must survive **inversion** — being turned upside down, every rising **interval** (the distance between two pitches) becoming a falling one and vice versa, the melodic shape mirrored — and still sound like itself. It must break into fragments that remain recognizable. It must tolerate being restated at different pitch levels without absurdity. *Finding* a motive means testing candidates against these criteria; the Inventions are fifteen worked examples of candidates that passed, each a different *kind* of seed, so that the student meets the whole taxonomy of viable ideas.

One distinction, since the vocabulary will matter later: this essay says *motive* for the Inventions' seeds and reserves **subject** for the longer, more formally treated theme of a **fugue** — the strict imitative genre [the third craft essay](craft-03-fugue.md) takes up. The line between them is real but soft; Invention subjects are motives promoted to protagonists, and by [the fifteenth Invention](../works/cothen/bwv-786.md) the promotion is nearly complete.

## Two voices: the grammar of economy

Now watch the method work. Open [Invention No. 1 in C major](../works/cothen/bwv-772.md): the first seven notes — a rising scale-step figure with a little arpeggio turn — are the *inventio*, and the entire piece is that figure examined: traded between the hands, run up and down in **sequence**, turned upside down by inversion. Nothing else happens in the piece. That's the lesson: not "here is a nice tune," but *here is how much music one well-chosen idea contains*.

The operations deserve plain definitions, because they are the verbs of the whole language. **Imitation** is one voice stating the motive and another voice repeating it shortly after, at the same or a different pitch — the fundamental gesture of contrapuntal conversation, the musical equivalent of "yes, and." A **sequence** is the immediate restatement of a figure at a stepwise higher or lower pitch level, usually two or three times in a row; it is how Baroque music *travels*, each restatement pulling the harmony one station along its journey. Inversion, already defined, is how a motive doubles its vocabulary without adding a note of new material. And beneath all three sits the discipline's quiet masterpiece: **invertible counterpoint at the octave** — also called **double counterpoint** — the craft of writing two simultaneous lines so that they can *trade places*, the top voice becoming the bottom and vice versa, and still make correct harmony. This is harder than it sounds, because intervals transform when voices swap: a sixth becomes a third, a third a sixth, and the stable fifth becomes a tense fourth, so a passage written carelessly collapses when flipped. Bach writes almost every Invention so that its two voices are exchangeable, which is why the pieces feel so democratic — either hand may hold the argument at any moment, and neither is ever mere accompaniment. That refusal of accompaniment is the core value the [first craft essay](craft-01-counterpoint.md) laid out; the Inventions are where a player's hands first *enact* it.

Each of the fifteen poses the finding-problem with a different kind of seed, and the variety is the curriculum. [Invention No. 2 in C minor](../works/cothen/bwv-773.md) plants [a canon-ready line](../works/cothen/bwv-773.md) — a long, singing melody secretly built so that the left hand can follow the right in strict **canon**, exact imitation sustained for bars at a stretch, the melody accompanying itself. [No. 4 in D minor](../works/cothen/bwv-775.md) spins a whirling triadic figure that works as hard inverted as upright, the set's first taste of storm. [No. 6 in E major](../works/cothen/bwv-777.md) is the rhythm lesson: one voice in steady notes while the other leans across the beat in **syncopation** — notes tied over the pulse so their accents fall where the beat isn't — and then the hands trade jobs at the double bar, a live demonstration of invertible counterpoint inside the set's clearest **binary shape**, a two-halves-each-repeated form borrowed from the dance suite. [No. 8 in F major](../works/cothen/bwv-779.md) is [a chase figure](../works/cothen/bwv-779.md), a bugle-call arpeggio pursued by its own echo at one bar's distance, imitation experienced as pure energy. And three pages later, [No. 9 in F minor](../works/cothen/bwv-780.md) is [a chromatic lament](../works/cothen/bwv-780.md) — **chromaticism** meaning motion through the pitches *between* the scale steps, the semitone slippages that European ears have always heard as intensified feeling — its expressive weight carried by **suspensions**: one voice holding its note while the other moves, creating a **dissonance** (a tense, unstable interval) that must lean and then **resolve** by step into consonance. [Invention No. 11](../works/cothen/bwv-782.md) makes that suspension technique a study in itself. The adjacency is the deepest joke in the book: the giddiest piece and the most grief-stricken sit almost side by side, built from the same toolbox, teaching a student that in this tradition the *affections* — the codified emotional characters of Baroque music — are adjacent teachable techniques, not moods waited for.

Two more stations complete the two-voice course. [No. 13 in A minor](../works/cothen/bwv-784.md) is the form lesson: a restless arpeggiated motive driven through the set's most sweeping sequences, tracing the standard tonal journey — away from the **tonic** (the home key and its anchoring chord), through the **dominant** (the key or chord built a fifth above home, tonal music's natural counterweight and source of forward pressure) or, in minor-key pieces, the **relative major** (the major key sharing the same notes as the home minor scale, its bright twin), and home again — each station marked by a **cadence**, the formulaic harmonic close that functions as musical punctuation. Hear that journey once and you hold the seed of every larger tonal form you will ever study. And [No. 15 in B minor](../works/cothen/bwv-786.md) is the exit exam: a crisp subject featuring a written-out **trill** figure — the rapid alternation of a note with its upper neighbor, ornament promoted to structure — treated with nearly fugal strictness, the two-voice course ending with a deliberate glance at the road ahead.

## Three obbligato parts: the Sinfonias

The second half of the book re-poses every problem with three voices, and the difficulty does not increase by half — it changes in kind. Two voices are a dialogue; three are a society. There is now a middle voice, which must be a real melody (that is the force of *obbligato*) while living in the least grateful register, shared between the thumbs, forever crossing from hand to hand. The player must know, at every instant, which voice carries the motive and which two are in supporting counterpoint — and the supporting parts must remain singable lines, never congealing into mere chords. **Voice leading** — the craft of moving each line smoothly and correctly from note to note — becomes three-dimensional. [Sinfonia No. 1 in C major](../works/cothen/bwv-787.md) makes the pedagogy explicit by rebuilding the world of Invention No. 1 — same daylight key, same rising-scale DNA — with the third voice added, so the student measures exactly what changed.

The summit of the set, by common consent, is [the F minor Sinfonia's](../works/cothen/bwv-795.md) three simultaneous subjects carrying real grief: a halting, sigh-broken figure, a chromatic descent, and a winding lament, combined in **triple counterpoint** — three-voice invertible counterpoint, meaning any of the three subjects can appear in any voice, top, middle, or bottom, and the harmony holds through every permutation. Across the piece Bach rotates the combinations like grief examined from every side. It is a Passion movement's substance inside a teaching book, and it states the set's deepest thesis: development *is* expression; the method and the meaning never separate. Then, characteristically, the course refuses to end in solemnity — [the final B minor Sinfonia](../works/cothen/bwv-801.md) is a whirling nine-sixteen dance, three-voice clarity tested at speed, graduation as earned play.

## Cantabile: the singing hand

Now the third promise, the one that looks like a performance note and turns out to be a philosophy. *Cantabile* — Italian, "singable," "in a singing manner" — is a strange demand to make of Bach's instruments. The harpsichord plucks and cannot swell a note; the clavichord whispers. Neither sustains the way a voice does. To play *cantabile* on such machines is therefore not a matter of pressing more expressively; it is a matter of **articulation** — the joining and separating of consecutive notes — and of timing, of shaping a line so the ear supplies the breath and the lean the instrument cannot. It means phrasing each voice the way a singer would: finding where it breathes, which note is its peak, how it falls away. The practical prerequisite is knowing the line *as a line* — which is why the discipline of singing each voice of an Invention away from the keyboard is not a supplementary exercise but the essay's whole argument in miniature.

Notice what the demand does when read backwards, as a criterion for the *composer*: if every voice must be playable in a singing style, every voice must be *worth singing* — must be a real melody with a shape, not filler. The performer's test and the composer's test are the same test. This is the sense in which the title page's third promise and its final one — the *strong foretaste of composition* — are one promise. And it is why the small print of the pieces matters: the **ornaments**, the little melodic decorations marked with signs — trills, mordents, turns — for which Bach had written out a table of execution, the famous *Explication*, in the same [1720 notebook](../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md) where these pieces were born. In this school ornaments are not frosting; they are melody at its most concentrated, dissonance and resolution compressed into a flick of the finger — which is why [Invention No. 15](../works/cothen/bwv-786.md) can take a trill and make it the structural motive of a whole piece.

Two features of this pedagogy are worth stealing for any kind of study. First, **it teaches composition and performance as one literacy.** A student who has traced how Invention 1 grows from its seed doesn't just play it better — they can *write* a passable invention, and Bach's students did, as assignments. He would give them a **figured bass** — a bass line annotated with numerals specifying the harmonies to be filled in above it, the era's working shorthand for harmony — or a subject, and demand elaboration; composition was learned the way languages are: by speaking, from day one, inside strict grammar. Second, **the constraints are the curriculum.** Two voices only; one idea only; every note accounted for. Freedom, in this classroom, is what you earn by exhausting a constraint — a principle that scales all the way up: [the Goldberg Variations](../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md) are the Inventions' method under a thirty-two-bar constraint; [the Art of Fugue](../works/leipzig/art-of-fugue.md) under a single subject for an entire book.

## The staircase to fugue

What, precisely, do the Inventions *not* teach? The strict protocols of fugue — and the gap is instructive, because it is exactly one step wide. A fugue, as [the next essay](craft-03-fugue.md) unpacks, opens with its subject alone, answers it in the dominant (the transposed second entry is called the **answer**), maintains a fixed number of voices, and alternates entries of the subject with **episodes** — connective passages that develop fragments of the subject, usually in sequence. An Invention is freer on every count: imitation typically happens at the octave rather than the fifth, no answer is obligatory, and the texture may thin or thicken at need. But look at the skills the freer genre has quietly installed: hearing a motive's every entry across voices; sustaining invertible counterpoint; building episodes from sequence; managing a tonal journey between cadences; keeping three obbligato lines singing at once. Those *are* the fugue skills. The Inventions are fugue with the formalities relaxed, which is why a student who has genuinely finished them finds the fugues of the Well-Tempered Clavier strenuous but never foreign. Early accounts of Bach's teaching describe exactly this sequence — the Inventions first, then suites, then [the Well-Tempered Clavier](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md) — a graded staircase from the first two-voice piece to the five-voice fugue.

The pipeline these little pieces fed was concrete and domestic: [begun in Friedemann's notebook](../events/cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md) for a nine-year-old, refined for the parade of private students who lived and learned [in the Bach household](../events/leipzig-1/cantors-household.md), and aimed squarely at [the Well-Tempered Clavier](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md), whose title page — "for the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning" — is the same voice, one flight up. The whole keyboard curriculum was a single designed ascent, and it still works: it is, essentially unchanged, the counterpoint sequence conservatories teach today.

One last thing the title page says between the lines. Bach wrote it [at Cöthen](../eras/cothen.md), a Kapellmeister with no church duties, at the height of his instrumental powers — and what he chose to perfect, alongside the Brandenburgs and [the solo-violin monuments](../works/cothen/sei-solo.md), was a beginners' book. Teaching was not what Bach did when he couldn't compose; it was one of the forms his composing took. The thirty little pieces are as finished as anything he made — which is why, alone in his output, [they have never once been out of use](../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) since the ink dried.

## How to use the set: a study method

Everything above condenses into a working method — usable whether you play well, a little, or not at all (a score and a recording will carry a non-player through every step but the fourth). Take the pieces one at a time; a common modern path opens with Nos. 1, 4, 8, and 13, then the minor-key interiors, then the Sinfonias in the same spirit.

- **Find the inventio.** Before anything else, isolate the motive: the opening handful of notes, before the second voice enters. Sing it. Then mark — pencil in the score — *every* entry of it in either voice, including inverted and fragmentary ones. In [Invention No. 1](../works/cothen/bwv-772.md) there are dozens; when your count stops growing, you know the piece.
- **Name the operations.** For each passage, say what is being *done* to the motive: imitation, sequence, inversion, fragmentation. Mark where the voices swap material — invertible counterpoint caught in the act. If a passage resists naming, you have found either an episode or the piece's one free gesture; both are worth knowing.
- **Map the journey.** Find the cadences and label the keys they close in: tonic, dominant, relative major or minor. Hear the piece once as pure travel — departure, farthest point, return. This is the skeleton every larger form will share.
- **Practice each voice as a song.** Hands separately until each line phrases like a singer — breathing points, peak notes, cantabile articulation. Then, in the Sinfonias, every *pair* of voices before all three. Play one voice while singing the other; it is the fastest ear-training the literature offers.
- **Take the foretaste literally.** Write eight bars. Take the motive of the Invention you have just studied — or a motive of your own that passes its tests — and develop it for two voices: an imitative opening, one sequence, one inversion, a cadence. It will be derivative and stiff, and it will teach you more about Bach's choices than a month of listening; this was, as far as we can tell, exactly the assignment his own students received.
- **Graduate deliberately.** When a two-voice piece is truly finished — playable, mapped, sung, imitated — take the corresponding step up: its Sinfonia neighbors, and eventually [the forty-eight](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md), where [the fugue essay](craft-03-fugue.md) and its practical liturgy are waiting.

The method is slow by design. Its unit is not the piece but the *idea* — found, developed, sung — and thirty ideas, honestly worked, are a compositional education. That is the wager of the one page of pedagogy Bach ever wrote, and three centuries of keyboards have yet to find it wrong.

## Terms of Art

*The essay's working vocabulary, gathered and defined — generously, assuming no prior knowledge.*

- **Answer** — In a fugue, the second voice's statement of the subject, transposed to the dominant (a fifth away from home). Inventions generally imitate at the octave instead, which is one of the genre's freedoms.
- **Binary shape** — A form in two halves, each usually repeated: the first half travels away from the home key, the second returns. The standard shape of Baroque dance movements; Invention No. 6 borrows it.
- **Cadence** — A formulaic harmonic close, the musical equivalent of punctuation. Cadences mark the stations of a piece's journey through its keys.
- **Canon** — The strictest imitation: one voice exactly reproduces another's whole line at a fixed time-lag, so the melody accompanies itself. Invention No. 2 sustains a canon at the octave for most of its length.
- **Cantabile** — Italian for "singable": playing so each line phrases like a singer, with connected notes, breathing points, and a shaped peak. Bach's title page names it the chief goal of the set.
- **Chromaticism** — Melodic or harmonic motion using the pitches between the ordinary scale steps (the piano's "extra" semitones). Baroque ears heard chromatic slippage as intensified emotion — grief above all.
- **Counterpoint** — The craft of combining two or more simultaneous melodies, each a real tune on its own, which together also make correct harmony. The discipline underlying everything in this essay.
- **Dissonance and resolution** — A dissonance is a tense, unstable interval between voices; resolution is its move to a stable (consonant) interval, normally by step. Handled by rule, the tension reads as feeling rather than error.
- **Dominant** — The key or chord built on the fifth degree of the scale, a fifth above the tonic. Tonal music's natural counterweight: moving to the dominant creates the pressure that makes returning home meaningful.
- **Episode** — In fugue (and loosely in inventions), a connective passage that develops fragments of the main idea — usually in sequence — between full statements of it.
- **Figured bass** — A bass line annotated with numerals indicating the harmonies to be improvised or written above it; the everyday harmonic shorthand of the Baroque, and one of Bach's standard teaching assignments.
- **Fugue** — A strict imitative genre: a subject stated alone, answered in the dominant, carried through a fixed number of voices, with episodes between later entries. The Inventions' destination genre, treated in the next essay.
- **Imitation** — One voice stating a figure and another repeating it shortly after, at the same or another pitch. The basic gesture of contrapuntal conversation.
- **Interval** — The distance in pitch between two notes, named by counting scale steps: third, fifth, octave, and so on. Counterpoint's rules are largely rules about intervals.
- **Inventio** — In classical rhetoric, the first task of the orator: *finding* the core idea of an argument, from which everything else is developed and arranged. Bach's title borrows the word to name the act of finding a musical idea rich enough to sustain a piece.
- **Invention** — Bach's name for a short two-voice keyboard piece grown entirely from one motive. Before him the word was not a standard genre title; the fifteen of 1723 made it one.
- **Inversion (melodic)** — Turning a melody upside down: every rising interval becomes an equal falling one and vice versa, mirroring the shape. A well-made motive survives inversion recognizably.
- **Invertible counterpoint at the octave** — Two-voice writing crafted so the voices can trade places — top becoming bottom — and still make correct harmony; also called double counterpoint. Tricky because intervals transform when voices swap (sixths become thirds, fifths become fourths).
- **Key** — The home pitch and scale a piece is built on (C major, F minor), together with the gravitational pull that home exerts. Pieces travel between related keys and return; the set presents fifteen keys in ascending order.
- **Motive** — A short, distinctive melodic-rhythmic figure — often just a few notes — treated as a piece's basic building block. The Inventions each grow from a single motive.
- **Obbligato** — Italian for "obligatory": a part that is a real, required melodic line, not optional filler or accompaniment. The title page promises three obbligato parts — three genuine voices.
- **Ornament** — A small melodic decoration — trill, mordent, turn — usually marked by a sign rather than written in full notes. In Bach's school, concentrated melody rather than frosting; he left a table of their execution in Friedemann's 1720 notebook.
- **Relative key** — The major and minor keys that share the same notes (C major and A minor; F minor and A-flat major). A minor-key piece's most natural first destination is its relative major.
- **Sequence** — Immediate restatement of a figure at a stepwise higher or lower pitch level, typically two or three times running. The engine of harmonic travel in Baroque music.
- **Sinfonia** — Bach's title for the three-voice pieces of the set (in earlier sources, "Fantasias"); in this context simply "three-part invention," unrelated to the later symphony.
- **Subject** — The principal theme of a fugue or fugue-like piece, longer and more formally treated than a motive; in fugue it is stated alone at the outset and returns throughout. The F minor Sinfonia combines three subjects at once.
- **Suspension** — A voice holds its note while the others move, creating a dissonance that then resolves downward by step: preparation, clash, release. The Baroque's most reliable device for making counterpoint ache.
- **Syncopation** — Accents falling where the beat isn't, typically by tying notes across the pulse. Invention No. 6 is the set's dedicated syncopation study.
- **Tonic** — The home note, chord, and key of a piece — the place of rest that everything else is heard against. Pieces in this tradition begin and end on it.
- **Trill** — The rapid alternation of a note with its upper neighbor, the most common Baroque ornament. Invention No. 15 writes one out in full notes and builds its motive from it.
- **Triple counterpoint** — Invertible counterpoint for three voices: three simultaneous lines written so that any line can occupy any position (top, middle, bottom) and the harmony still works — six possible arrangements. The F minor Sinfonia's machinery.
- **Voice** — One continuous melodic line in a texture, as if assigned to a single singer — even when one player's hands supply all of them. Two-voice and three-voice writing means two or three such lines, each with its own integrity.
- **Voice leading** — The craft of moving each voice smoothly and correctly from note to note as harmonies change: steps preferred to leaps, dissonances prepared and resolved, lines kept singable.
