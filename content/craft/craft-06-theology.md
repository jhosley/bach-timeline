Handle this topic with tongs, because it attracts two opposite errors. The first — the Romantic error — makes Bach a mystic whose every interval encodes a sermon, finds the Trinity in every group of three, and counts letter-numbers until B+A+C+H=14 explains the universe. The second — the modernist error — makes him a pure structuralist whose church employment was incidental, the texts mere scaffolding for abstract design. The documents support neither, and this timeline's whole method — *documented, tradition, disputed* — matters more here than anywhere. So: what do we actually know?

We know his library. The estate inventory lists over **eighty theological volumes** — two complete editions of Luther, the major orthodox commentators — a working pastor's shelf, in a musician's house. We have his own **Bible**, the three-volume Calov edition with commentary, rediscovered in Michigan in the 1930s: read, underlined, and *annotated in Bach's hand*. Beside 1 Chronicles 25 — David appointing the temple musicians — he wrote: "This chapter is the true foundation of all God-pleasing church music." And beside 2 Chronicles 5, where music fills the temple and the glory descends: **"NB. Where there is devotional music, God with his grace is always present."** That sentence, in his own ink, is the closest thing we have to Bach's theology of his art — and note what it claims: not that music *describes* grace, but that it is an occasion of *presence*. We also have the habits: **J.J.** (*Jesu juva* — "Jesus, help") at the head of scores; **S.D.G.** (*Soli Deo Gloria* — "to God alone the glory") at the end — on church cantatas *and* on secular and instrumental works. And we have [the 1708 *Endzweck* letter](../events/muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md) and [the Orgelbüchlein's couplet](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md): God's honor and the neighbor's instruction, declared as one purpose. The frame is documented. The question is how far down into the notes it goes.

Here is a sober middle way, in three layers of decreasing certainty.

**Layer one — rhetoric, essentially certain.** Bach's era held that music's job was to move the *affections* (the codified passions — grief, joy, rage), and that specific figures carried specific meanings: the descending chromatic line for lament ([BWV 12](../works/weimar/bwv-12.md), the Crucifixus), sharp dissonance for sin and piercing ([*Buß und Reu*](../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md)), rising scales for resurrection, the halo of strings around Christ's words. This isn't speculation; it's the period's own theory, taught in its textbooks, and Bach deploys it with documentable consistency. Reading these figures is reading the music's *public* language.

**Layer two — structure as meaning, strong but interpretive.** Sometimes the architecture itself preaches, and the design is too pointed to dismiss. [The St. Anne fugue's](../works/leipzig/bwv-552.md) three-in-one construction in a volume of catechism music; [the Actus tragicus's](../works/muehlhausen/bwv-106.md) old-covenant fugue dissolving under a new-covenant solo voice — *law and grace argued contrapuntally*; the [Mass's](../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md) *Dona nobis pacem* returning the *Gratias* music, so that asking-for-peace and giving-thanks are revealed as one act; [the chorale quotations](craft-05-chorale.md) that let one text comment silently on another. Here theology isn't decoration on the counterpoint — the counterpoint *is* the argument. [Gardiner](../people/john-eliot-gardiner.md)'s fine phrase for the whole phenomenon: Bach as a composer who "makes theology audible."

**Layer three — number and cipher, mostly disputed.** Fourteen as B-A-C-H, [membership number fourteen](../events/leipzig-3/mizler-1747.md), counted notes and gematria: the timeline flags nearly all of it *tradition or worse*. Some symbolism is real but blatant (three flats and three subjects for the Trinity — symbolism meant to be *noticed*); the hidden-number industry, by contrast, mostly discovers what it imports. Rule of thumb: if a symbol required a calculator to find, Bach's congregation couldn't hear it — and everything documented about him ([the neighbor's instruction](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md)) says he wrote to be heard.

And then the finding that reframes the whole question: **the sacred/secular line barely exists in his practice.** The same *S.D.G.* closes [a birthday serenata](../works/cothen/cothen-serenatas.md); [the parody workshop](../events/leipzig-2/christmas-oratorio-1734.md) moves music from prince to Christ-child without embarrassment; [a coffee-house comedy](../works/leipzig/bwv-211.md) and [a five-voice Kyrie](../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md) come from the same pen in the same seasons. The consistent Lutheran position beneath: *all* well-made work praises its maker; the "secular" is not the ungodly but simply the weekday. If counterpoint was theology for Bach, it was not because fugues smuggled sermons — it was because **ordered, independent voices cohering into harmony was his experience of how creation is put together**, and he practiced that order in whatever room he stood in: church, court, or coffee house.

For your studies, the usable takeaway is a habit of proportion. Read layer one fluently (it's the period's grammar). Argue layer two piece by piece, with the score open. Treat layer three as reception history — interesting for what *later generations* needed Bach to be, which is exactly where [this timeline's next arc](../eras/eclipse.md) begins.
