Bach was formally installed as **Cantor of the Thomasschule** on 1 June 1723, having signed a fourteen-point pledge whose clauses repay reading: he would live soberly, treat the boys kindly, **not leave town without the burgomaster's permission**, keep the music "not too long, nor... operatic in character, but rather incite the listeners to devotion," and teach — the Latin instruction he immediately, with council consent, hired a deputy to cover from his own pocket.

The structure of the post explains the next twenty-seven years of friction. He was simultaneously: schoolmaster in a boarding school of ~55 scholarship boys (his singers — admitted, examined, and drilled by him, the same Mettenchor machine [he had sung in at Lüneburg](../lueneburg/luneburg-scholarship-1700.md)); **director musices** responsible for four churches every Sunday and feast; civic official supplying council-election and university music; and tenant of his employer, raising his family in the school building. His fixed salary was around 100 thalers — the ~700-thaler real income came from **Accidentien**, fees for weddings and funerals, a dependency he described to [Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md) with mordant precision: when the air was healthy, fees fell.

Above him sat not one authority but three, jurisdictionally tangled — council, church consistory, university — a design guaranteeing that every reform required a fight and every fight had a venue.

**Why it matters.** The baseline document of the whole Leipzig positions track. Every later card — [the 1730 memo](../leipzig-2/memo-1730.md), the [prefect war](../leipzig-2/prefect-dispute-1736.md), the [Dresden title-hunting](../leipzig-2/dresden-title-1733-36.md) — unfolds from this pledge's structure: the greatest composer alive had signed on as a *middle manager*, and both parties spent three decades discovering what that meant.
