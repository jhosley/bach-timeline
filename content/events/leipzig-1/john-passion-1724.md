Leipzig's concerted Good Friday Passion Vespers was a young tradition — barely a decade old — and 7 April 1724 brought Bach's first: the **[St. John Passion](../../works/leipzig/st-john-passion.md)**, at the Nikolaikirche (he had planned the Thomaskirche; the council's correction of his printed announcement was an early taste of jurisdiction). The form: John's Passion narrative sung complete by an Evangelist in recitative, the crowd's words erupting from the choir, the action suspended at chosen moments for contemplative arias and chorales — opera's tools, forbidden from looking like opera, turned on the central story of the faith.

John's Gospel gave Bach a specific drama — swift, confrontational, its Christ sovereign even in suffering — and the music matches it: the opening chorus (*Herr, unser Herrscher*) churning over an orchestral surface of extraordinary turbulence; the crowd choruses (*turba* movements) of the trial scene set with a ferocity nothing in the era approaches; the whole engineered as an arch of keys and reprises around the trial's pivot.

Bach never stopped rebuilding it — at least four versions (1725's with a different frame; later restorations), the last left partially revised at his death. And the work's harshest afterlife belongs to the timeline too: its trial scene sets John's "the Jews" verbatim, and the work's modern performance carries [a necessary reckoning](../../eras/living-bach.md) with that text's history — flagged here, treated honestly in the R3 card.

**Why it matters.** The first Leipzig demonstration that the [cantata machine](cantata-machine.md) could scale to monumental drama — and the opening of the Passion thread that culminates [three years later](matthew-passion-1727.md), is silenced by decades of neglect, and then, in 1829, resurrects its composer's name.
