On 2 March 1714 the duke promoted Bach to **Konzertmeister** — a title created for the occasion, ranking below the Kapellmeister and his deputy but salaried above both (250 florins), which tells you what the court actually thought. With it came the duty that opens a new chapter of the works track: **one cantata per month**, performed in the [Himmelsburg](himmelsburg.md) with the full Capelle.

The first was ***Himmelskönig, sei willkommen*** (BWV 182) for Palm Sunday 1714 — followed over the next two years by a run of masterpieces out of all proportion to the schedule's modesty: *Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, Zagen* (BWV 12, whose lament-bass chorus Bach recycled thirty-five years later as the **Crucifixus of the [B minor Mass](../../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md)** — the longest-range self-borrowing in his output), *Ich hatte viel Bekümmernis* (BWV 21), *Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland* (BWV 61).

The craft shift is audible immediately: these cantatas adopt the **modern Italian architecture** — secco recitative (speech-rhythm narration over bare continuo) and the da capo aria — imported by the reform librettist Neumeister's school (Bach set texts by Weimar's court poet Salomon Franck, mostly) and powered by [the freshly absorbed Vivaldi engine](vivaldi-transcriptions.md). The Mühlhausen cantatas were magnificent *old* German church music; from 1714 the cantata is opera's vocabulary in the sanctuary — the form Bach will industrialize at Leipzig.

**Why it matters.** The monthly cycle is the **prototype of the Leipzig weekly machine** — production on a calendar, at quality no deadline should permit. And for study purposes it marks the line: cantatas before 1714 and after belong to different architectural species. Dürr's chronology (this card's authority) turns on exactly that.
