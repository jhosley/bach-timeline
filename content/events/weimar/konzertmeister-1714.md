Court hierarchies were designed to say things plainly, and the order Duke Wilhelm Ernst issued on 2 March 1714 says two things at once. Bach was promoted to Konzertmeister — a title that did not previously exist at Weimar, created for the occasion — and in the table of ranks the new post stood below both the Kapellmeister and his deputy. But the salary attached to it, 250 florins, stood above both. Read the two facts together and you have the court's actual opinion in a single document: the old Kapellmeister Drese held the dignity, his son held the succession, and the organist held the value. The promotion arrived, not coincidentally, within weeks of [the Halle affair](halle-gambit-1713.md) — Weimar paying to keep what another town had just tried to hire — and it came with the clause that opens a new chapter of the works track: Bach was now obliged to compose one cantata per month, performed in the [Himmelsburg](himmelsburg.md) with the full Capelle.

It was his first regular composing duty — remarkable to say of a man approaching thirty, but true: until this order, Bach had been paid to play, and composition was what he did around the job. Now the calendar itself demanded new music, and he answered it immediately. The first cantata under the new duty was *Himmelskönig, sei willkommen* (BWV 182), for Palm Sunday 1714, and over the next two years the monthly schedule yielded a run of masterpieces out of all proportion to its modesty: *Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, Zagen* (BWV 12), *Ich hatte viel Bekümmernis* (BWV 21), *Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland* (BWV 61). One of these carries the timeline's longest thread. The lament chorus of BWV 12, built over a grinding chromatic bass, stayed in its composer's mind for thirty-five years until he took it up again and re-texted it as the Crucifixus of the [B minor Mass](../../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md) — the longest-range self-borrowing in his output. When the dying Bach assembled his sacred summa, the darkest movement he could find in a lifetime's work was one he had written as a newly promoted Konzertmeister for the gallery in the Himmelsburg ceiling.

The craft shift in these pieces is audible from the first entrance, and it is why the promotion matters to listeners and not just to biographers. The Weimar cantatas adopt the modern Italian architecture: secco recitative — speech-rhythm narration over bare continuo, opera's connective tissue — and the da capo aria, imported into Lutheran church music by the reform school of the librettist Neumeister (Bach's own texts came mostly from Weimar's court poet, Salomon Franck). And driving the whole is [the freshly absorbed Vivaldi engine](vivaldi-transcriptions.md): the promotion arrived within months of the transcription project, so Bach's first sustained cantata production began at the precise moment his sense of form had been rebuilt on Italian lines. The timing is the era's great fortune. The Mühlhausen cantatas were magnificent *old* German church music, motet-built, rooted in the previous century; from 1714 the cantata speaks opera's vocabulary in the sanctuary — recitative, aria, ritornello — and it never goes back. This is the form Bach will industrialize at Leipzig, arriving here fully specified.

Two reasons, then, to mark this date heavily. The first is prophetic: the monthly cycle is the prototype of the Leipzig weekly machine — composition on a calendar, deadlines met with music of a quality no deadline should permit. Everything the Leipzig years reveal about Bach as a producer, the discipline of manufacturing masterpieces on schedule, is rehearsed here at one-quarter speed. The second is practical, for anyone using this timeline to study: 1714 is the line. Cantatas before it and after it belong to different architectural species, and knowing which side of the line a piece falls on tells you most of what you need to know about how it will behave. Dürr's chronology of the cantatas — this card's authority — turns on exactly that division. A court order about rank and salary turns out to be one of the sharpest stylistic boundaries in the repertoire; the paperwork and the music, as so often in this life, are the same story told in two hands.
