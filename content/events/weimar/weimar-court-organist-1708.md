In June 1708 Bach played before **Duke Wilhelm Ernst of Saxe-Weimar** and was promptly offered the post of court organist and chamber musician; by July the family had moved, and Maria Barbara bore their first child, Catharina Dorothea, in Weimar that December. The salary — about 150 florins plus substantial allowances, with raises to follow in 1711 and 1713 — roughly doubled Mühlhausen, and the symbolism doubled too: five years earlier this same court had carried him on its books as a *lackey*; now he returned as a titled officer of its music.

The employer mattered as much as the money. Wilhelm Ernst was severe, celibate, and devout — a duke who held Bible quizzes for his servants, imposed a 9 p.m. curfew (8 in winter), and preached his own funeral sermon in advance — but his piety funded exactly what Bach sold: **organ music at the highest level**. The duke prized his chapel organist above most of the Capelle, paid accordingly, and financed the organ's renovation to Bach's specification.

For the *Endzweck* declared in [the Mühlhausen resignation](../muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md), Weimar looked like fulfillment: a well-funded chapel, a musical establishment, a sympathetic (if suffocating) patron. The organ decade began at once.

**Why it matters.** The positions track's cleanest trade to date: Bach exchanged a free city's committee politics for a strict duke's deep pockets — and the music followed the funding, as it always did. The era's masterworks are organ works because this employer wanted organ works.
