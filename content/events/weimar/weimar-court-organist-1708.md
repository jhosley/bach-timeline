The Weimar court had employed Bach once before, and the books of that first engagement preserve a small indignity: five years earlier, in the gap between school and his first organ post, this same court had carried the teenage Bach on its rolls as a *lackey* — a liveried servant who happened to be useful with a violin. In June 1708 he came back and played for Duke Wilhelm Ernst of Saxe-Weimar, and whatever the duke heard settled the matter quickly: Bach was promptly offered the post of court organist and chamber musician, and by July the family had moved. That December [Maria Barbara](../../people/maria-barbara-bach.md) bore their first child, Catharina Dorothea, in Weimar. The man the court had once listed among the footmen now returned as a titled officer of its music, and it is hard to believe the symmetry escaped him. Few careers offer so clean a measure of five years' work: same court, same ledgers, different column.

The terms said the rest in the plain arithmetic courts reserved for such statements. The salary — about 150 florins plus substantial allowances, with raises to follow in 1711 and 1713 — roughly doubled what Mühlhausen had paid. For a twenty-three-year-old organist two positions into his career, this was not merely a better job; it was the market's first unambiguous acknowledgment that he had become someone worth competing for.

But the money mattered less than the man paying it, because Wilhelm Ernst was one of the stranger patrons in the timeline, and his strangeness worked almost entirely in Bach's favor. The duke was severe, celibate, and devout to a degree that startled even his own pious century: he held Bible quizzes for his servants, imposed a nine o'clock curfew on his town (eight in winter), and famously preached his own funeral sermon in advance. A court run on those principles wanted nothing from the fashionable end of music — no opera, no French dances after supper. What its master prized, and paid for, was exactly the commodity Bach had spent his youth perfecting: organ music at the highest level, played in a chapel where worship was the court's most serious business. Wilhelm Ernst valued his chapel organist above most of the Capelle, compensated him accordingly, and financed the renovation of the chapel organ to Bach's own specification — an employer not merely tolerating his organist's ambitions for the instrument but underwriting them.

Set this appointment against the letter Bach had just written, and it reads like an answer. Weeks earlier, [resigning from Mühlhausen](../muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md), he had put his life's goal on paper — the *Endzweck*, a well-regulated church music to the glory of God — and named as his obstacle the hindrance and vexation of working where that goal had no institutional support. Weimar looked like the remedy itemized: a well-funded chapel, a standing musical establishment, a patron whose personal piety guaranteed that sacred music would never be an afterthought. That the patron's piety came bundled with curfews and catechism quizzes — sympathetic and suffocating in the same gesture — is a tension the era would take nine years to resolve, and would resolve badly. But in July 1708 the ledger showed only the credit side, and the organ decade began at once: the years that produced the core of the instrument's repertoire started here, with this employer, in this chapel.

Which is the point this card exists to make, because the trade Bach executed in 1708 is the positions track's cleanest specimen. At Mühlhausen he had served a free city — governance by committee, factions among the clergy, church music dependent on whoever won the last argument. At Weimar he served one strict duke with deep pockets and a single settled conviction about what music was for. Everything that made Wilhelm Ernst oppressive as a sovereign made him ideal as a patron of organ music, and Bach — who would spend his whole career triangulating between what employers wanted and what he meant to write — had for once found the two aligned. The music followed the funding, as it always did in this life: the era's masterworks are organ works because this employer wanted organ works. Later shelves of the timeline repeat the rule with different nouns — a prince who wanted concertos, a council that wanted cantatas — but Weimar states it first and most simply: to understand what Bach wrote, start with who was paying, and for what.
