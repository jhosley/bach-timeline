Sometime in the mid-Weimar years Bach prepared a manuscript book with **164 chorales** laid out by title, one opening each, spanning the entire church year — a complete liturgical organ companion, planned like an architect's elevation. He composed **46** of them before the project (like several of his grandest schemes) was overtaken by life; the empty ruled pages in the surviving autograph are among the most eloquent blanks in music.

What the finished pages contain is the **chorale prelude** perfected as a miniature form: each hymn tune presented once, unadorned or lightly ornamented, over an accompaniment woven from a single figure — and each figure chosen as *interpretation*. Falling chromatic lines under the Fall of Adam; a walking bass of serene tread under trust in God; in the ornamented masterpieces (*O Mensch, bewein* — later the great frame of the St. Matthew Passion's first part in another form), the melody itself dissolving into grief-laden arabesque. Schweitzer built his whole pictorial theory of Bach on this book, and if he overreached, the book invited it.

The title page — added later, at Cöthen, when Bach repurposed the book for teaching — is the timeline's second great mission statement, a rhymed couplet: ***Dem höchsten Gott allein zu Ehren, dem Nächsten, draus sich zu belehren*** — "In honor of the most high God alone; for the neighbor, that he may instruct himself from it." Worship and pedagogy declared as one purpose, on one page.

**Why it matters.** For the Craft section, the chorale-as-classroom essay's primary exhibit: maximum expressive theology at minimum scale, one technique per piece — Bach teaching exactly the way [his own Inventions title page](../../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) promises. And biographically: the planned-164 grid is the encyclopedic instinct's first full blueprint, a decade before the WTC.
