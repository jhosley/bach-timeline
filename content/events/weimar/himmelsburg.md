The palace chapel where Bach worked for nine years was nicknamed the ***Himmelsburg*** — "Heaven's Castle" — and its design is the era's best physical metaphor. The chapel was tall and narrow; at the very top, an opening in the painted ceiling held the **music gallery**: organ, singers, and instrumentalists performed from the heights, invisible or nearly so, their sound descending on the congregation like the ceiling's painted heaven made audible. Theology as architecture; Bach spent a decade composing for a room that treated music, literally, as coming from above.

Practical consequences shaped the works. The gallery was compact — the Weimar cantatas are chamber-scaled, a handful of singers and players, intimate and concentrated where Leipzig's will be civic and broad. And the organ, a Compenius instrument, was renovated under Bach's own direction (1713–14): new bellows, added stops — the timeline's first case of Bach *designing* his primary instrument rather than inheriting it (the [Mühlhausen specification](../muehlhausen/muehlhausen-appointment-1707.md) was drafted for a successor to enjoy).

Around him worked the full apparatus of a small Capelle — singers, string players, the town's church musicians borrowed as needed — and one colleague who mattered: his cousin and friend **Johann Gottfried Walther**, organist of the town church, lexicographer, and fellow transcription addict, with whom Bach traded scores, canons, and (Walther's later dictionary implies) mutual professional respect of the highest order.

**Why it matters.** When you hear a Weimar cantata's inwardness (BWV 12, BWV 182) or the organ works' vertical grandeur, you are hearing this specific room — the timeline's recurring thesis that **acoustics and architecture are compositional forces** — and [Gardiner](../../people/john-eliot-gardiner.md) thought the image essential enough to name his Bach book after it.
