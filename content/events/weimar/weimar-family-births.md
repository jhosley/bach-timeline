The Weimar years filled the Bach household. Catharina Dorothea arrived in December 1708, within months of the move; **Wilhelm Friedemann** — the adored eldest son, future recipient of [the Clavierbüchlein](../cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md) — in November 1710; twins in February 1713 who died within days; **Carl Philipp Emanuel** in March 1714; and Johann Gottfried Bernhard in May 1715. Four survivors, two of them destined to overshadow their father's fame for half a century (a fact Arc II turns on).

The register entry for Emanuel's baptism carries the era's best networking detail: his godfather was **Georg Philipp [Telemann](../../people/telemann.md)** — then at Frankfurt, already the most famous young composer in Germany, and evidently on warm personal terms with Bach. Hold the relationship in view across the timeline: Telemann the friend at the font (1714), [Telemann the first choice for the Leipzig job Bach got](../cothen/leipzig-audition-1723.md) (1722), Telemann the celebrity whose fashionable style the age preferred (Arc II's context). No rivalry documents survive — by every indication the two simply liked each other while the market ranked them — which makes the pairing more instructive, not less.

**Why it matters.** The family track here is also a craft track: this nursery becomes the *workshop* — these are the children for whom the Inventions and the notebooks will be written, and the sons whose training is our best record of the Bach teaching method. And Emanuel, the godson of Germany's most famous composer, will grow up to co-write the Obituary and curate his father's legend: the family's memory machine is being born here alongside the children.
