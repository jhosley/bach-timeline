In December 1713 Bach traveled to Halle, where the organist post at the Liebfrauenkirche stood vacant — the church of the late **Zachow, Handel's teacher**, with a magnificent new organ under construction (63 stops, one of Germany's largest). He auditioned, reportedly composing and performing a cantata on site; the board elected him and sent the contract.

Then he stalled. The salary disappointed him against the duties; letters went back and forth; and meanwhile the Weimar court — informed, one way or another — improved his position substantially (the raise and [the Konzertmeister promotion](konzertmeister-1714.md) followed within weeks). Bach declined Halle. The board wrote him an angry letter accusing him of having used their offer as a **negotiating lever** from the start; his reply survives, wounded and lawyerly — he had sought the post in good faith, he insisted, and could hardly be blamed that his current employer valued him.

Historians split on who to believe; both readings fit the documents, and the card flags the motive as *disputed* while the facts stand. What no one disputes is the epilogue, which is pure Bach: two years later Halle invited him back to **examine the finished organ**, he accepted graciously, and the parties dined together in full cordiality. Professional standards outlived professional grudges.

**Why it matters.** The clearest documented instance of a career-long pattern — outside offers as leverage (compare Cöthen 1717, and the [Dresden title](../leipzig-2/dresden-title-1733-36.md) sought during the Leipzig wars). Musicians had no unions and no contracts worth the name; a rival court's interest was the only bargaining power that existed, and Bach used it with modern fluency.
