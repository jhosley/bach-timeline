In December 1713 Bach traveled to Halle to audition for a job he would never take, and the episode tells us more about how his career actually worked than most of the posts he accepted. The vacancy was a distinguished one: the organist's bench at the Liebfrauenkirche, the church of the late Zachow — Handel's teacher — with a magnificent new organ under construction, sixty-three stops, one of the largest instruments in Germany. For an organist of Bach's ambitions the pull is obvious; an instrument on that scale was an argument in itself. He auditioned in earnest, reportedly composing and performing a cantata on site, and the church board was persuaded: they elected him and sent the contract.

Then he stalled, and the stalling is where the story becomes interesting. The salary disappointed him when he weighed it against the duties; letters went back and forth between Halle and Weimar while the contract sat unsigned. And meanwhile — informed one way or another, as courts always were — Weimar moved. Bach's position improved substantially within weeks: the raise came, and then [the Konzertmeister promotion](konzertmeister-1714.md), a new title created for him with a salary above the Kapellmeister's own. With his home court's counteroffer in hand, Bach declined Halle.

The board did not take it graciously. Their angry letter survives, and its accusation is the one any modern reader will have already formed: that Bach had used their offer as a negotiating lever from the start — that the audition, the cantata, the election had all been theater staged for an audience in Weimar. His reply also survives, wounded and lawyerly in equal measure: he had sought the post in good faith, he insisted, and could hardly be blamed if his current employer, learning of the matter, chose to value him properly. Note what the defense concedes. He does not deny that the Halle offer produced the Weimar raise; he denies only that producing it was the plan.

Which was it? Historians split, and the honest answer is that both readings fit the documents, so this card flags the motive as disputed while the facts stand undisputed on either reading: real audition, real election, real contract, real counteroffer, real refusal. Perhaps Bach genuinely wanted Halle's great organ and was genuinely deterred by the pay; perhaps he never intended to leave a court that was underpaying him and saw the vacancy as the instrument it became. The documents cannot settle it, and there is no reason to assume Bach himself kept the two motives cleanly separated — a working professional rarely does.

What no one disputes is the epilogue, and it is pure Bach. Two years later, the great organ finally finished, Halle invited him back to examine it — the standard engagement for a recognized expert, and a pointed olive branch to the man they had accused of bad faith. He accepted graciously, performed the examination, and the parties dined together in full cordiality. Whatever had been said in the letters, professional standards outlived professional grudges; an organ needed examining, Bach was the man for it, and both sides let competence close the account that pride had opened.

The card earns its shelf space because Halle is the clearest documented instance of a pattern that runs the length of the career: the outside offer as leverage. The same mechanism reappears when the Cöthen Kapellmeistership pries him out of Weimar in 1717, and again — in its most elaborate form — in [the Dresden court title](../leipzig-2/dresden-title-1733-36.md) he pursued for years as armor in his Leipzig wars. It is worth pausing on why the pattern existed, because it was not a personality quirk. An eighteenth-century musician had no union, no professional association, and no contract worth the name against a sovereign employer; the labor market's entire bargaining apparatus consisted of one instrument — a rival's demonstrated interest. Salaries did not rise on merit reviews. They rose when someone else might pay more. Bach, so often cast as an unworldly servant of his art, used that one instrument with entirely modern fluency: quietly, repeatedly, and well. The Halle gambit — if gambit it was — bought him the promotion that gave the world the Weimar cantatas. It is hard to argue with the trade.
