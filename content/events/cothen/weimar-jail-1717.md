The single most startling document in Bach's employment history is a dry note by the Weimar court secretary: on 6 November 1717, the concertmaster and court organist Bach was confined to the district judge's place of detention **"for too stubbornly forcing the issue of his dismissal,"** and on 2 December was released **"with notice of his unfavorable discharge."**

Bach had a signed appointment from Cöthen and salary already accruing there; Duke Wilhelm Ernst — an autocrat who regulated his servants' lives down to fining musicians for missteps — simply refused to release him. Court musicians were not free agents; they were bound servants, and quitting required the ruler's consent. Bach, characteristically, would not drop the matter. Nearly four weeks in detention followed before the Duke gave up on him.

It is worth pausing on what this says about both the man and the era. About the era: this is what "working conditions" meant for even the most gifted musician alive — you could be jailed for trying to change jobs. About the man: this is the same Bach who at Arnstadt had brawled with a bassoonist he'd insulted and overstayed a leave by three months, and who at Leipzig would wage decades of paper war with the town council. His stubbornness in defense of his own worth is one of the most consistent threads in the documentary record — Gardiner reads it as the temperament of a man orphaned at nine who learned early to fight for himself.

**Why it matters.** The freedom Bach found at Cöthen — and the music that poured out of it — was purchased with a month of his life in a cell. It is also a corrective to the plaster-saint image: the documents show a proud, difficult, self-assured professional, not a meek servant of authority.
