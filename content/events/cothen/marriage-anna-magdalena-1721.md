On the third of December, 1721, in his own lodgings and by special princely permission, Johann Sebastian Bach married for the second time. The dispensation to wed at home rather than in church was a standard courtesy to a widower, sparing him the full public ceremony; the parish record notes the arrangement, and the court's books — helpful to biographers in their tireless way — note the tax paid on the Rhine wine laid in for the celebration. The bride was Anna Magdalena Wilcke, twenty years old to his thirty-six, and to describe her as the new wife of the Kapellmeister is to get the emphasis exactly backwards. She was a colleague.

Anna Magdalena came from the trumpet aristocracy of central Germany — her father, grandfather, and uncles were court trumpeters, members of the most jealously guarded of the musical guilds — and she had made her own way as a singer of professional rank. By 1721 she was employed at the Cöthen court as a soprano, and paid, remarkably, on a scale that placed her among the best-compensated members of the entire musical establishment; some registers put her salary at half of Bach's own, which for a twenty-year-old woman in 1721 was an extraordinary figure. How they met requires no speculation at all: [the prince's birthday serenatas](../../works/cothen/cothen-serenatas.md) needed a soprano, and the Kapellmeister directed what the soprano sang. The Bach household had always been a workshop; the workshop was simply acquiring a partner whose credentials the account books could certify.

What she became to the enterprise over the following three decades is one of the quiet epics of music history. She bore thirteen children and buried seven of them. She ran a household that functioned as boarding school, copying shop, and concert agency. And she became her husband's most trusted copyist — her music hand converging on his so closely that scholars battled for generations over which pages were whose. That last fact is no footnote: several major works survive *only* in her copies, most famously [the six cello suites](../../works/cothen/cello-suites.md), for which no autograph exists at all. Every cellist who has ever played that music has been reading, in effect, Anna Magdalena's handwriting. When Bach later assembled [a notebook of keyboard pieces for her](wtc-and-notebooks-1722.md) — the *Clavierbüchlein* that generations of piano students have met without knowing her story — the gift ran both directions: teaching material for the bride, and a window, for us, into what this couple did together at home, which was music, always music.

The nineteenth century, wanting its genius solitary, made her invisible; modern scholarship, led by the ledgers, has restored the truer picture of the Bach marriage as a family firm in the old clan tradition — the same structure that had trained Sebastian himself, now scaled to the largest musical enterprise in Germany. When Bach boasted to his friend [Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md), in the one personal letter we have, that he could *"form a concert, vocal and instrumental, within my own family,"* adding that his wife *"sings a fine soprano,"* he was describing not a hobby but the firm's capacity statement.

Her story's ending belongs to [the eclipse](../../eras/eclipse.md), and it is the darkest fact in this whole timeline: widowed in 1750, shortchanged by the estate's division, she lived her final decade in Leipzig on charity and died in 1760 as what the records call an alms-woman, buried in an unmarked grave by the city her husband had served for twenty-seven years. The reader meeting her here, at her wedding — twenty years old, gifted, well-paid, marrying the greatest musician alive eight days before [the prince's own wedding changed everything](leopold-marriage-1721.md) — deserves to know how the century repaid her. This timeline exists partly so that somebody keeps the account.
