In March 1719 the Cöthen court accounts record a travel disbursement that looks, on its face, like the most ordinary of errands: the Kapellmeister sent to Berlin to collect a newly commissioned harpsichord and see it safely home. Out of that receipt, by the crooked routes history prefers, came the most famous orchestral music of the entire Baroque.

The errand itself tells us things worth knowing. The instrument was a large two-manual harpsichord from the Berlin workshop of Michael Mietke, court builder to the Prussian crown and one of the most admired makers in Germany — an acquisition of real extravagance for a principality Cöthen's size, and further evidence of how seriously Prince Leopold spent on his music. That the prince sent Bach personally, a hundred miles each way, rather than trusting a carter with the crate, says the rest: when you employ the best organ examiner in Germany, a man who tests wind-chests and voicing for a fee, you send him to receive the goods. One imagines Bach in Mietke's workshop, playing the new machine hard before accepting it, the builder watching the way builders watch.

Berlin, meanwhile, offered its own society, and somewhere in the visit Bach played before Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg — uncle of the Prussian king, a fifty-something princeling with a modest household Capelle and a collector's enthusiasm for music. We know the meeting happened because Bach himself tells us, in the one document the whole episode left behind: the dedication he wrote two years later, which recalls that the Margrave had heard him play and had *"deigned to command"* that Bach send him some compositions. Royal requests of that kind were conversational currency — a compliment in the shape of an order — and busy Kapellmeisters presumably let most of them lapse. Bach let this one ripen for two years, and then, [in March 1721](brandenburg-dedication-1721.md), answered it with a calligraphic presentation score of six concertos: the collection posterity would name not for their composer, their court, or their qualities, but for the man who happened to ask.

The harpsichord had its own compositional consequence, and scholars have long connected the two. Among the six concertos Bach eventually sent was one — [the Fifth Brandenburg](../../works/cothen/bwv-1050.md) — unlike anything ever written: a concerto in which the harpsichord, the ensemble's eternal servant, rises out of the accompanying continuo section, seizes the music, and dismisses the entire orchestra for a colossal solo cadenza. It is the birth certificate of the keyboard concerto, the ancestor of every piano concerto from Mozart to Rachmaninoff, and it is difficult not to hear in it the glamour of the court's new Berlin machine — and of the Kapellmeister's own hands, showing colleagues and prince what the acquisition could really do. The scholarly consensus is measured, as ever: the connection is plausible and undocumented. This timeline flags it accordingly and confesses to believing it anyway.

There is a quiet lesson in method here, one this project keeps relearning. The monuments of the repertoire rarely announce themselves in the record; they enter history sideways, through freight receipts and courtesy calls. A crate, a courtier's compliment, a two-year delay — and then six concertos that outlived the kingdom of Prussia itself. Watch the errands.
