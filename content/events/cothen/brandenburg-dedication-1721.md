On 24 March 1721 Bach signed, in elaborately courteous French, the dedication of *Six Concerts avec plusieurs instruments* — "six concertos with several instruments" — to Margrave Christian Ludwig of Brandenburg, fulfilling (two years late) the request made when they met in Berlin. The dedication copy, in Bach's finest calligraphic hand, survives in Berlin; the nickname "Brandenburg Concertos" was coined by a biographer 150 years later.

Read plainly, the dedication is a **job application**. Bach, sensing Cöthen's musical budget was mortal, was advertising to a Hohenzollern prince — and the letter's almost groveling humility ("begging Your Highness not to judge their imperfection by the standards of the refined and delicate taste…") is a masterclass in the era's patronage language, worth reading in full on this card for what "working conditions" meant even at the top of the profession.

Nothing came of it. No Berlin post materialized; the Margrave's modest Capelle probably couldn't have fielded the exotic line-ups the six works demand; the score sat in his library, unperformed as far as we know, and was sold off after his death for a trivial sum. The music, of course, was not lost — Bach, ever practical, had written the concertos over his Cöthen years for his own virtuosi and kept performing materials at home.

**Why it matters.** The gap between the artifact's fate (ignored, shelved, nearly lost) and its eventual status (the most famous orchestral music of the entire Baroque) is the whole reception-history theme of this timeline in miniature — Arc II (eclipse and rediscovery) writ small.
