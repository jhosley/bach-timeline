Leopold, whatever his cooling budget, behaved handsomely at the end: he released his Kapellmeister without obstruction (a pointed contrast with Weimar's jail cell six years earlier) and let Bach keep the honorary title *Kapellmeister von Haus aus* — Kapellmeister "from home," retained at a distance. Bach valued court titles as armor in civic disputes and would collect them for the rest of his life.

A Leipzig chronicle records the arrival with municipal precision: on **22 May 1723** two carriages carrying the Bach family — Johann Sebastian, Anna Magdalena (pregnant that year with her first child), and the four children of Maria Barbara — and four wagons loaded with household goods pulled up at the Thomasschule, where the cantor's apartment was being renovated for them. Eight days later he directed his first cantata as cantor.

The Cöthen coda came six years on. Leopold died in November 1728, aged thirty-three, and in March 1729 Bach returned with Anna Magdalena and a body of Leipzig musicians to perform the funeral music (BWV 244a, *Klagt, Kinder, klagt es aller Welt*) — its music drawn largely from the **St. Matthew Passion**, then fresh from its own first performances. The text survives; the specifically Cöthen score does not; but the gesture is legible: for the prince who had loved and known music, Bach reached for the greatest music he had.

**Why it matters.** Closes the era with its themes intact — the dignity of the Kapellmeister title, the warmth of the one good patronage relationship of Bach's life, and the workshop practicality of parody (reusing and re-texting one's own music), which will be a major craft topic in the Leipzig and B-minor-Mass eras.
