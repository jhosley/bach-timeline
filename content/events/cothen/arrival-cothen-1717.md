Bach walked out of the Weimar detention rooms on the second of December, 1717, a dismissed man with a jail record — and moved with the speed of someone who had been packed for months. Within days he was a hundred miles north in Cöthen, where the court was preparing the festivities for Prince Leopold's birthday on the tenth of December, and where the account books, which had been paying their absent Kapellmeister since August, could finally record him present and earning. It is pleasant, and probably accurate, to imagine his first official music-making at Cöthen as birthday music for the twenty-three-year-old prince who had just waited out a duke's tantrum to get him.

That same December, before the year closed, Bach accepted an outside engagement that history has underlined in retrospect. Leipzig's university church, the Paulinerkirche, had just completed a great new organ — fifty-odd stops, the most ambitious instrument the city had ever commissioned — and its builder, Johann Scheibe, needed the customary formal examination before final payment. The examiner the university summoned was the new Kapellmeister of Cöthen. Organ examination was one of Bach's steadiest sideline trades, and the surviving reports from a lifetime of such inspections form a portrait of him that students of the music too easily skip: Bach the engineer. An examination meant days of crawling through the instrument's anatomy — testing the wind supply under full load, checking the voicing of individual pipes, working the action for weight and speed, hunting for leaks and rattles the builder hoped no one would find — followed by a public demonstration in which the examiner improvised at full brilliance to show the machine's range. His written verdict on Scheibe's organ survives: thorough, technical, scrupulously fair, noting defects without cruelty and merits without flattery. The builder's son, it is worth remembering for later, was a boy named Johann Adolph Scheibe, who would grow up to write [the most famous attack on Bach's music ever printed](../leipzig-2/scheibe-1737.md) — the eighteenth century was a small world, and Leipzig smaller still.

The detour matters to this timeline for two reasons beyond its foreshadowing. The first is what it reveals about how Bach understood instruments — all instruments. The man who tested wind-chests for a fee wrote for the violin, the cello, the oboe, and the human voice with the same mechanical empathy, as if he had crawled inside each of them too and knew exactly where the bellows strained. The idiomatic perfection that performers still marvel at — music that sits in the hand, under the bow, in the breath, even at its most difficult — is the examiner's knowledge turned outward. The second reason is geographic, and the map view of this timeline makes it visible at a glance: Weimar, Cöthen, and Leipzig sit within a couple of days' travel of one another, three points on the small Saxon-Thuringian triangle inside which almost the whole of Bach's life was conducted. In a single December he touched all three — released by the first, employed by the second, and leaving his first professional fingerprints on the third, the city that would eventually claim his last twenty-seven years and his grave.

No one present in the Paulinerkirche that December could have read the omen, least of all the examiner, who had every reason to believe he was done with career moves forever. He had the title, the prince, the salary, and the finest small orchestra in Germany waiting for him back in Cöthen. The golden age was about to begin.
