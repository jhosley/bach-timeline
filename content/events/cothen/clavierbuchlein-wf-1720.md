On 22 January 1720, Bach ruled staves into a small oblong book and wrote a title page: *Clavierbüchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach* — "Little keyboard book for Wilhelm Friedemann Bach," his eldest son, then nine years old.

The notebook opens with the absolute basics — an explanation of clefs, an ornament table in Bach's own hand showing exactly how to play each trill and mordent (still a primary source for Baroque ornamentation today) — and a graded sequence of pieces: little preludes, dance movements, and then, appearing here in their earliest versions, the pieces that became the **Inventions and Sinfonias** (first titled *Praeambula* and *Fantasias*) and eleven preludes that became part of **The Well-Tempered Clavier**.

This is Bach's pedagogy caught in the act, and its philosophy is visible in the sequencing: sound fundamentals, then *two-voice thinking*, then three; always real music rather than dry exercises; composition and playing taught as one skill, not two. Friedemann — by general agreement the most naturally gifted of the sons — got the concentrated version of the family method that had trained Bachs for over a century.

**Why it matters.** For a student of the Inventions and WTC, this notebook is the reminder that these towering works began as *homework for a specific nine-year-old* — and that Bach's greatest abstract structures almost always have a concrete, practical origin.
