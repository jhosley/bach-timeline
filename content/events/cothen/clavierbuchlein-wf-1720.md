On the twenty-second of January, 1720, the Kapellmeister of Cöthen ruled staves into a small oblong notebook and inscribed a title page in his best hand: *Clavierbüchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach* — a little keyboard book for Wilhelm Friedemann Bach. The intended reader was nine years old, the composer's eldest son, and the date at the top makes this one of the very few moments in Bach's whole biography where we can watch him, on a particular winter day, doing something purely domestic: sitting down to teach his boy.

What follows the title page is the most complete record we possess of how the Bach family method actually worked, and its opening moves are almost touchingly practical. First, a page explaining the clefs — the child must learn to read. Then an ornament table, written out in the father's hand: a row of trills, mordents, and turns with their exact executions notated above, a Rosetta stone so authoritative that performers of Baroque music still consult it three centuries later when they need to know what a squiggle meant to the man who wrote it. Only then does the music begin, and it begins gently — little preludes, small dances, pieces a nine-year-old's hand can close around — before the gradient starts to climb.

And climb it does, into history. As the notebook fills over the following two or three years, there appear, in early versions and under earlier names, the pieces the world now knows as the [Inventions and Sinfonias](../../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) — here still called *Praeambula* and *Fantasias* — and eleven preludes that would grow into the opening pillars of [the Well-Tempered Clavier](../../works/cothen/wtc-1.md). This is the fact that gives the little book its permanent claim on our attention: two of the foundational monuments of Western keyboard music began as homework, drafted for one specific child, revised in the traffic of actual lessons. The masterpieces were not adapted for teaching afterward. They were *born* teaching.

The pedagogy embedded in the sequence rewards a close look, because it is a philosophy disguised as a table of contents. Fundamentals first, but never dry ones — every exercise is a real piece of music, composition and technique arriving as a single substance. Two-voice thinking before three. The learner's own hand always the measure of the next step. And underneath it all, the assumption the Bach clan had run on for a century: that playing and composing were one literacy, learned together the way a child learns speaking and thinking together. Friedemann — by every account the most naturally gifted of the sons, and the one whose later life would unravel most sadly — received the concentrated version, the crown prince's education.

It is worth pausing on where and when this tenderness was happening. January 1720 sits in the golden middle of the Cöthen years: the Brandenburgs gathering toward their dedication, the solo-violin *[Sei Solo](../../works/cothen/sei-solo.md)* reaching fair copy that same year, the court's music at full flood. In the midst of the most productive instrumental period any composer has ever had, Bach's dated, deliberate act of that winter was a child's notebook. Teaching was not what he did when composing paused; it was one of the forms his composing took — a truth the [Craft essays](../../craft/craft-02-invention.md) trace all the way to the end of his life. Six months after the notebook was begun, [Maria Barbara was dead](maria-barbara-death-1720.md), and the boy the book was written for had lost his mother. The lessons, the sources suggest, went on. In that household they were not separable from love.
