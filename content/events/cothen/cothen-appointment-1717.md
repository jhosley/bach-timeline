In August 1717, Bach accepted appointment as **Kapellmeister** — director of music — to Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Cöthen, and Cöthen's account books show his salary running from that month, even though he was still stuck in Weimar. The title mattered: in the hierarchy of German musical life, Kapellmeister to a reigning prince outranked any organist or cantor post, and Bach would sign himself with the title (and cling to successor versions of it) for the rest of his life.

The move solved two problems at once. At Weimar, Bach had been passed over for exactly this title when the old Kapellmeister died and the job went to the late master's mediocre son. And in Leopold he had something rarer than a promotion: an employer who was a genuine, trained musician assembling one of the best small orchestras in Germany. The salary — around 400 thalers, roughly double Weimar — made him the second-highest-paid official at the Cöthen court.

There was one complication: the Duke of Weimar had not agreed to let him go. What happened when Bach pressed the point is the next event on this timeline.

**Why it matters.** Job titles drove Bach's output more than inspiration did. Organist posts (Arnstadt, Mühlhausen, Weimar) meant organ music and cantatas; a Calvinist court Kapellmeister post meant instrumental music; the Leipzig cantorate meant the great sacred cycles. Watch the title change and you can predict the music.
