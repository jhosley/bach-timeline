The most consequential hiring decision in the history of music is preserved, meeting by meeting, in the minute-books of the Leipzig town council — and the documents are merciless to posterity's sense of how these things ought to have gone. No committee has ever been so thoroughly embarrassed by its own paper trail; none has ever had less reason to expect the scrutiny.

The vacancy opened in June 1722 with the death of Johann Kuhnau, and the job was, by any German measure, enormous: Cantor of the Thomasschule and director of music for the city of Leipzig — the musical governance of four churches, a famous boarding school of choristers, and the civic ceremonies of Saxony's commercial capital, a post whose previous holders stretched back through two centuries of distinguished names. The council knew exactly whom it wanted. **Georg Philipp Telemann** was the most famous composer in Germany, a former Leipzig student who had founded the city's beloved Collegium Musicum two decades earlier; the councilmen offered him the job with open delight, and Telemann accepted, traveled home to Hamburg, extracted a handsome raise from his employers there on the strength of the offer, and declined. It was the smoothest career play of the era, executed by its smoothest careerist. The council, regrouping, turned to **Christoph Graupner** — Kapellmeister of Darmstadt, another fashionable name — who auditioned successfully and then discovered that his prince simply refused to release him, sweetening the refusal with a raise of his own. Leipzig had now improved the salaries of two of its rivals' music directors.

Only then, in the winnowed third round, did the field settle on the remaining candidates, among them the Kapellmeister of Cöthen. On the seventh of February, 1723 — Quinquagesima Sunday, the last Sunday before Lent silenced concerted music — Bach stood before the Leipzig congregations and auditioned with two cantatas composed for the occasion, *Jesus nahm zu sich die Zwölfe* and *Du wahrer Gott und Davids Sohn*, music of a seriousness that ought to have ended all deliberation. It did not. The council deliberated for two more months, and when it finally voted for Bach in April, Councilman Abraham Platz summarized the body's feelings in a sentence that has followed Leipzig ever since: since the best men could not be obtained, *"mediocre ones would have to be accepted."* He meant it administratively — Bach lacked the university degree the post traditionally preferred, and his fame was an organist's, not a cosmopolitan composer's — but the sentence has escaped its context forever, and deserves to. It is the most precisely wrong assessment in the history of institutional judgment, and it was entered into the record about the man who would write the St. Matthew Passion for that council's churches within four years.

Bach had his own reservations, and confessed them later with disarming honesty in the Erdmann letter: *"It seemed strange to me at first that I should become a cantor after having been a Kapellmeister"* — a genuine step down the era's ladder of rank, from a prince's officer to a town's schoolmaster, and his friends said so. What tipped him is a study in mixed motives of the most recognizable kind: [Cöthen's music was visibly dimming](leopold-marriage-1721.md); Leipzig had a university for his sons, a matter he cared about intensely, having been denied one himself; and the cantorate, whatever its rank, commanded the largest stage for sacred music in Protestant Germany — the arena for [the "well-regulated church music" he had declared as his life's purpose fifteen years before](../muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md). He signed the council's pledge in May. The wagons rolled [three weeks later](farewell-cothen-1723.md).

Every student should sit with this episode until its lesson is permanent. Institutions judge in real time, with real information, and they reach for the familiar name, the credentialed résumé, the smooth fit; the Leipzig council did nothing unusual and everything typical. Genius arrived third on their list, was hired with a shrug, and spent twenty-seven years being managed as a difficult employee. The minute-books survive to keep every committee that ever ranked candidates appropriately humble — and to remind the rest of us that the verdicts of one's own era, favorable or dismissive, are drafts that history feels free to rewrite entirely.
