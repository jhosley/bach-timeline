In November 1718, in the Bachs' second year at Cöthen, [Maria Barbara](../../people/maria-barbara-bach.md) gave birth to her seventh child, a boy. The parish register records his baptism and, in its list of godparents, a small sensation: at the head stood His Serene Highness Leopold, Prince of Anhalt-Cöthen, together with other members of the ruling house. The child was christened Leopold Augustus, and the name was the least of the honor. For a reigning prince to stand at the font for his Kapellmeister's son was a gesture with precise social weight in a world that measured such things to the gram — a public declaration that this musician was not merely staff but something closer to a friend of the house. Nothing in Bach's Weimar years, and nothing in his Leipzig decades to come, would match it.

Leopold Augustus lived ten months. He was buried in September 1719, the register as laconic in death as it had been ceremonious in baptism, and he became the fourth of Maria Barbara's seven children to die in infancy or early childhood. His mother had less than a year to live herself, though no one could know it; [her own death, sudden and unwitnessed by her traveling husband](maria-barbara-death-1720.md), waits eight months down this timeline.

It falls to this card to state, once and plainly, the demographic ground on which Bach's entire family life was built, because the family track will return to it again and again and the numbers deserve to be faced rather than glimpsed. Johann Sebastian Bach fathered twenty children across two marriages. Ten of them died before reaching adulthood. That ratio — one in two — was not a Bach family tragedy in the singular; it was the ordinary arithmetic of early eighteenth-century Germany, where baptism and burial registers were kept in the same book and every parent filled both pages. There was scarcely a period of Bach's adult life without a recent small grave in it. His parents had buried children; his brothers and sisters did; his colleagues, his princes, and every family in every pew he ever played to did the same.

Students of the music reach instinctively for this fact when they encounter the bottomless consolation of the funeral works — the *Actus tragicus* with its child's-voice serenity, "Ich habe genug" with its lullaby for one's own death, the cradle-song tenderness that keeps surfacing in music about dying. The instinct is human and probably not wrong, but this timeline flags it honestly: no document connects any particular loss to any particular note, and Bach left no diaries, no letters of grief, nothing but registers and music. What can be said with documentary confidence is subtler and perhaps weightier. The Lutheran art Bach practiced — the chorales, the cantata texts, the whole theology of death as homecoming rather than horror — was not an abstract inheritance he decorated. It was a working technology of consolation, built by and for people who buried half their children, and Bach was both its greatest craftsman and one of its steadiest customers. When his music speaks to modern grief with such uncanny directness, it is because it was engineered under heavier loads than most of us will ever carry.

The prince who had stood godfather would himself be dead within a decade, at thirty-three, and Bach would return to Cöthen one last time to bury him with music borrowed from the [St. Matthew Passion](../../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md). The little boy who briefly linked their houses lies in the Cöthen registers between a baptism and a burial, ten months apart — the timeline's smallest entry, and one of its heaviest.
