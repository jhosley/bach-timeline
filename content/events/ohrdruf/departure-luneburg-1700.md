The Lyceum register closes Bach's Ohrdruf years with three Latin words: he departed ***ob defectum hospitiorum*** — "for lack of free places at table." His brother's family had grown; the subsidized board that made schooling possible for a penniless orphan had run out; and at fifteen, Sebastian needed an institution that would feed him for singing.

One was found, apparently through a Lyceum connection: the **Michaelisschule at Lüneburg**, whose *Mettenchor* (matins choir) offered poor boys with good voices free schooling, board, and small fees. The catch was geography — Lüneburg lay two hundred miles north, beyond the clan's entire Thuringian world, further from home than any musical Bach had ever settled. In March 1700 Sebastian set out with his schoolfellow **Georg [Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md)**, two teenagers walking north through early spring.

Mark Erdmann's name: the friendship formed on that road lasted. Thirty years later it was to Erdmann — by then a diplomat in Danzig — that Bach wrote the frankest letter of his life, the 1730 survey of his career quoted throughout this timeline ([the *amusa* passage](../cothen/leopold-marriage-1721.md), the "it seemed strange to become a cantor" confession). The road out of Ohrdruf runs directly to our best window into Bach's mind.

**Why it matters.** Poverty, not vocation, chose Bach's next school — and thereby chose everything: Lüneburg put him inside the north-German organ world (Böhm, Reincken, and the road to [Buxtehude](../../people/dieterich-buxtehude.md)) that became one-third of his musical formation. The timeline's recurring lesson: watch the material conditions; the destiny follows them.
