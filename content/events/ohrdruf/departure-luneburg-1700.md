The Lyceum register closes Bach's Ohrdruf years with three Latin words, entered in March 1700 beside the name of its best student: he departed *ob defectum hospitiorum* — "for lack of free places at table." Not for ambition, not for a post, not for the pull of some distant teacher. For lack of a free lunch. His brother Johann Christoph's family had kept growing; the subsidized board that made schooling possible for a penniless orphan had run out; and at fifteen, Sebastian needed an institution that would feed him in exchange for the one asset he possessed — a good voice.

One was found, apparently through a Lyceum connection: the Michaelisschule at Lüneburg, whose *Mettenchor*, the matins choir, offered poor boys with good voices free schooling, board, and small fees. The arrangement was made for exactly his case, and the catch was purely geographic. Lüneburg lay two hundred miles north — beyond Thuringia, beyond the entire home territory of the Bach clan, farther from home than any musical Bach had ever settled in the family's recorded history. The clan that had staffed the towns of central Germany for a century simply did not go that far north; its network of cousins and recommendations thinned to nothing on that road. In March 1700 Sebastian walked it anyway, setting out with his schoolfellow Georg [Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md), two teenagers heading north through early spring on foot.

Mark Erdmann's name, because the friendship formed on that road turns out to be one of the timeline's quiet load-bearing beams. Thirty years later, Erdmann had become a diplomat in Danzig, and it was to him — the old walking companion, safely distant from every Saxon and Thuringian ear — that Bach wrote the frankest letter of his life: the 1730 survey of his whole career, quoted throughout this timeline, with [the *amusa* passage](../cothen/leopold-marriage-1721.md) on the souring of Cöthen and the astonishing confession that "it seemed strange to become a cantor." Nearly everything candid we know about how Bach judged his own path passes through that single letter, and the letter exists because of this walk. The road out of Ohrdruf runs directly to our best window into Bach's mind.

It also runs, though no one could have known it, into the third great tributary of his formation. Ohrdruf had given him the Pachelbel method through his brother; the clan had given him central Germany's craft traditions from birth. What a poor boy's scholarship now added, by pure economic accident, was the north: Lüneburg placed him inside the north-German organ world — Böhm in the town itself, Reincken within reach in Hamburg, and, further down the same road, the path that would one day lead to [Buxtehude](../../people/dieterich-buxtehude.md) in Lübeck. A third of the mature Bach's musical inheritance came from a region no Bach had ever worked, and he was sent there not by vocation but by the arithmetic of his brother's dinner table.

That is the reading this card asks you to keep. It is tempting to narrate young Bach's life as destiny unfolding — the genius drawn ineluctably toward the teachers he needed. The register's dry Latin says otherwise, and the timeline sides with the register: poverty chose the school, and the school chose the future. Watch the material conditions, here and throughout; the destiny follows them. A grown family, an exhausted subsidy, a scholarship for boys who could sing — and from those three mundane facts, the northern fire in the organ works, the frankest letter of 1730, and the first Bach in history to leave Thuringia behind. He was fifteen, and he went on foot.
