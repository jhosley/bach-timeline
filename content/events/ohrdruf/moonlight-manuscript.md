Sometime around 1698, in his brother's house in Ohrdruf, a boy of about thirteen began stealing music. The story, as the Obituary of 1754 tells it, runs like this: Johann Christoph Bach — Sebastian's eldest brother, guardian, and keyboard teacher — owned a manuscript volume of pieces by the most celebrated masters of the age, Froberger, Kerll, Pachelbel. Sebastian begged to study it and was refused. The book lived in a cabinet with a latticed front, and the boy's hands were small enough to reach through the grille, roll the volume out, and copy it — by moonlight, having no candle — returning it before morning. Six months of such nights completed the copy. Then Johann Christoph discovered it and confiscated it, and Sebastian recovered his stolen labor only after his brother's death. It is the most famous story of Bach's childhood, and this card wears the flag it deserves: *tradition* — unverifiable, and irresistible.

Honesty about the provenance first. The tale is family memory, published fifty-six years after the events it describes, in an Obituary written partly by Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach — who could only have had it from one source, his father. Which means the moonlight manuscript is very likely Bach's own story about himself, told to his children and polished by decades of retelling. The skeptic's objections line up readily: the moonlight is suspiciously literary; six months of copying-bright moons defies astronomy; and the cruelty of the confiscation may well have grown in the telling. No independent document confirms a word of it.

And yet biographers keep the story, and not from sentimentality: its shape is corroborated by everything documentable about the grown man. He learned by copying all his life — the Lüneburg tablatures, the Weimar Vivaldi transcriptions, fifty years of arranging and absorbing other composers' work one written note at a time. His appetite demonstrably outran anything any curriculum could satisfy, and his indifference to obstacles was total; here, as ever, he responded by teaching himself. John Eliot [Gardiner](../../people/john-eliot-gardiner.md), reading the tale alongside the documents, adds a darker corroboration — the flash of resentment at authority that flickers through it never left Bach either, as the consistory minutes and council files of his later cities attest.

The household setting sharpens the story rather than softening it. Johann Christoph was no fairy-tale villain: fourteen years older, organist of St. Michael's, recently married and raising a growing family on a thin salary, with two orphaned brothers to feed besides. He was also a serious teacher — three years a student of Johann Pachelbel in Erfurt, the most celebrated keyboard teacher in central Germany — and he passed the Pachelbel method to Sebastian directly: fingering, chorale technique, the composing-by-copying discipline of the German organ tradition. The strange power of the anecdote is that the man who locked the cabinet was the same man supplying, by day, the finest keyboard education available in Thuringia. The refusal reads as a teacher's judgment — too advanced, not yet — and the theft as a pupil's verdict that the judgment was wrong. Even the skeptics concede what the story was preserved to say: the boy's hunger had outrun what his teachers were willing to feed him, and he took the rest for himself.

So keep the two lessons separate, as this timeline tries to do throughout. As fact, the moonlight manuscript is unverifiable and will remain so; the card's flag is not negotiable. As self-portrait, it is the single best story we have about Bach's own idea of how music is learned: with the hands, at night, against permission, one copied note at a time. That idea is the one part of the legend the documents confirm again and again — copying as analysis, the working method of an entire career — and it is why the Craft section's pedagogy essay opens exactly here, at a latticed cabinet in Ohrdruf, with a book going back into its place before dawn.
