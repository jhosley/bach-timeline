The story, as the Obituary tells it: Johann Christoph owned a manuscript volume of pieces by the most celebrated keyboard masters — Froberger, Kerll, Pachelbel — which Sebastian begged to study and was refused. The book lived in a cabinet with a latticed front. The boy's hands were small enough to reach through the grille, roll the book out, and copy it — by moonlight, having no candle — returning it before morning. Six months of nights completed the copy; the brother discovered it and **confiscated it**; Sebastian recovered it only after Johann Christoph's death.

**Provenance, honestly.** This is *tradition* — family memory published fifty-six years later in the Obituary (written partly by Emanuel Bach, who could only have had it from his father: which means it is likely *Bach's own story about himself*). The moonlight is suspiciously literary; six months of full moons defies astronomy; and the confiscation's cruelty may be dramatized. Biographers keep the story anyway, because its *shape* is corroborated by everything documentable about the man: the lifelong learning-by-copying (the Lüneburg tablatures, the Vivaldi transcriptions), the appetite no curriculum could satisfy, the total indifference to obstacles, and — Gardiner notes — the flash of resentment at authority that never left him.

**Why it matters.** Keep the two lessons separate. As *fact*: unverifiable. As *self-portrait*: the single best story we have about Bach's idea of how music is learned — with the hands, at night, against permission, one copied note at a time. The Craft section's pedagogy essay opens here.
