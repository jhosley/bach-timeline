The Ohrdruf Lyceum kept records, and they are the one bright documentary lamp in these dim years: Sebastian ranked fourth in his class within months of arriving as a grieving ten-year-old, and thereafter stood first or second — while consistently among the **youngest boys in each class**, promoted faster than the norm. The curriculum was serious: Latin (Cicero, rhetoric), Greek New Testament, Lutheran theology via Leonhard Hutter's dense compendium, arithmetic, history.

Two takeaways deserve flagging. First, Bach was not a musical savant with a neglected mind; he was, by the only school records we possess, an **excellent general student** — which matters when, decades later, the Leipzig cantorate requires him to teach Latin and he negotiates with the council as a man of learning among the university-trained. Second, note the theology: the rhetorical training (inventio, dispositio, elaboratio — finding, arranging, and developing an argument) is the very vocabulary Bach's circle later used to describe his composing, and the title of the [Inventions](../../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) is borrowed straight from it. He was taught to build arguments before he was taught to build fugues, and the fugues show it.

He also earned money throughout as a chorister — the school choir sang for fees and alms — a working student from age ten to the end of his education.

**Why it matters.** The learned counterpoint of the later eras ("the learned musician," Wolff titles the whole biography) starts in a provincial classroom's Latin drills. When the Craft essays describe a fugue as an *argument*, that's not metaphor imposed from outside; it's the education talking.
