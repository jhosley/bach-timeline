The choir Bach sang in drew on one of the richest church-music libraries in Protestant Germany: surviving inventories list over a **thousand works by some 175 composers**, reaching back through the seventeenth century's masters — the tradition of Schütz, Schein, and the Lutheran motet — and forward to the moderns of the 1690s.

For most choristers the library was simply the week's folder of parts. For a boy who learned by copying, it was a panorama: two hundred years of the German sacred tradition, organized, performable, and *in his hands* week after week. Scholars presume — the performing evidence allows no more — that Bach's deep fluency in the older motet style, the *stile antico* that resurfaces so strikingly in his late music (the B minor Mass's Credo opening; the motets), was seeded here, the way a great novelist's style is seeded by adolescent reading.

Pair this card with its keyboard twin in Ohrdruf and the pattern of Bach's education locks into place: **repertoire was the curriculum**. No conservatory, no composition treatise, no counterpoint course — the boy learned what music could be by singing, playing, and copying an enormous amount of it, then reverse-engineering the machinery. The Craft essays return to this repeatedly, because it remains the most Bach-like way to study Bach: analysis as intimate acquaintance, scores in the hands.

**Why it matters.** When the timeline reaches the Leipzig motets and the late contrapuntal summa, their "archaic" mastery will have a documented address: a teenager's choir loft, two hundred miles from home, in the largest music library he would ever sing from.
