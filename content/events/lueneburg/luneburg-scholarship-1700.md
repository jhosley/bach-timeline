In the spring of 1700 the account books of St. Michael's in Lüneburg — the wealthy convent-church whose school, the Michaelisschule, it maintained — recorded two new names among the recipients of the Mettenchor allowances: [Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md) and Bach, boys newly arrived from Thuringia. The entry is as dry as ledger entries always are, but it preserves the terms of an adolescence. The Mettenchor was the matins choir — roughly fifteen boys who sang the early service and the church's liturgy — and in exchange the school provided free tuition, free board, and small cash fees. Admission required two qualifications, and the institution was unembarrassed about naming either: a good voice, and poverty. This was the standard machine by which Lutheran Germany converted musical talent into education for boys who had neither family money nor prospects, and in April 1700 it took in a fifteen-year-old named Johann Sebastian Bach, who possessed both qualifications in abundance.

The scholarship's premise collapsed almost immediately. Bach had come north as a soprano, and within months of his arrival the voice broke — the Obituary preserves the strange detail that for a short while he spoke and sang in octaves at once, and then the treble was gone entirely. By the contractual logic of the arrangement, that should have ended his schooling: the church had paid for a voice, and the voice no longer existed. Instead, the school kept him. He was simply too useful — as a violinist, as a keyboard accompanist, possibly as a prefect drilling the younger boys — and his usefulness kept buying him education, which is the pattern of his entire youth compressed into a single quiet administrative decision. The lesson sitting just under the surface of the account books is one the boy cannot have missed: the voice was rented, and nature could repossess it without notice. Instrumental skill was capital, and it was his.

It is worth pausing over what kind of machine had caught him, because he would spend most of his life inside it. The Mettenchor at Lüneburg ran on the same design as the boarding choir of Leipzig's Thomasschule — the institution whose choristers Bach would one day command as cantor, and whose working conditions he would contest with the town council for years. The symmetry deserves to be felt at full weight: he spent his adolescence inside the very institution he would spend his last twenty-seven years directing. When he argues with Leipzig about choir conditions in 1730, he is not theorizing about the lives of poor scholarship singers from the comfortable distance of a director's desk; he is describing a life he lived, matins by matins, meal by meal. Few of his superiors could say the same, and none of them ever seemed to grasp how precisely he knew what he was talking about.

That is the deeper reading this card asks for, and it belongs to the positions track at its clearest. Bach's entire secondary education was a job. His meals were contingent on performance; his employment had been continuous since the age of ten; school and service, learning and livelihood, were for him a single undifferentiated thing. Later cards in this timeline will show him bargaining ferociously — over salary, over fees, over titles — with a tenacity that has struck some admirers as unbecoming in a genius, as though the composer of the Passions ought to have been above itemizing his allowances. Remember, when those cards arrive, where the story starts: with a poor boy singing the early service for his bread, in a town far from home, holding a scholarship whose stated premise had already failed. Music and livelihood were fused for him from childhood, and he never had the luxury of learning them separately. The fusion was not a distortion of his artistic character — it was the ground the character grew in, and Lüneburg is where the account books first show it plainly.
