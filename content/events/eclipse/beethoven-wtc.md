Beethoven's first appearance in print — Cramer's *Magazin der Musik*, 1783 — introduces a twelve-year-old in Bonn who "plays chiefly ***The [Well-Tempered Clavier](../../works/cothen/wtc-1.md)* of Sebastian Bach**, which Herr Neefe put into his hands," calling the collection what it was to insiders: a *non plus ultra*. Note what that notice assumes: the book was **unpublished** — Beethoven learned it, as everyone did, [from manuscript copies](../../eras/eclipse.md) — yet a provincial teacher owned one, assigned it to a prodigy, and a journalist expected readers to recognize the title. That is the eclipse in one sentence: invisible to the public, canonical to the trade.

Bach never left him. In Vienna he played the 48 at [van Swieten's](van-swieten-salon.md) (the Baron demanding fugues "as an evening blessing"); the sketchbooks quote and wrestle with Bach across the decades; and the late works — the *Hammerklavier* fugue, the Missa Solemnis, the Diabelli Variations ([answering the Goldbergs](../../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md)), the Grosse Fuge — take up late-Bach's own project: [the fusion of strict counterpoint with the modern style](../../works/leipzig/wtc-2.md), carried into the next century. The famous remark attributed to him — ***"nicht Bach, sondern Meer sollte er heißen"*** ("not Brook but Sea should he be called," punning on *Bach* = brook) — is imperfectly sourced (*tradition* flag) and perfectly representative: for Beethoven's generation of professionals, Bach was already the unbounded thing.

He also subscribed toward the first collected editions as they began around 1800, and pressed publishers for more — the professional relay ([students → salon → prodigy](van-swieten-salon.md)) now turning into commerce, the eclipse's last decade.

**Why it matters.** Completes Arc II's underground genealogy: **Mozart and Beethoven were both formed by Bach before the public revival existed** — so when [1829 arrives](../revival/mendelssohn-revival-1829.md), the "rediscovery" ratifies what the art's practitioners had known continuously. Reception history's recurring shape, worth carrying into Arc III: the professionals lead, the public follows, the legend then reverses the order.
