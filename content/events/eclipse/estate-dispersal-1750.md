The document survives, and its ordinariness is the tragedy. The *Specificatio* of Johann Sebastian Bach's estate — the notary's inventory drawn up in the months after his death — records the household of a prosperous Leipzig burgher, item by item: shares in a mining concern, the family silver, eight harpsichords and ten other instruments, an eighty-volume theological library. And then, listed almost in passing among the household goods, the manuscripts: the largest body of unpublished masterworks any composer had ever left behind, to be divided among nine surviving heirs under ordinary inheritance law, like the chairs. No malice operated here, and no plan either — which is precisely the point. In 1750 there existed no institution whose business it was to receive a cantor's life work whole, and so Saxon probate did what probate does. It divided.

The logic of that division, far more than any verdict of taste, decided what the world would ever be permitted to hear. Carl Philipp Emanuel — established at Frederick's court in Berlin, careful by temperament, pious toward his father's memory — took a great share, including [the St. Matthew Passion](../../works/leipzig/st-matthew-passion.md) and [the B minor Mass](../../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md). He preserved his portion, catalogued it, and lived long enough to hand it toward institutions that would outlast him. Wilhelm Friedemann — the adored eldest, [the boy for whom the Clavierbüchlein had been made in Cöthen](../cothen/clavierbuchlein-wf-1720.md), and by mid-century already entering the long professional and personal unraveling that would fill the rest of his life — received, among other things, most of the chorale-cantata scores. Across three decades of deepening poverty he sold them off piecemeal, to whoever would pay, and most of what passed through his hands is simply gone. And [Anna Magdalena](../../people/anna-magdalena-bach.md), the widow, gave her share — the performing parts of the chorale cantatas, the working materials of [the weekly machine](../../events/leipzig-1/cantata-machine.md) her husband had driven through the 1720s — to the Thomasschule. Hers was the one tributary that flowed into an institution rather than a market, and that is exactly why the chorale-cantata cycle survives better than its siblings. Her own reward was a decade of poverty and, in 1760, an alms-grave.

Three heirs, three fates for the paper: the curator, the seller, the donor. Hold them side by side and you have the entire mechanism of the eclipse in miniature — not a bonfire, not a purge, just the differing gravities of institution and market acting on loose sheets of manuscript paper for eighty years.

The arithmetic scholars have assembled from the wreckage is sober and terrible. Perhaps two-fifths of the cantatas are lost. At least two Passions. Untold instrumental works. And the manner of the loss matters as much as the scale: nothing burned in a dramatic fire, nothing was condemned or suppressed. The music *dissolved* — sheet by sheet, through auction rooms and grocers' wrapping paper — let go, one transaction at a time, by a century that had concluded it was not worth keeping. A masterwork's survival came down to which heir's cupboard it happened to occupy in November 1750.

This is why the estate division, and not any critic's verdict, is the material foundation of everything that follows on this dimmed stretch of the timeline. The eclipse was never only a failure of taste; it was, first and most fatally, a failure of *custody*. What taste forgot, custody could have kept — as the Thomasschule kept what Anna Magdalena gave it, as Emanuel kept the Passions. And every later scene in this arc is best read as someone, somewhere, rebuilding custody by hand: [Mozart in Leipzig with the motet parts spread around him](mozart-motet-1789.md), [Mendelssohn in Berlin with a copyist's score of a forgotten Passion](../revival/mendelssohn-revival-1829.md), [the Gesellschaft's forty-six volumes](../revival/bach-gesellschaft-1850.md) — custody rebuilt, at last, at the scale of a nation. The inventory that scattered the manuscripts is also where the long work of gathering them begins.
