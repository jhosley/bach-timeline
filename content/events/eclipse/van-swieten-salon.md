Every Sunday at noon, in Vienna in 1782, a small company gathered at the rooms of Baron Gottfried van Swieten to play music that fashion had buried — and the eclipse, for one hour a week, quietly reversed itself. Van Swieten was the imperial librarian, a former ambassador to Berlin, and the eighteenth century's most consequential musical antiquarian. His Berlin posting had placed him inside the one city where Sebastian Bach's memory was still professionally warm — the circle of the old students, the library of Princess Anna Amalia — and he had spent those years collecting what that circle could supply: manuscripts of Bach and Handel, carried back to Vienna in a diplomat's luggage. Home again, he made a ritual of them. At his Sunday salons nothing was played but this "ancient music," and in 1782 the newest arrival in town became a regular.

We know exactly what happened next, because Mozart wrote it down. "I go every Sunday at twelve to Baron van Swieten's," he reported that April, "— and nothing is played there but Handel and Bach. I am just making a collection of Bach fugues..." The collecting was not idle. He arranged several fugues from the Well-Tempered Clavier for string ensemble — K. 405 survives — and began writing fugues of his own in response, the modern style's most fluent master suddenly apprenticing himself, on his day of rest, to a dead cantor's counterpoint. The infection spread through the household: his wife Constanze, he reports with evident wonder, "fell quite in love with them," and took to scolding him whenever he left a fugue unfinished. It is one of the era's most domestic images of reception history at work — the century's supreme melodist being held to his counterpoint exercises by his bride.

What went in on those Sundays went in deep. Scholars trace the contrapuntal saturation of Mozart's late style — the five-theme finale of the *Jupiter* Symphony, the chorale-singing armed men of *The Magic Flute*, the fugues of the Requiem — substantially to this Sunday education. The polyphony did not sit in his music like a quotation; it dissolved into the bloodstream, and the last decade of the classical style's development carries Bach in solution.

Step back, and van Swieten's salon is the eclipse era's relay station made visible — every link in the chain of private custody present in one room. Manuscripts preserved by Bach's own students; carried out of Berlin by a diplomat-collector with antiquarian tastes; handed, across a music stand on a Sunday afternoon, to the greatest living master of the style that had supposedly superseded them. This is what the public forgetting of Bach actually looked like from inside the profession: not forgetting at all, but a narrowing of the channel — the music withdrawing from churches and concert rooms into libraries, salons, and the working memories of composers, where it waited. Public taste would not carry Bach; private custody did. And the salon was not a terminus but a junction: a decade later Beethoven arrived in the same circle, the Well-Tempered Clavier already in his fingers [from his Bonn training](beethoven-wtc.md), and the relay ran on into the next century.

This card, then, is the standing counter-narrative to the phrase "Bach was forgotten." At the exact bottom of the public eclipse — the years when the name meant the sons and the father was a portrait in the hall — his hardest, least fashionable music was actively reshaping the composers who mattered most, invisibly, on Sundays, at noon. Nothing about it was accidental except the timing; everything about it was fragile except the result. And when [Leipzig ambushed Mozart with a motet seven years later](mozart-motet-1789.md) — when the counterpoint stood up and sang at him in a stone church — the astonishment was real, but the ground had been prepared here, one Sunday at a time, in a baron's rooms in Vienna. The eclipse's great secret was never that the light had gone out. It was that the light had gone indoors.
