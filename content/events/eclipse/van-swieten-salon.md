**Baron Gottfried van Swieten** — imperial librarian in Vienna, former ambassador to Berlin, and the eighteenth century's most consequential musical antiquarian — spent his Berlin years collecting what that city's Bach-trained circle (the old students, Princess Anna Amalia's library) could supply: manuscripts of Sebastian Bach and Handel. Back in Vienna, he held Sunday salons where this "ancient music" was played — and in 1782 the newest arrival in town became a regular.

**Mozart's own letters** report what happened: "I go every Sunday at twelve to Baron van Swieten's — and nothing is played there but Handel and Bach. I am just making a collection of Bach fugues..." He arranged several WTC fugues for string ensemble (K. 405 survives); he wrote fugues of his own in response; and his wife Constanze, he reports with some wonder, "fell quite in love with them" and scolded him whenever he left a fugue unfinished. The counterpoint went in deep: scholars trace the contrapuntal saturation of Mozart's late style — the *Jupiter* Symphony's five-theme finale, the *Magic Flute*'s chorale-singing armed men, the Requiem's fugues — substantially to this Sunday education.

Van Swieten's salon is the eclipse-era's **relay station made visible**: manuscripts preserved by Bach's students, carried by a diplomat-collector, handed to the classical style's greatest master — private custody doing what public taste wouldn't. (Beethoven's arrival in the same circle a decade later, WTC already in his fingers [from his Bonn training](beethoven-wtc.md), completes the relay.)

**Why it matters.** The counter-narrative to "Bach was forgotten": at the exact bottom of the public eclipse, his hardest music was reshaping the composers who mattered most — invisibly, on Sundays, at noon. When [Leipzig ambushed Mozart with a motet seven years later](mozart-motet-1789.md), the ground had been prepared in this room.
