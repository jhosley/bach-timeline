In October 1705, Bach requested four weeks' leave, installed a cousin at his organ, and set out — **on foot, roughly 250 miles north** — for Lübeck, to hear **Dieterich [Buxtehude](../../people/dieterich-buxtehude.md)** at the Marienkirche: the greatest living master of the northern organ school, then nearly seventy. The timing was deliberate: Advent brought Buxtehude's famous **Abendmusiken** — "evening musics," grand public concerts of sacred music with orchestra, financed by the business community — the nearest thing in Germany to a civic concert institution, and in 1705 mounting extraordinary memorial and accession programs (Emperor Leopold I had died) of exceptional splendor.

Bach stayed **four months**, through Christmas and the great performances, returning around February 1706 to face [the consistory](consistory-rebukes-1706.md). Asked to explain the absence, he offered the least apologetic justification in the annals of employment: he had gone *"in order to comprehend one thing and another about his art"* (the Obituary's phrasing of what the minutes paraphrase). No regret is recorded.

A tantalizing sidelight: Buxtehude was seeking a successor, and the post carried a customary condition — marriage to the incumbent's daughter. Handel and Mattheson had visited in 1703 and famously declined; scholars suspect the option was at least visible to Bach too. He walked home instead. *(Inference, flagged as such — the sources are silent on any offer.)*

**Why it matters.** The purest expression of the education track's whole pattern — when Bach wanted knowledge, he walked to the source and overstayed — and the most consequential: the northern *stylus phantasticus* at full Buxtehude voltage flows straight into the great early organ works, and the Abendmusik model of large-scale sacred music for a whole town arguably seeded the ambition that became the Passions. Four weeks of permission, four months of formation; posterity sides with the truant.
