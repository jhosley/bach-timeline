In June 1703 the town of Arnstadt needed its newly finished organ examined. The Neue Kirche's instrument had just come from the workshop of the builder Wender, and before a town paid for such a machine it engaged an expert to test it — a task belonging, by long custom, to senior men with decades of experience in wind pressure, voicing, and the thousand ways a builder might cut corners. Arnstadt sent for an eighteen-year-old. That fact alone, before a note was played, says how far the young Bach's reputation had already traveled: through the clan network above all, for this was the deepest Bach territory in Germany — Bachs had worked the town's music for a century — and the family's word on a promising son evidently carried. He came, he tested the machine, and then he played it for the town.

What happened next has happened around Bach's playing in every era of his life: the examination became an audition. The town heard him, and the town wanted him. Six weeks later, on 9 August 1703, the consistory issued his appointment as organist of the Neue Kirche, and the terms of the certificate — which survives — tell the story more eloquently than any anecdote. His combined salary and allowances came to some eighty-plus florins, more than either of the town's two senior organists earned, for duties officially confined to organ service at a handful of weekly services. Six months earlier he had been on a court's books as a lackey; now, at eighteen, he was the best-paid organist in a proud town, hired over the heads of his seniors on nothing but demonstrated ability.

The certificate itself repays reading, because the consistory wrote it like a sermon: he is charged to be true, industrious and faithful, to appear promptly, to take good care of the organ. Each line is boilerplate; each line is also, in retrospect, a small dramatic irony, since the coming cards will show him testing every one of these charges in turn — the punctuality, the faithfulness, the industriousness as Arnstadt defined it. The town thought it was hiring a servant of the liturgy. It had in fact acquired a force of nature on a light leash, and the leash's lightness is the appointment's most consequential feature.

For consider what the post actually amounted to. An organist with a brand-new instrument fresh from the builder's hands, generous pay, minimal duties, and no superior musician for miles: whatever the certificate said, Arnstadt was functionally a composition studio with a salary attached. These become Bach's studio years, the first period of his life in which time, instrument, and independence align. The earliest organ masterpieces take shape here — the toccatas, the chorale partitas in the manner of his Lüneburg mentor Böhm, the first preludes that sound fully like him and no one else. A caution the timeline owes its readers: dating individual early works is notoriously soft, and the catalog cards will flag each case rather than pretend to precision the sources cannot support. But the broad picture is secure. The leap in the music's ambition and finish belongs to these years, and the light duty load is why.

Read as a positions-track document, the appointment is the ladder's second rung and its steepest: lackey to best-paid organist in six months, on raw ability, with the terms bent in his favor. But read it also for what it seeded. A town that pays a teenager more than his seniors has created resentments as surely as it has created opportunity — among the passed-over, among the students he would soon be expected to lead, among every burgher who felt the arrangement's presumption. Both facts, the meteoric rise and the quiet grudges accumulating around it, are load-bearing context for what follows: the sword drawn in the market square, the hearings, the strange harmonies, the long unraveling of a job that had begun as a triumph. Arnstadt gave Bach his terms. The coming cards show what he did with them, and what it cost.
