In June 1708 Bach traveled to Weimar, played for Duke Wilhelm Ernst, and came away with an offer that settled the matter: court organist and chamber musician, at roughly double his entire Mühlhausen package. He had held the St. Blasius post barely a year. The letter of resignation he addressed to the Mühlhausen council on 25 June 1708 survives complete, and it is the closest thing to a personal manifesto Bach ever wrote — a job resignation that biographers have treated, ever since, as the key to the man.

The letter's surface is impeccable civil-service courtesy. He thanks the town. He names no names. But he itemizes, in careful abstractions, what had gone wrong: he had sought to advance church music "without vexation," and had met "hindrance"; and conditions, he observes, gave no sign that a better state of affairs was coming. Behind the diplomatic fog stands the situation the era essay describes plainly — Mühlhausen's clergy split between orthodox and Pietist tendencies, the Pietists suspicious of elaborate art-music in worship as worldly display, and Bach's own pastor leaning Pietist while the ambitious music-lover among the clergy served the other church. A composer of his aims was structurally in the wrong pulpit's parish, and no amount of talent could compose his way out of an ecclesiastical org chart.

Then comes the sentence around which everything turns. He had always kept in view, he writes, one *Endzweck* — a final purpose — namely *"a well-regulated church music to the glory of God"* (*eine regulirte Kirchenmusik zu Gottes Ehren*); and where he could not pursue it, he must go where he could. Twenty-three years old, and he had already formulated his life in a single clause.

It repays unpacking, because "well-regulated" is easily misread as a pious mood. It is not a mood; it is a program. A well-regulated church music meant competent professional forces, adequate funding, systematic repertoire covering the whole church year — the institutional machinery of excellence, specified like an engineering requirement. It is precisely the demand he would submit to Leipzig in the famous [1730 memorandum](../leipzig-2/memo-1730.md), a lifetime later, nearly word for word: the same man, the same program, the same collision with institutions that had budgeted for something smaller. And that is the letter's eerie second function. It announces not only the mission but the exact shape of every conflict to come, because Bach's employers, from here to the end, repeatedly hired an *organist* or a *cantor* and got, unbargained-for, the *program* — a systematic vision of sacred music that no single post's job description had room for.

What happened next deserves registering, because it never happened quite this way again. The council released him with honor. It kept him on as consultant for the St. Blasius organ rebuild he had himself specified — his engineering memo survives, stop by stop — and invited him back to perform the following year's council cantata. He returned; no bridges burned. Of all Bach's departures, this was the gentlest, and the contrast is worth holding on to as the timeline moves forward: the next leaving, from the very Weimar post he was now accepting, [ends in a jail cell](../cothen/weimar-jail-1717.md). Mühlhausen shows that the pattern of conflict was not simple churlishness — where an employer dealt with him honorably, he answered in kind, for years. The wars came where the program met obstruction, and only there.

If this timeline had to keep a single sentence of Bach's prose, it keeps this one. The *Endzweck* is the thesis statement of Arc I: everything from the Weimar organ works through the Leipzig cantata cycles is the working-out of the clause a young organist filed with a town council in June 1708, and its final proof — the last exhibit in the case the sentence opens — is the [B minor Mass](../../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md), completed in the final year of his life. Public triumph in February, private mission stated in June: at twenty-three, the man is fully legible, and the remaining forty-two years are execution.
