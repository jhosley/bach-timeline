Mühlhausen inaugurated its new town council each February with a festival service, and on 4 February 1708 the new organist supplied the music: ***Gott ist mein König*** ("God is my king"), BWV 71, styled a *motetto* — for four separated instrumental-vocal choirs (trumpets and drums; recorders and cello; oboes and bassoon; strings) deployed antiphonally around the church, with organ. Psalm texts praising godly authority, an aria blessing the aged outgoing burgomaster, splendor calibrated precisely to civic self-regard. The council loved it — and voted to have the parts **engraved and printed** at town expense.

That printing is a fact worth its weight in the whole timeline: of the roughly two hundred surviving sacred cantatas (of perhaps three hundred written), **BWV 71 is the only one published in Bach's lifetime** — and it happened because a proud town council wanted a keepsake, not because any publisher saw a market. Bach's real publications, later, were keyboard works, issued mostly at his own expense. The vocal music on which his posthumous supremacy rests lived and died, as far as his century was concerned, in manuscript performing parts. Arc II's eclipse begins exactly here, in the economics of German music publishing.

**Why it matters.** Twenty-three years old and commanding multi-choir spatial forces with total assurance — the piece announces the *ambition* named four months later in [the resignation letter](muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md): church music, well-regulated, on the grandest available scale. And its printing is the timeline's standing exhibit on fame's machinery: what survives, and why, is never merely a function of merit.
