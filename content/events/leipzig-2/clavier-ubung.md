Nearly fifty, with a manuscript legacy the size of a library, Bach finally entered print on his own account: ***Clavier-Übung*** — "Keyboard Practice," a title borrowed from his Leipzig predecessor Kuhnau — four volumes across a decade, engraved and sold largely **at his own expense and risk** (agents included old [Böhm](../../people/georg-boehm.md) in the north).

**Part I (1726–31):** the six **Partitas**, issued singly then collected as his self-declared *Opus 1* — the German dance suite at terminal sophistication. **Part II (1735):** the **Italian Concerto** and **French Overture** — the two national orchestral styles transcribed into pure two-manual harpsichord, a compare-and-contrast exhibit deliberately bound as a pair. **Part III (1739):** the great organ volume — the "St. Anne" Prelude and triple Fugue in E-flat framing chorale settings of the catechism hymns, Lutheran doctrine as contrapuntal architecture (nicknamed the *German Organ Mass*). **Part IV (1741):** [the Goldberg Variations](../leipzig-3/goldberg-1741.md).

Note the design: the series *ascends* — suite, concerto, liturgy, variation-summa; two manuals, then organ; galant surfaces, then strict canon. It is a curated public monument, the encyclopedic instinct now aimed at posterity — and, read against [Scheibe's 1737 attack](scheibe-1737.md), a decade-long rebuttal in engraved copper: *this* is what the "turgid" art can do.

**Why it matters.** Economics again: no publisher solicited this; the century's supreme keyboard music reached print only because its composer **paid for it**. What he chose to immortalize (keyboard works, not cantatas) shaped his afterlife for a hundred years — Arc II's keyboard-first Bach is, in part, Bach's own editorial decision. The vocal works stayed in manuscript, and slept.
