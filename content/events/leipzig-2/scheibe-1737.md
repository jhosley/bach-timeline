On 14 May 1737, in the pages of his Hamburg journal *Der critische Musicus*, a twenty-nine-year-old critic named Johann Adolph Scheibe published, anonymously, the most consequential review of the century. He did not name his target; he did not need to. The greatest keyboard master alive, Scheibe wrote, "would be the admiration of whole nations" *if* only he had more agreeableness and less bombast — *Schwulst*, turgidity, the swollen style. The charges were specific and, in their way, acute. This composer "darkened the beauty of his works by an excess of art." He wrote out every ornament in full, smothering the melody that gracefulness should have left free. He demanded that singers and players "do with their throats and instruments whatever he can play on the clavier" — an unnatural, laborious style whose effort "is vain, since it conflicts with Nature." Every word was aimed at Bach, and Leipzig knew it by breakfast.

The messenger had a history worth noting. Scheibe was the son of the Leipzig organ builder [whose university instrument Bach had examined in 1717](../cothen/arrival-cothen-1717.md), and he had himself once failed an audition that Bach judged — facts that posterity has enjoyed perhaps too much, since the personal grudge, if there was one, explains the attack's timing better than its content. The content was the genuine conviction of a generation.

The defense arrived in January 1738, over the name of Johann Abraham Birnbaum, a Leipzig lecturer in rhetoric — though its inside knowledge of compositional detail betrays Bach's collaboration at every turn; the cantor did not write his own defense, but he evidently sat at the rhetorician's elbow. Birnbaum's argument went to the philosophical root: art does not oppose nature, it *completes* it; the fullest harmony is nature raised to its perfection; what Scheibe called excess was not decoration laid over the music but the music's very substance. The pamphlets volleyed for years afterward, and the profession's partisans — Mizler's learned society among them — duly chose sides.

Read past the insults, and the exchange is the eighteenth century's aesthetic fault line drawn in miniature, with unusual clarity because both sides argued well. Scheibe spoke for the *galant* generation: melody first, ease, sentiment, the listener sovereign — music as agreeable communication, judged by the pleasure it gives. Birnbaum and Bach spoke for learned counterpoint's claim that musical truth is structural — that the interweaving of voices is not a scholar's obstacle between listener and tune but the deepest thing music can do. Neither position is stupid; the fault line runs through arguments about art to this day, wherever accessibility and density are set against each other. What gives the 1737 exchange its permanent fascination is the verdict of time, which performed a precise half-reversal. Scheibe accurately described the music — dense, maximal, every voice worked, nothing left to casual grace — and accurately predicted his own era's taste, which did indeed move his way for two generations. Time reversed only the evaluation, not the description: what Scheibe called turgid, posterity calls inexhaustible. The future filed its complaint in 1737, and the further future overruled it.

For this timeline, the attack is essential context for everything Bach did afterward. He was fifty-two; the institutions were demoting his office in [the prefect war](prefect-dispute-1736.md), and now the press had demoted his style — the two fronts of the era's pressure, arriving in consecutive years. Against that backdrop, the late encyclopedic works read as conscious answers: [Clavier-Übung III](clavier-ubung.md) in 1739, [the Goldbergs](../leipzig-3/goldberg-1741.md) in 1741, [the Art of Fugue begun](../leipzig-3/art-of-fugue-begun.md) — counterpoint's case stated not in pamphlets but in the art itself, entered into the record for a posterity Bach could not know would come. The symmetry of the aftermath belongs to the whole arc of this site: Arc II opens with Bach's own sons half-agreeing with Scheibe, writing the agreeable music the new taste demanded; Arc III ends with nobody remembering Scheibe for anything else. The review survives because its target did — the complaint preserved forever, like an insect in the amber of the reputation it failed to diminish.
