The trigger was small: the rector of the Thomasschule, **Johann August Ernesti** — twenty-nine, a rising star of classical philology, a modernizer — punished a choir prefect (a senior student who conducted in the cantor's absence) and installed a replacement, one Krause, whom Bach judged musically unfit. Bach, holding prefect appointments to be the *cantor's* jurisdiction, expelled the young man from the choir loft — publicly, twice, mid-service. Ernesti reinstated him. The volleys of formal complaint that followed — Bach's four escalating memoranda, Ernesti's lawyered rebuttals — ran **two years**, ascending from council to consistory to, ultimately, the King-Elector in Dresden ([whose new court composer](dresden-title-1733-36.md) Bach had, with suspicious timing, just become; the royal visit of 1738 seems to have imposed the peace whose exact terms are lost).

The record is mutually unflattering — Ernesti pedantically vindictive, Bach immovably territorial (his Arnstadt consistory file, aged fifty) — and the schoolboys, by a later student's memoir, sorted into partisans. But the farce carried a real question: Ernesti belonged to a new pedagogy for which **music was a subordinate subject**, not the school's organizing purpose; boys' rehearsal hours were Latin hours lost. The cantor's jurisdiction was the old Lutheran settlement — [the one that had fed and educated Bach himself](../lueneburg/luneburg-scholarship-1700.md) — and it was losing, in Leipzig as everywhere.

**Why it matters.** The last and longest of Bach's institutional wars, and the first one that was really about *the era changing* rather than a budget or a title. Read with [Scheibe's aesthetic attack the following year](scheibe-1737.md): the same tide, hitting the same man, on two fronts at once — and the biographical backdrop to his late-life turn toward [monuments beyond any institution's reach](../../eras/leipzig-3.md).
