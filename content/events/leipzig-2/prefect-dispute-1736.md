The longest war of Bach's institutional life began with a matter so small that summarizing it risks parody: the question of who had the right to appoint a student conductor. A prefect, in the Thomasschule's economy, was a senior boy who led the choir in the cantor's absence — a working necessity in a system where one cantor supplied four churches with music. In the summer of 1736 the school's rector, Johann August Ernesti — twenty-nine years old, a rising star of classical philology, and a modernizer to his fingertips — punished one of these prefects and installed a replacement, a young man named Krause whom Bach judged musically unfit to lead anything. Bach held prefect appointments to be the cantor's jurisdiction, and no one else's. His response was not a memo. He expelled Krause from the choir loft publicly, twice, in the middle of divine service — the fifty-one-year-old Director of Music physically asserting his authority before the assembled congregation. Ernesti reinstated the boy. And the paper war began.

It ran two full years. Bach filed four escalating memoranda; Ernesti answered with lawyered rebuttals; the dispute ascended from the town council to the church consistory and finally to the King-Elector in Dresden himself — [whose new court composer](dresden-title-1733-36.md), with timing too neat to be coincidence, Bach had become just as the quarrel opened. The royal visit of 1738 seems to have imposed the peace, though its exact terms are lost; the armor Bach had spent three years acquiring in Dresden was tested here, and apparently held.

Honesty requires saying that the record flatters neither man. Ernesti emerges as pedantically vindictive, pursuing a point long past its worth; Bach as immovably territorial, the same man whose Arnstadt consistory file records the same temperament at twenty — aged fifty and undiluted. A later student's memoir reports that the schoolboys sorted themselves into partisans, cantor's men against rector's men, which suggests how thoroughly the quarrel consumed the institution. Read as personalities, the affair is farce: two brilliant, stubborn officials of a provincial school, escalating a staffing decision to a king.

But read as history, the farce carried a real question, and the question is why this card matters more than any of Bach's earlier employment battles. Ernesti belonged to a new pedagogy — enlightened, philological, forward-looking — for which music was a subordinate subject in a school's curriculum, not the school's organizing purpose. Every hour a boy spent in rehearsal was, on this view, a Latin hour lost. The cantor's jurisdiction that Bach defended so ferociously was not a personal privilege; it was the old Lutheran settlement, in which the choir school existed *for* the church's music and a poor boy with a voice could be fed, housed, and educated by singing — [the very settlement that had fed and educated Bach himself](../lueneburg/luneburg-scholarship-1700.md), a Thuringian orphan turned scholarship chorister. When Bach fought for the right to name a prefect, he was fighting for the proposition that music stood at the school's center. And that proposition was losing — in Leipzig, and everywhere, to a century that had begun to file music under ornament.

This is what distinguishes the prefect war from everything that preceded it. The 1730 memo was about a budget; the Dresden campaign was about a title; those were battles a shrewd man could win, and Bach partly did. The prefect dispute was the first of his wars that was really about the era changing underneath him, and no memorandum reaches that adversary. It is worth reading this card beside [Scheibe's aesthetic attack, published the following year](scheibe-1737.md): the same tide striking the same man on two fronts at once — the institutions demoting his office while the critics demoted his style. Seen against that double pressure, the last decade's turn makes biographical sense: a composer who could not win the argument with his own era began addressing [monuments beyond any institution's reach](../../eras/leipzig-3.md) — to no employer, no rector, no council, but to the art itself, and to whoever might be listening later. The prefect war was unwinnable. The response was to change wars.
