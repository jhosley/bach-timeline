In August 1730 the Leipzig town council convened in what its own minutes preserve as one of the ugliest sessions of the era, and the subject was the cantor. Seven years of friction boiled into the record at once: the man "does nothing," the councilmen complained; he shirked his teaching, left town without leave. The burgomaster reached for a single summarizing word — "incorrigible" — and the council voted to withhold income. Read from the council's side of the table, the file describes an ordinary personnel problem: a difficult employee, hired to teach in a school and supply four churches with music, who kept treating the job as something larger than its terms. Read from the cantor's side, the same seven years had been a slow demonstration that the job's terms could not sustain the job's purpose. Both readings are honest, which is exactly why the collision of that August produced something more valuable than a quarrel.

Bach's answer is dated 23 August 1730, and it is the central document of the era: the *Short but Most Necessary Draft for a Well-Appointed Church Music*. It is not a complaint, and it is not an apology. It is an audit. The "well-regulated church music" he had declared as his life's purpose in [his Mühlhausen resignation of 1708](../muehlhausen/muehlhausen-resignation-1708.md) — his *Endzweck*, the through-line of his whole professional life — is here translated into an engineer's tables. Proper concerted music, he itemizes, requires at minimum twelve to sixteen singers, three to four voices per part, four choirs' worth to cover four churches; and eighteen to twenty instrumentalists besides. Against these requirements he then sets what he actually has, listed by name and graded by competence: a handful able, the rest middling or unfit, and the town's professional instrumentalists aging and undermanned — "modesty forbids me to speak," he writes of their quality, in the memo's most quietly devastating line, and then speaks anyway by declining to.

What lifts the document above grievance is its diagnosis. Bach does not blame the boys or the players; he explains them, with a sociologist's clarity a century ahead of the genre. Musicians must live. Dresden's court musicians are paid well enough to devote themselves to mastering a single instrument; Leipzig's scramble for bread, and their playing shows it. Excellence, in other words, is not a moral quality to be exhorted out of people but an economic condition to be funded — pay comparable to Dresden's, or accept the difference audibly, every Sunday. It is the case every underfunded institution's best employee has wanted to make to management, made once, perfectly, by the greatest music director alive. And then the record supplies the memo's most eloquent feature: as far as any surviving document shows, it received no reply. Not a rebuttal, not a counter-proposal. Silence.

The private sequel came two months later, in the letter to [Georg Erdmann](../../people/georg-erdmann.md), a school friend from thirty years before, now Russian resident in Danzig. There Bach dropped the auditor's manner and confessed: the post had proved less lucrative than promised, living in Leipzig was costly, the authorities were "odd and little interested in music," and he lived amid "almost constant vexation, envy, and persecution." Would Danzig, he asked, have anything for him? It is the only moment in the documented life where Bach can be watched actively job-hunting out of Leipzig, and nothing came of it. No better job existed; the memo's logic — that the problem was structural, not personal — held wherever he might have gone.

What he did instead is why this card anchors the era. Denied resources and denied escape, Bach engineered leverage: within three years he was in Dresden presenting a Missa to a new king, and every subsequent institutional battle of his life — [the pursuit of the court title](dresden-title-1733-36.md), [the two-year war over the prefects](prefect-dispute-1736.md) — is this memo continued by other means. For the theme of working conditions that runs through this whole timeline, no document of the century says more: a precise, funded, unanswerable account of what excellence costs, entered into the record, and ignored. The council kept its minutes; the minutes kept the council's judgment; and posterity, which got the music anyway, is left to wonder how much more there might have been if anyone had answered the memo.
