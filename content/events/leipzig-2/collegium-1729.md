In March 1729 Bach assumed direction of the Collegium Musicum, the concert society of university students and town professionals that [Telemann](../../people/telemann.md) had founded back in 1702, in his own student days — an ensemble of forty-odd capable players giving weekly public concerts at Zimmermann's coffee house: Friday evenings indoors in winter, Wednesday afternoons in the coffee garden in summer, extra series when the trade fairs filled the city. Notice what is missing from that description: no church, no court. Admission came with the coffee. This was the bourgeois public concert, functioning and durable, decades before "concert hall" existed as a building type anywhere in Germany, and for the next stretch of his life — 1729 to 1737, and again 1739 to 1741 — it was where Bach's non-liturgical energy lived.

Why would the most overworked musician in Saxony volunteer for a weekly concert series? The honest answer is that every reading fits, and they reinforce rather than exclude one another. Here was an ensemble actually worth directing — compare [the 1730 memo's grim inventory](memo-1730.md) of his church forces, written the following year, to understand what forty able players must have felt like. Here was repertoire freedom, and extra income, and a stage for university-age sons who needed one. And here — after the council's treatment of him, after the church's non-reception of the Passions — was something simpler: an audience that wanted him. The coffee house heard Bach the way we might have wished to ourselves, as living entertainment, applauded on the spot by people who had chosen to come.

The repertoire consequence was epochal almost by accident. Needing fresh material every week, with himself, Friedemann, and Emanuel available at the keyboards, Bach rebuilt his Cöthen and Weimar concertos as harpsichord concertos — BWV 1052 through 1065, including the multi-keyboard spectaculars that put two, three, and four harpsichords on the platform at once. In doing so he effectively founded the public keyboard concerto as a genre: the line that runs through Mozart's Vienna evenings to every piano concerto since starts, for practical purposes, in a Leipzig coffee house, and [Brandenburg No. 5](../../works/cothen/bwv-1050.md), with its harpsichord breaking out of the ensemble a decade earlier, had planted the seed. Around the concertos flowed orchestral suites, chamber music, visiting virtuosi dropping in during fair weeks — and [Picander](../../people/picander.md)'s comic secular cantatas, above all the Coffee Cantata of around 1734, a miniature opera about a daughter's caffeine addiction, performed *inside the coffee house it gently mocked*. Anyone who doubts that Bach possessed a sense of humor may consult the documentation; it survives, with a libretto.

The deeper significance of the Collegium years is what they prove about the man and what they preview about music itself. They prove that Bach's art was never church-bound by temperament, whatever his job description said — give him a public and he entertained it brilliantly, week after week, in a room smelling of coffee. And they preview the coming economy of music: the court and church models that employed Bach his whole life were already yielding, in embryo, to the ticket-buying public assembling at Zimmermann's tables — the same public that would, a century on, [fill halls to resurrect him](../../eras/revival.md). The cantor spent his Sundays serving the old order and his Friday evenings, without knowing it, rehearsing the new one.
