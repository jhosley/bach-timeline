The Eisenach burial register records that Elisabeth Bach was buried on 3 May 1694. It records no cause. Behind the entry stood twenty-six years of marriage and eight children, and in front of it stood a nine-year-old son; the register, being a register, notes none of that. Her husband remarried that November — a widow, herself twice bereaved, so that the marriage joined two households already schooled in loss — and the speed of it should be read structurally rather than sentimentally. Johann Ambrosius held a civic post that assumed a functioning family workshop; he had a household of dependents to feed and apprentices to manage; a town musician without a wife was an enterprise without a manager. The eighteenth century remarried the way it did everything else: because the work could not wait.

Then the work stopped anyway. Three months into the new marriage, in late February 1695, Ambrosius died at fifty. His widow petitioned the town to keep the music post in the family — the clan's reflex, the machinery of succession trying to engage one more time — and was refused. Within months the household broke up. Sebastian had been nine when his mother died; he was not yet ten when his father followed. Within nine months the boy had lost both parents, and the world built around the family workshop — the strings and brass by day, the eighth child's whole known life — dissolved with the estate. Of the surviving children, the two youngest boys, Sebastian and Johann Jacob, were sent thirty miles south to Ohrdruf, into [the household of their eldest brother](../ohrdruf/ohrdruf-brother-1695.md), Johann Christoph — a twenty-three-year-old organist, newly married and modestly paid, who took them in.

About what any of this felt like, the record is silent in a way that deserves to be stated plainly, because this timeline's honesty depends on such statements. The documents give us only the administrative skeleton: burial registers, a remarriage, a petition and its refusal, a school enrollment thirty miles away. No letter survives, no reminiscence, no anecdote. Even the Obituary — the memorial biography assembled by Bach's own son and students, which lingers lovingly over keyboard rivalries and manuscript-copying — passes over the entire catastrophe in a phrase. Whatever the boy felt in that nine-month collapse is outside the record, and this timeline will not invent it.

But refusing to invent feelings is not the same as refusing to see structure, and the structural fact belongs on the family track in the plainest possible terms: the composer whose sacred music returns again and again to death as homecoming — the *Actus tragicus*, "Komm, süßes Kreuz," "Ich habe genug" — buried both parents before his tenth birthday. He then spent the rest of his life inside a liturgy and a theology that were built, with a craftsman's thoroughness, to answer exactly that experience: a tradition that had spent two centuries teaching orphans and widows to sing about death as consolation rather than terror. [Gardiner](../../people/john-eliot-gardiner.md), surveying the documentary record of the adult Bach — the bristling self-reliance, the readiness to fight any authority over the terms of his work, the ferocious industry, and that lifelong preoccupation in the texted music with death as a friend — puts the unavoidable point plainly: everything in that record belongs to a man who was an orphan before he was ten. Biographers rightly resist psychologizing, and the caution is honored here; no document connects the boy's losses to the man's music. What the documents do establish is the sequence — first the graves, then the theology, then sixty years of music in which the theology's answer to the graves is set again and again, with an insistence no colleague of his ever matched. The reader may draw the line between those points or leave it undrawn. The timeline's job is only to mark them.
