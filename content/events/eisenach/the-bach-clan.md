In 1735, at fifty, Bach sat down and wrote a genealogy: *Ursprung der musicalisch-Bachischen Familie* — "Origin of the Musical Bach Family" — tracing fifty-three musical Bachs back to Veit Bach, a sixteenth-century baker who (in the family's founding legend) took his cittern to the mill and played while the wheel ground his grain. That document, kept and annotated by his son Emanuel, is our best window into how Bach understood himself: not as a lone genius but as the ripest fruit of a **family enterprise**.

The clan operated like a guild within the guilds: fathers trained sons and nephews; posts as organist, cantor, or town musician across Thuringia passed along kin networks; and once a year the scattered Bachs gathered — the family reunion reportedly opening with a chorale and ending with improvised comic **quodlibets** (songs stitching several tunes together at once — counterpoint as family joke; remember this when you meet the quodlibet that ends the [Goldberg Variations](../../works/leipzig/goldberg-variations.md)).

Among the dozens of journeymen, one figure towered: cousin **Johann Christoph Bach** (1642–1703), organist at Eisenach's Georgenkirche, whose motets and lament *Ach, dass ich Wassers gnug hätte* carry an expressive chromatic daring nothing else in the family approached. The Obituary calls him "profound" — the family's own word for its pre-Sebastian summit.

**Why it matters.** Bach's staggering fluency was infrastructure before it was inspiration: a birthright of training methods, professional networks, and accumulated repertoire. Nurture had stacked the deck for six generations; then nature dealt Sebastian.
