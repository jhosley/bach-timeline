The job had small print, and in the end the small print took his eyes. A lifetime of tiny notes copied and composed by candlelight had strained Bach's sight for decades; through 1749 it failed badly. The town council responded with characteristic tact: that June, while their cantor lived and worked, they held a quiet pre-audition for his successor — "in case." The proceedings were not secret enough. He surely knew.

In late March and early April of 1750 he submitted to two eye operations by John "Chevalier" Taylor, a traveling English oculist then passing through Leipzig — a man of maximal self-promotion and dubious technique, whose later clientele would include Handel, with the same result. The surgeries and their brutal aftercare left Bach fully blind and gravely weakened. The Obituary assembled by his son and his student supplies the last scenes: ten days before the end, his sight briefly and inexplicably returned; hours later, a stroke. The deathbed tradition — that he dictated a final chorale, *Vor deinen Thron tret ich hiermit*, "Before thy throne I now appear" — is flagged *tradition* here, and the documented truth is gentler and messier: he revised an existing setting, probably earlier. The legend gives the life a closing cadence; the record gives it what the life had always actually contained, a craftsman revising. He died on the evening of 28 July 1750, sixty-five years old, and was buried three days later at St. John's cemetery. The register records an oak coffin. No gravestone was set.

The aftermath moved fast on every track, and its speed is its own commentary. The council filled the post within weeks, and a burgomaster summarized three decades of institutional friction in one immortal sentence: the school needed *a cantor, not a Kapellmeister*. They had employed one of the supreme artists of the age for twenty-seven years and were, on the record, relieved to be done with the arrangement. The estate — the instruments, the great music library, a modest sum of money — was inventoried and divided among nine surviving heirs. The performing parts and manuscripts scattered with the sons, and [the whole second arc of this timeline opens with the consequences](../../eras/eclipse.md) of that dispersal: the fate of the music now hanging on the diligence, the luck, and the finances of individual heirs. [Anna Magdalena](../../people/anna-magdalena-bach.md), left with the youngest children, slid into her alms-supported decade — the widow of the Kapellmeister dying as a recipient of city charity.

Then there is the grave itself, which compresses the entire reception history this timeline exists to tell into a single piece of ground. The location was lost within a generation — not desecrated, simply forgotten, which is worse. It took an excavation in 1894 to rediscover the remains, and in 1950, two centuries after the burial, they were moved into the choir of the Thomaskirche, where they lie today under a bronze slab that is among the most visited spots in Leipzig. Hold the two burials side by side: an unmarked plot at St. John's, mourned by a family about to scatter; and a bronze slab in the great church, sought out daily by travelers from every continent. Everything between those two images — the eclipse, the custody, the revival, the canonization — is the rest of this timeline.

So Arc I ends with every one of its themes in view. The working conditions: blinded by the job's small print, replaced with relief by the employer he had outworked and outfought. The family economy: broken at the moment its engine stopped. The manuscripts: beginning their century of dispersal, attic by attic. And the reputation: at the lowest ebb it would ever know, an old contrapuntist's name held, for the moment, by a handful of sons and students. The man who had spent his last decade addressing posterity was now entirely in posterity's hands — and posterity, for two generations, would barely notice what it was holding.
