The autograph survives from about 1742: a cycle of fugues — Bach's term is ***Contrapunctus*** — every one built from the same plain D-minor subject, arranged by ascending discipline: simple fugues; counter-fugues (the subject against its own inversion); double and triple fugues; strict canons; and a pair of **mirror fugues** playable entire in inversion — the whole texture flipped upside down, and still correct. In his final years he revised and expanded the cycle for engraved publication, adding among other things a culminating fugue on three subjects, of which the third spells **B–A–C–H** (in German note-names, B-flat–A–C–B-natural): the signature entering the cathedral, at last, as a subject.

That final fugue **breaks off mid-phrase** in the surviving manuscript — and on the page, in Emanuel's hand: "at this point... the composer died." The note is not literally true (the handwriting predates the final illness; the missing ending may even have existed separately), and the R3 card will walk the scholarship — but as *curated legend* it out-performs every fact in the file: generations have heard those empty staves as the most eloquent silence in music. Published posthumously in 1751 by the sons' efforts, the print sold a few dozen copies; the plates were eventually offered for the value of the copper.

No instrument is specified — open score, four voices — and the old "abstract, unplayable" reading has yielded to the modern consensus (and keyboard-shaped evidence): it is playable, and meant to be, at a keyboard. Theory *as* music, not instead of it.

**Why it matters.** The Craft section's terminal exhibit: the [Invention's](../../works/cothen/inventions-sinfonias.md) promise — one idea, fully developed — executed at life's-work scale on a single theme. If the timeline's counterpoint thread has a destination, this is it.
