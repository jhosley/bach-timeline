Before Lambaréné and the Nobel Peace Prize, **Albert Schweitzer** was an Alsatian organist-theologian whose 1905 Bach study (French first, then the expanded German of 1908) became the twentieth century's founding Bach book. Its thesis: Bach is a **tone-poet** — his music built from a consistent pictorial-symbolic vocabulary, legible above all in [the chorale preludes](../../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md), where one figure per piece *depicts* the hymn's idea: falling motives for the Fall, wave-figures for water, the "grief" chromatics, the "joy" rhythms. Read the figures, Schweitzer argued, and the "abstract" instrumental Bach dissolves into a preacher with motifs for words.

The scholarship has aged the way strong theses do: the core observation is [period-true](../../craft/craft-06-theology.md) — the figures *are* a rhetoric, and Schweitzer taught generations to hear them — while the system overreaches (not every third is a Trinity; this timeline's layer-model exists partly to keep his insight without his excess). His practical legacy aged better: as a leading voice of **organ reform**, he campaigned against the orchestral monster-organs of his day and for the clear, singing instruments Bach knew — the first major turn of [the Romantic portrait](romantic-transcriptions.md) toward what would become [the historical movement](hip-1971.md). His own recordings survive: slow by modern taste, and moving precisely for their preacher's deliberateness.

**Why it matters.** Arc III's first *interpretive* revolution — Bach re-read rather than re-arranged — and a biographical emblem the timeline can't resist: the century's great humanitarian formed himself on [the Orgelbüchlein's](../../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md) theology of figures, then lived it. Reception history occasionally produces saints.
