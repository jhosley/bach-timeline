For the year 2000 — the 250th anniversary of [Bach's death](../leipzig-3/death-1750.md) — **John Eliot Gardiner**, the Monteverdi Choir, and the English Baroque Soloists undertook the maddest and most illuminating project of the modern Bach era: performing **all ~198 surviving sacred cantatas, each on its own liturgical date**, across a single church year — some sixty programs in churches from Iona to Rome, Weimar's Himmelsburg territory to New York for the final Christmas concerts. Not a cycle *of* the cantatas; a year lived *inside* [the machine that made them](../../events/leipzig-1/cantata-machine.md) — learning weekly, traveling weekly, performing weekly: the closest any modern musicians have come to Bach's own working calendar, and Gardiner's journals record the discovery that the works *explain themselves* in that rhythm — each cantata answering its Sunday's Gospel, its season's light.

The project's business coda became legend: partway through, Deutsche Grammophon dropped the recording contract — so Gardiner **founded his own label, Soli Deo Gloria (SDG)**, [Bach's own colophon repurposed](../../craft/craft-06-theology.md), and released the Pilgrimage himself: twenty-eight volumes, the timeline's default cantata recommendation. Thirteen years later came the book, ***Music in the Castle of Heaven*** — [this project's most-cited modern source](../../BIBLIOGRAPHY.md): the conductor's Bach, humanized ([the unruly schoolboy](../../events/arnstadt/geyersbach-1705.md), [the fractious employee](../../events/leipzig-2/memo-1730.md)) and made urgent by a lifetime of performance.

**Why it matters.** The arc's fullest answer to *how should this music live now*: not museum, not modernization — **re-inhabitation**. And for this study companion, the reason Gardiner threads the whole site: catalog recommendations, era epigraphs, [the Himmelsburg's very name](../../events/weimar/himmelsburg.md). The Pilgrimage proved the cantatas are a *calendar*, and this timeline's liturgical dates are, in part, its debt.
