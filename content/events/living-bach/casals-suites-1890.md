In 1890, a thirteen-year-old cello student and his father were browsing a secondhand shop near the Barcelona harbor for sheet music when the boy pulled out a worn volume: *Six Suites for Violoncello Solo* — [music he had never heard of](../../works/cothen/cello-suites.md), because in 1890 almost nobody had: cellists knew at most a movement or two, treated as exercises. **Pablo Casals** later told the story all his life — how the pages "seemed to glow," how he hurried home clutching them — with a storyteller's polish this timeline duly flags (*the scene is his own memoir; the dates and the outcome are documented*).

What is beyond dispute: he studied them daily for **twelve years before playing one suite complete in public**, and his eventual performances — then the epochal **Abbey Road and Paris recordings of 1936–39**, made in his sixties as Spain burned behind him — settled the question forever. The suites were not études; they were, in his words, "the very essence of Bach," and the cello had a scripture. Every cellist since — Fournier, Rostropovich, Yo-Yo Ma (whose multiple traversals bracket a modern career the way Casals's bracketed his) — records them *against* Casals; the work's place at the repertoire's center is one man's act of attention, compounding.

**Why it matters.** Arc III's purest case of **single-performer canonization** — reception history's power concentrated in one pair of hands — and a study-companion's parable: the greatest works can sit unread on the shelf for a century, waiting for someone to take them literally. Check the shelf.
