Two years, three teachers — only one of whom ever knew he held the job.

Bach came to Lüneburg as a **Mettenchor scholar**: a poor boy singing matins for his bread at the Michaelisschule, attached to the wealthy St. Michael's convent-church. The scholarship's premise collapsed almost immediately — his soprano voice broke — but the school kept him: he was too useful as a violinist and keyboard player, and (the pattern of his whole youth) his usefulness kept buying him education.

The first teacher was a **library**. St. Michael's held one of the great church-music collections of Protestant Germany — over a thousand works spanning two centuries, from Renaissance polyphony to the moderns. For a boy whose method was copying, it was a candy store with the cabinet unlocked. Here the choral tradition — Schütz's generation back through the Lutheran motet — entered his bloodstream alongside the keyboard training he'd brought from Ohrdruf.

The second was **Georg Böhm**, organist of the Johanniskirche in town: a Thuringian countryman, a master of the chorale partita and of the French-tinted north-German style. The Obituary is oddly silent about a formal connection, but in 2006 scholars identified the teenage Bach's handwriting in organ tablatures (a German note-letter notation) copied *"à Dom. Georg: Böhme"* — in the house of, or under, Master Georg Böhm — including Reincken's massive chorale fantasia *An Wasserflüssen Babylon*. It is the earliest surviving trace of Bach's hand, and it settles the relationship: the boy was working in Böhm's orbit, copying the hardest repertoire of the northern school. Note the piece: eighteen years later, in Hamburg, Bach improvised on that same chorale for half an hour [before the dying Reincken himself](../events/cothen/hamburg-reincken-1720.md) — a full-circle moment this timeline can now draw as a single thread.

The third teacher was the **north itself**. From Lüneburg Bach made trips (some on foot, thirty miles) to Hamburg to hear **Reincken** at St. Catherine's — the most spectacular organ playing in Germany on one of its greatest instruments — and absorbed, at the nearby francophile court of **Celle**, the living French style: Lully's orchestral manners, French dance rhythms, *agréments* (the ornament vocabulary). File the geography: the boy walked to his education, north to the organists, and the French suites, ouvertures, and ornament tables of his maturity all have receipts from these two years.

He finished school in 1702, seventeen, degree-equivalent in hand, qualified for university but penniless for it. The clan's gravity pulled him home: back to Thuringia, to the job market. The education years were over; the employment wars were about to begin.
