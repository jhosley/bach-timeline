title: "Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars"

The middle Leipzig years run on two parallel rails that barely touch: downtown, the most delightful music-making of Bach's life; at the school and council chambers, its bitterest institutional grind.

The delight had an address: **Zimmermann's coffee house**, home of the **Collegium Musicum** — the concert society of university students and professionals founded by Telemann a generation earlier, whose direction Bach assumed in 1729. Friday evenings in winter, garden afternoons in summer, admission by coffee: Germany's first durable *public* concert culture, and Bach's laboratory for a decade. Here the Cöthen concertos were revived and rebuilt for harpsichord — creating, almost as a byproduct, **the keyboard concerto as a public genre**, with Bach and his sons at the instruments; here the secular cantatas sparkled (the *Coffee Cantata* gently mocking the very house that hosted it); here visiting virtuosi dropped in on fair weeks. After the church's non-reception of the Passions, the coffee house heard Bach the way we might have wanted to: as living entertainment.

The grind produced the era's great documents. In August 1730 the council, in ugly session, voted to discipline its "incorrigible" cantor; Bach answered with the **Draft for a Well-Appointed Church Music** — the memo itemizing, with an engineer's tables, what his *Endzweck* actually required (twelve to sixteen singers minimum, a proper orchestra, and pay comparable to Dresden's) versus what he had (a fraction, and aging). Two months later came [the Erdmann letter](../people/georg-erdmann.md), privately confessing he was job-hunting. No better job came; instead he engineered **leverage**: the 1733 *Missa* (the future B minor Mass's first half) presented to the new Elector in Dresden, and — after three years' pursuit — the title of **Royal Polish and Electoral Saxon Court Composer** (1736), armor for the local wars.

The wars obliged. The **prefect dispute** (1736–38) — a two-year jurisdictional siege against the new rector Ernesti over who appointed the student choir leaders — reads as farce and was really a referendum on whether music still stood at the school's center (it was losing). And in 1737 the critic **Scheibe** published the era's verdict on Bach's style itself: *turgid*, over-artful, against nature — the fashionable *galant* generation dismissing counterpoint as grandfather's mathematics. The public defense (Birnbaum's, with Bach's evident help) argued art *is* nature perfected; the deeper answer was the era's works: the **Clavier-Übung** publications (Partitas, Italian Concerto, the great organ mass of Part III, [the Goldbergs](leipzig-3.md)) — Bach self-publishing his counterpoint, at his own expense, into a marketplace that preferred easier pleasures; and the **Christmas Oratorio** (1734–35), recycling his most brilliant secular court music into scripture with the parody workshop at full power.

**Study anchor:** read [the 1730 memo card](../events/leipzig-2/memo-1730.md) with [the Scheibe card](../events/leipzig-2/scheibe-1737.md): resources and reputation, the two fronts on which the era pressed him — and against which the late encyclopedic turn of Leipzig III is best understood.
