title: "Mühlhausen: the mission statement"
years: "1707–1708"

One year — and in it, a marriage, a masterpiece in print, and the sentence that explains the next four decades.

Mühlhausen was a step up in every visible way: a free imperial city (answering to no duke — its council was sovereign, a preview of the civic politics Bach would spend his Leipzig decades navigating), a venerable organist post at the church of St. Blasius, and better pay, negotiated by Bach with a confidence the documents relish — he asked for, and got, his full Arnstadt package plus perquisites. He auditioned around Easter 1707 (quite possibly with *Christ lag in Todes Banden*, BWV 4 — that stark, blazing masterpiece of the old chorale-variation type; the association is scholarly inference, flagged on its card), took office in June, and that October, flush with a small inheritance from an uncle, married **Maria Barbara Bach** in the village church at Dornheim.

The year's public monument was the town-council election cantata of February 1708, *Gott ist mein König* (BWV 71) — festive brass, multiple choirs deployed in space, the young composer flexing everything the occasion allowed. The council was proud enough to have it **printed**: the only cantata of the thousand-odd Bach wrote to be published in his lifetime. Sit with that fact; Arc II's whole eclipse story is folded inside it.

But the church-music situation soured him with remarkable speed. Mühlhausen's Lutheran clergy were split between **orthodox** and **Pietist** tendencies — the Pietists suspicious of elaborate art-music in worship as worldly display; Bach's own pastor leaned Pietist, while the ambitious music-lover among the clergy was at the *other* church. A composer of Bach's ambitions was structurally in the wrong pulpit's parish. When Weimar offered him the ducal court organist post in June 1708 at nearly double the salary, his resignation letter to the Mühlhausen council delivered — politely, and apparently without irony — the **mission statement of his life**: he would go wherever he could pursue his true goal, *"a well-regulated church music to the glory of God."* (*Endzweck*, he called it: his final purpose.)

The council, to its credit, released him graciously and kept him consulting on the St. Blasius organ rebuild he had designed — his engineering memo survives, stop by stop. He returned the next year to perform; no bridges burned. Of all Bach's departures, the gentlest.

**Study anchor:** hold the two artifacts of this year together — the printed BWV 71 and the resignation letter's *Endzweck* sentence. Public triumph and private mission, both at twenty-three: the man is now fully legible, and the timeline's remaining forty-two years are the working-out.
