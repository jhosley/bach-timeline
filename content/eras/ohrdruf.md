The five Ohrdruf years are the least documented of Bach's life and among the most important: this is where he became a keyboard player.

His guardian and teacher was his eldest brother, **Johann Christoph Bach** — fourteen years older, organist of St. Michael's in Ohrdruf, recently married, raising a growing family of his own on a thin salary, and now responsible for two orphaned brothers besides. Whatever strain that household bore, it delivered: Johann Christoph had studied three years with **Johann Pachelbel** in Erfurt — the most celebrated keyboard teacher in central Germany — and he passed the Pachelbel method to Sebastian directly. Fingering, chorale technique, the composing-by-copying discipline of the German organ tradition: the pipeline ran Pachelbel → brother → Sebastian, one generation with no leakage.

School mattered too, more than is usually noticed. The Ohrdruf Lyceum was ambitious, and the registers survive: Sebastian ranked at or near the top of his class, years younger than his classmates — the only independent academic evidence we have for the man who later dueled Leipzig's rector over Latin teaching duties. He also earned his keep singing in the school choir, a fee-for-service chorister from age ten. Music as *livelihood* was never abstract to him.

From these years comes the most famous story of Bach's childhood: the **moonlight manuscript** — the brother's locked book of advanced keyboard music, the small hands reaching through the latticed cabinet, six months of secret midnight copying, the copy discovered and confiscated. It comes from the Obituary, is unverifiable, and is flagged *tradition* on its card — but even skeptics concede what the story was preserved to say: the boy's appetite outran anything his teachers were willing to feed him, and he responded by teaching himself. Copying other men's music remained his method of study for the next fifty years.

The era ends with a plain economic fact. Johann Christoph's household kept growing; free places at the school's table ran out; and at fifteen Sebastian needed a situation that would feed him. A choral scholarship for poor boys with good voices was found — two hundred miles north, in Lüneburg. In March 1700 he walked out of Thuringia, out of the clan's home territory, for the first time in any Bach's recorded history that far north. The apprentice years were over.

**Study anchor:** the [moonlight manuscript card](../events/ohrdruf/moonlight-manuscript.md) doubles as the timeline's introduction to *how Bach learned* — copying as analysis — a thread that runs through the Lüneburg tablatures, the Weimar Vivaldi transcriptions, and his lifelong habit of arranging other composers' work.
