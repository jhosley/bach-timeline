The ribbon reignites in a single documented evening — and, like everything on this timeline, the miracle had infrastructure.

The infrastructure was Berlin's **Singakademie**: a bourgeois choral society (founded 1791) whose directors had collected Bach manuscripts for decades and whose choir privately read through the old counterpoint its public would never buy tickets for. Its director **Carl Friedrich Zelter** — Goethe's musical confidant, gruff keeper of the flame — owned the treasures, doubted the public was ready, and taught, among others, a prodigy named **Felix Mendelssohn**. The relay's last handoff: for Felix's fifteenth birthday (or thereabouts — the family accounts vary), his grandmother had a **copyist's score of the St. Matthew Passion** made from Zelter's material. The boy studied it for years; a circle of young enthusiasts (the singer-actor Eduard Devrient decisive among them) pushed the reluctant Zelter; and on **11 March 1829** — a century, near enough, [after the work's Leipzig performances](../events/leipzig-1/matthew-passion-1727.md) — the twenty-year-old Mendelssohn conducted the Singakademie in the first performance of the St. Matthew Passion since Bach's death.

Berlin's intellectual world packed the hall — Hegel, Schleiermacher, Heine, the court; hundreds were turned away; two repeat performances followed within weeks (the second on Bach's birthday). The performance itself was a period artifact — heavily cut, re-orchestrated for modern forces, romantically inflected — and none of that mattered: the *event* was the point. [Applegate's study](../BIBLIOGRAPHY.md) shows what it crystallized: Forkel's national-heritage thesis, the Protestant revival, the Romantic hunger for depth — every current of the 1820s found its monument in one resurrected Passion, and Bach's public return was thereby **not a concert but a cultural founding**. Mendelssohn's own wry summary (via Devrient, *tradition* flag): *"To think that it took an actor and a Jew's son to revive the greatest Christian music for the world!"*

What followed had the character of a dam breaking in slow motion: Passion performances spreading city by city; the vocal works creeping into print; **Schumann** turning his critical journal into a Bach pulpit (and prodding, with Mendelssohn — who premiered the organ works in recital and led Leipzig's own revival as Gewandhaus kapellmeister — the great institutional act); until, on the death-centenary in **1850**, the founding of the **Bach-Gesellschaft**: the society whose forty-six volumes would print, for the first time, *everything* — [the cantatas by the hundred](../events/leipzig-1/cantata-machine.md), [the Mass](../works/leipzig/b-minor-mass.md), the lot. Brahms called its volumes the event of his lifetime; [the edition's first page](../works/leipzig/bwv-1.md) gave BWV 1 its number. The custody [lost in 1750](../events/eclipse/estate-dispersal-1750.md) was, at last, institutionalized.

One irony deserves its own sentence: the revival canonized Bach through the *vocal* works his own century had ignored — the exact inverse of [the keyboard-first eclipse](../events/leipzig-2/clavier-ubung.md). Each era, this arc keeps showing, gets the Bach it needs; the whole Bach exists only across all of them.

**Study anchor:** the [1829 card](../events/revival/mendelssohn-revival-1829.md) — the single most consequential concert in Western music history, and the reason this timeline's third arc exists at all.
