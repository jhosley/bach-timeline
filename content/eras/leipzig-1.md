Nothing else in the history of composition resembles Bach's first Leipzig years. For roughly two years without meaningful pause — and at high intensity for five — he composed, rehearsed, copied, and performed a new half-hour masterwork **every week**, on top of a teaching load, four churches' worth of liturgy, and a household the size of a small school. The surviving result is about two hundred cantatas from perhaps three hundred written, two Passions, the Magnificat — the largest concentrated body of great music ever produced by one person, made on a deadline that would break a modern film composer.

Understand the job to understand the feat. The **Thomaskantorat** was a church-school-civic hybrid: Bach was fourth master of the Thomasschule (obliged to teach — he immediately paid a colleague to cover the Latin), responsible for music in the city's four churches, and *director musices* of Leipzig — a proud commercial city, no court, run by the same species of council he'd known at Mühlhausen, now his permanent employer, landlord (the family lived in the school building, renovated around them), and adversary. His fixed salary was modest; the bulk of his ~700-thaler income came from **fees** — weddings, funerals — making his family's finances literally dependent on the city's mortality (his grimmest joke to Erdmann: a healthy year was bad for business).

The music ran on a weekly production cycle: text approved, composition early in the week, parts copied by the household (Anna Magdalena, the older boys, students), one rehearsal, performance Sunday at the Thomaskirche or Nikolaikirche in alternation — then again. The first annual cycle (1723–24) already beggars belief; the second — the **chorale cantata cycle** of 1724–25, each work built on one hymn as a unified design — is by common consent one of the supreme sustained achievements in Western art, and it was *workflow*.

Twice in these years the machine surged to something larger: the **St. John Passion** (1724), dramatic, urgent, controversial ever since; and the **St. Matthew Passion** (1727), double-choir, double-orchestra, contemplative and vast — music at a scale Leipzig's Good Friday had never imagined, and, as Arc II will show, the very work whose revival a century later resurrected its composer.

By decade's end the pace slackens — cycles complete, a librettist's cycle (Picander's, 1728–29) mostly lost, the composer's attention turning toward [the Collegium and the wider world](leipzig-2.md). What never slackened was the friction with his employers, which the next era's cards inherit in full.
