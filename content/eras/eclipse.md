The timeline's ribbon dims here, and the design is honest: for roughly eighty years after his death, Johann Sebastian Bach was not a famous composer. He was a famous *organist*, dead; a *pedagogue*, useful; and, above all, a *father* — because through the second half of the eighteenth century, "the great Bach" meant **Carl Philipp Emanuel**, and "Bach" without qualification might mean Emanuel, or the fashionable "London Bach" Johann Christian, before anyone thought of old Sebastian at all. The sons the timeline watched being born [in the Weimar nursery](../events/weimar/weimar-family-births.md) genuinely eclipsed him — Emanuel shaping the emerging classical style that Haydn and Mozart built on, Christian charming London (and personally influencing the eight-year-old Mozart). The era's taste had ruled, and it ruled for the sons: clarity, feeling, one melody at a time. [Scheibe had merely said early](../events/leipzig-2/scheibe-1737.md) what the century concluded.

The physical legacy fared worse than the reputation. [The estate divided](../events/leipzig-3/death-1750.md), the manuscripts scattered with the heirs: Emanuel, a careful curator, preserved his share (the Passions, the Mass); Friedemann — brilliant, unstable, increasingly poor — **sold his off piecemeal**, and much of what this timeline mourns as lost (cantata cycles, [Passions](../works/leipzig/st-mark-passion.md)) vanished through his hands. [Anna Magdalena's decade of poverty](../people/anna-magdalena-bach.md) ended in an alms-grave in 1760; the [Art of Fugue's plates](../works/leipzig/art-of-fugue.md) were nearly melted for copper. The vocal music — the weekly miracles of [the cantata machine](../events/leipzig-1/cantata-machine.md) — went silent almost totally: unpublished, liturgically obsolete, textually Lutheran in an Enlightenment century. What survived in *use* was exactly what this timeline flagged along the way: [the motets, sung continuously at the Thomasschule](../works/leipzig/motets.md); the chorales, collected as textbooks; [the keyboard works Bach himself had published](../events/leipzig-2/clavier-ubung.md); and the WTC.

Because here is the eclipse's great secret: it was never total, and where it wasn't, it glowed. **The [Well-Tempered Clavier](../works/cothen/wtc-1.md) circulated for eighty years in manuscript copy** — professional to student, hand to hand, a samizdat classic — and its copyists were the whole future: the young **Beethoven** made his Bonn reputation playing it; **Mozart**, ambushed by the counterpoint at [van Swieten's Vienna salon](../events/eclipse/van-swieten-salon.md) and then by [a motet in Leipzig itself](../events/eclipse/mozart-motet-1789.md), spent his last years folding Bach's polyphony into his own late style; **Haydn** owned the scores; the Berlin school of Bach's own students (Kirnberger, Agricola) codified his teaching into the theory books the next century learned from. The eclipse, examined closely, is a relay: the public forgot; the *professionals never did*. Every composer who built the classical style had Bach's counterpoint somewhere in the basement — which is why, when the revival came, it felt less like discovery than like the basement being opened.

The era ends with its own instruments of return: [Forkel's 1802 biography](../events/eclipse/forkel-1802.md) — the first, written with the sons' help and an openly nationalist thesis (this music as *German* patrimony); the swelling Romantic appetite for the old, the deep, the Gothic; and, in Berlin, a choral society quietly rehearsing from a copyist's score of a forgotten Passion. The ribbon is about to reignite — [one hundred years, almost to the season, after the music first sounded](../events/revival/mendelssohn-revival-1829.md).

**Study anchor:** the [Mozart motet card](../events/eclipse/mozart-motet-1789.md) — one documented scene that contains the whole arc: the era's greatest living composer, ambushed by the dead cantor's counterpoint, demanding the parts.
