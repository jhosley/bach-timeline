Nine years, two transformations: Bach arrived a brilliant north-German organist and left the complete European composer — and the change has a specific address and a specific crate of sheet music.

The address was the **[Himmelsburg](../events/weimar/himmelsburg.md)** — "Heaven's Castle," the court chapel of Duke Wilhelm Ernst, whose architecture preached a sermon: the musicians' gallery was set high in the ceiling, nearly a hundred feet above the congregation, so that music literally descended from above. ([Gardiner](../people/john-eliot-gardiner.md) took his book's title from it.) Here Bach was court organist, with a good instrument he soon had rebuilt to his specification, a pious duke who prized organ music, and a decade to work. The result is the core of the organ repertoire: the great preludes, toccatas, and fugues, and the **[Orgelbüchlein](../works/weimar/orgelbuechlein.md)** — the "Little Organ Book" of chorale preludes whose title page states his double creed: *to honor the highest God alone, and for the neighbor to learn from.* Worship and pedagogy, one gesture.

The crate arrived around 1713. The teenage **Prince Johann Ernst** — a gifted composer studying in the Netherlands — came home with the newest Amsterdam music prints, above all **Vivaldi's *L'estro armonico***. Bach devoured them the way he devoured everything: by transcription, arranging concerto after concerto for solo keyboard. What he absorbed was not tunes but *engineering* — the ritornello principle, the driving harmonic rhythm, the dramatic clarity of Italian form — and it fused with his German counterpoint into the hybrid that is simply "Bach's style" from then on. Ask what changed between the early toccatas and the mature works, and the shortest answer is: Vivaldi, metabolized.

In 1714 came promotion to **Konzertmeister** with a duty that changed the vocal music the same way: one cantata per month, his first sustained cantata production, in which the Italian operatic forms — recitative and da capo aria — entered his church music for good.

Weimar's politics finally soured everything. Two dukes co-ruled from adjacent palaces and detested each other; the court musicians were caught between — Wilhelm Ernst threatening fines for serving the rival household Bach befriended. When old Kapellmeister Drese died in 1716, Bach was passed over for the succession (it went to Drese's mediocre son); he stopped writing cantatas, essentially on strike, and secured the Cöthen offer. How Weimar ended — [a month in the duke's jail](../events/cothen/weimar-jail-1717.md) — is told on the Cöthen shelf, where its consequences lived.

**Study anchor:** the [Vivaldi transcriptions card](../events/weimar/vivaldi-transcriptions.md) is the era's hinge — and the best single answer in the whole timeline to "how did Bach learn form?"
