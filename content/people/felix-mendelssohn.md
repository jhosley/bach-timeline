Grandson of the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, son of a Berlin banking family, baptized Lutheran at seven, and the most complete musical prodigy since Mozart — trained by Zelter in the strict counterpoint that most of Europe had shelved, and gifted at fourteen [the manuscript score](../events/revival/singakademie-score-1824.md) that changed music history.

His Bach work bookends everything else he did. At twenty, [the 1829 revival](../events/revival/mendelssohn-revival-1829.md) — organized against his teacher's skepticism, conducted from memory. Then a lifetime of consolidation: organ recitals that returned [the great preludes and fugues](../works/weimar/bwv-582.md) to public life (his 1840 Leipzig recital raised money for a Bach monument he ultimately financed himself — it still stands near the Thomaskirche); the Gewandhaus "historical concerts"; his own Bach-saturated composing (the organ sonatas, the oratorios' chorales); and the advocacy that seeded [the Gesellschaft](../events/revival/bach-gesellschaft-1850.md) he didn't live to join.

The double position — the Jewish-descended reviver of Protestant Germany's musical scripture, inside a national movement that would later disown him (Wagner's poisonous essay appeared three years after his death; a later Germany tore down his statues while playing the Passion he revived) — is not a footnote to this timeline but one of its lessons: [reception is never neutral](../events/eclipse/forkel-1802.md), and the man who gave Bach back to the world knew it better than anyone in the hall.
