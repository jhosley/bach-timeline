A professional musician in her own right. Anna Magdalena Wilcke came from a family of court trumpeters and arrived at Cöthen around 1721 as a court singer — a soprano good enough to be among the best-paid members of Leopold's establishment. She was twenty when she married the thirty-six-year-old Kapellmeister in December 1721.

Her importance to the Bach story goes far beyond the household (though that was immense: thirteen children over the next two decades, six surviving). She became Bach's most trusted **copyist** — her music hand grew so close to his that scholars struggled for years to tell them apart, and some major works survive primarily in her copies, including the six [cello suites](../works/cothen/cello-suites.md), for which no autograph exists. The two *Clavierbüchlein* (little keyboard books) Bach compiled for her — 1722 in Cöthen, 1725 in Leipzig — are among the most humane documents we have of the family: teaching pieces, French suite movements, arias, and songs mixing pedagogy with domestic music-making.

Her ending is one of the saddest facts in the whole story, and it belongs to Arc II: after Bach's death in 1750 the estate was divided, the sons scattered, and Anna Magdalena lived her last decade in Leipzig in increasing poverty, dying in 1760 as an "alms woman." The city that had employed her husband for twenty-seven years buried her in an unmarked grave.
