The teenager who changed music history with his luggage. Johann Ernst — younger half-brother of the co-duke Ernst August, resident at the Rote Schloss — was a genuinely talented composer: violin from childhood, composition lessons with the town organist Walther (Bach's cousin and friend), and by his mid-teens concertos of real quality, six of which were later published and several of which **Bach thought worth transcribing** alongside his Vivaldi arrangements — the sincerest compliment in Bach's vocabulary.

His historical moment came at seventeen: returning in 1713 from studies in Utrecht, he brought home the newest Amsterdam concerto prints — Vivaldi's *L'estro armonico* above all — and set his teachers transcribing ([the era's hinge event](../events/weimar/vivaldi-transcriptions.md)).

He was already ill. Sent to Frankfurt for treatment in 1714, he died in August 1715, eighteen years old. The Weimar court's mourning silenced its music for months. He remains the timeline's most affecting minor figure: a composer who died before his own gift could ripen, remembered because his shopping ripened someone else's.
