Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy

1809 – 1847 · The reviver — conductor of the 1829 St. Matthew Passion

Grandson of the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, son of a Berlin banking family, baptized Lutheran at seven, and the most complete musical prodigy since Mozart — trained by Zelter in the strict counterpoint that most of Europe had shelved, and gifted at fourteen the manuscript score that changed music history.

His Bach work bookends everything else he did. At twenty, the 1829 revival — organized against his teacher's skepticism, conducted from memory. Then a lifetime of consolidation: organ recitals that returned the great preludes and fugues to public life (his 1840 Leipzig recital raised money for a Bach monument he ultimately financed himself — it still stands near the Thomaskirche); the Gewandhaus "historical concerts"; his own Bach-saturated composing (the organ sonatas, the oratorios' chorales); and the advocacy that seeded the Gesellschaft he didn't live to join.

The double position — the Jewish-descended reviver of Protestant Germany's musical scripture, inside a national movement that would later disown him (Wagner's poisonous essay appeared three years after his death; a later Germany tore down his statues while playing the Passion he revived) — is not a footnote to this timeline but one of its lessons: reception is never neutral, and the man who gave Bach back to the world knew it better than anyone in the hall.
