Lüneburg, Duchy of Brunswick-Lüneburg

1700–1702 (ages 15–17) · Choral scholar (Mettenchor) at the Michaelisschule

The town then. A once-mighty Hanseatic salt town in the north-German plain, two hundred miles from Bach's Thuringia — prosperous, Low-German-speaking, culturally oriented toward Hamburg thirty miles north. For a Thuringian teenager, effectively a foreign country.

St. Michael's. A former Benedictine convent turned Lutheran foundation, with a school that maintained two choirs — the select Mettenchor (matins choir) of about fifteen poor scholars, Bach among them, singing for their keep alongside the sons of nobility being educated at the attached Ritterakademie (knights' academy). The class line ran straight through the schoolyard: scholarship boys sang; young nobles learned French manners. Bach plausibly got his first exposure to both.

The musical map. In town: Georg Böhm at the Johanniskirche's great organ. Thirty miles north: Hamburg — Reincken at St. Catherine's, the opera, the organ builders. Fifty miles south: Celle, whose duke kept a French orchestra in the Versailles image. Bach worked all three edges of the triangle on foot.

What survives today. The Michaeliskirche stands (the school buildings are gone); the Johanniskirche preserves the organ case Böhm presided over. Lüneburg's old town survived the twentieth century intact — of all Bach's places, the one whose streets look most as he saw them.
