Dresden, Electoral Saxon capital

Visits from 1717 (Marchand) through the 1730s–40s (title, recitals, sons) · The glamorous capital — leverage, audience, and aspiration

The city then. Everything Leipzig wasn't: the Saxon Elector-Kings' residence — Catholic court (the rulers had converted for the Polish crown; the populace stayed Lutheran), Italian opera, and the best orchestra in Europe, whose salaried virtuosi Bach held up to his own council as the standard Leipzig refused to fund ("one need only go to Dresden," the memo says in effect, "to see what musicians paid to master their instruments sound like").

Bach's Dresden. The stage of the Marchand non-duel (1717); the court to which he addressed the 1733 Missa and the title campaign; the scene of celebrated organ recitals (the 1736 Frauenkirche Silbermann concert that publicly rang in his court title); his son Friedemann's first post (Sophienkirche, 1733); and a semi-regular escape — he reportedly joked about going to Dresden "to hear the pretty little songs" at the opera. Aspiration, leverage, and pleasure: the capital was all three.

What survives today. The Frauenkirche — destroyed in the 1945 firestorm, left as rubble through the DDR decades, rebuilt stone by stone (reconsecrated 2005) — with a new organ; the Hofkirche holds a great Silbermann. Dresden's double identity in Bach's story (splendor; the standard Leipzig wouldn't pay for) reads differently, and more movingly, against its later history.
