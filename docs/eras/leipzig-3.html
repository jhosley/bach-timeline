Leipzig III: the summing-up

1741–1750

The last decade turns inward and upward. The weekly cantata machine has long since built its repertory and runs on revivals; the Collegium fades after Zimmermann's death (1741); the institutional wars subside into cold coexistence (the council quietly auditioned a successor "in case" while Bach still lived — he surely knew). What fills the space is a project no employer assigned: the complete statement of his art, genre by genre, for no deadline but mortality's.

The sequence reads like a curriculum: the Goldberg Variations (1741) — variation raised to cosmology; the Well-Tempered Clavier Book II (assembled ~1742) — the 24-key journey made twice; the Musical Offering (1747) — a king's challenge answered with an encyclopedia of canon and fugue; the Canonic Variations (1747) — strict canon for his admission to Mizler's learned society (which also required the portrait: the Haussmann painting, the only certain likeness, shows him holding a canon — how he wished to be seen); the B minor Mass (completed 1748–49) — his life's sacred music gathered into one Latin summa; and The Art of Fugue — all of fugue, from one theme, left just short of finished.

Two events of these years supplied the legend's final scenes. The Potsdam visit (May 1747): the aging cantor at the candle-lit court of Frederick the Great — Europe's modern prince, flute under his arm, C.P.E. Bach at the harpsichord — improvising on the chromatic "royal theme" and going home to build the Musical Offering around it: old counterpoint bowing to new power, then quietly out-lasting it. And the end itself (1750): two operations by the itinerant English oculist John Taylor — the same "Chevalier" who later blinded Handel — leaving Bach sightless and broken; a stroke; death on 28 July 1750. The estate was inventoried and divided; the performing parts scattered among the heirs; Anna Magdalena was left to her decade of poverty. No monument marked the grave.

The obvious reading of the decade — the neglected master writing for eternity — is romantic, and the timeline flags it as interpretation. The sober version is stranger and better: a working craftsman who had answered every occasion for forty years spent his last years answering the discipline itself — as if counterpoint were the final patron, and the Endzweck of 1708 had widened past any church that could contain it. Either way, the shelf he left — Goldbergs, WTC II, Offering, Mass, Art of Fugue — is the densest concentration of musical thought one person has produced, and Arc II begins with almost nobody wanting it.

Study anchor: the Potsdam card and the B minor Mass card — the era's public legend and private summa, side by side.
