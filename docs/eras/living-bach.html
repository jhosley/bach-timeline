The Living Bach: 1850–present

1850–present

After the Gesellschaft, Bach's survival was assured; the remaining history — this arc — is the story of what each era needed him to be, and the timeline's ribbon here becomes a gallery of portraits, each true, none complete.

The Romantic Bach was a cathedral: massed choirs for the Passions, Liszt and Busoni's thunderous piano transcriptions, the Air renamed for a violin's G string, Gounod's Ave Maria floating over the first prelude — grandeur, sentiment, and the organist as mystic. Against it, around 1900, came the first correctives: Schweitzer reading the cantatas' pictorial language and demanding clearer, plainer performance; Casals pulling the cello suites out of the étude drawer; Landowska returning the harpsichord to the argument (her deathless quip to a pianist: "You play Bach your way, and I'll play him his way").

The twentieth century then split him productively in two. The modernists' Bach was pure structure: Webern orchestrating the six-voice ricercar as proto-serialism, Stravinsky arranging the Canonic Variations, and in 1955 a young Canadian's Goldberg recording making contrapuntal clarity itself a mass-market thrill. The historians' Bach — the historically informed performance movement — asked instead what the music sounded like in its own rooms: Harnoncourt and Leonhardt's period-instrument complete cantatas (1971–89) rebuilt the sound world; Rifkin's one-voice-per-part thesis pushed the question to its edge; and by century's end the movement's second generation had made "period Bach" simply normal Bach.

Which brings the arc to your requested modern voices. John Eliot Gardiner's Bach Cantata Pilgrimage (2000) performed all the surviving sacred cantatas on their own liturgical days, in churches across Europe, in one year — the cantata machine re-experienced as a lived calendar — and his Music in the Castle of Heaven (2013) gave this timeline much of its human Bach. Masaaki Suzuki's Bach Collegium Japan spent eighteen years recording every sacred cantata — Lutheran Leipzig's weekly bread, perfected in Kobe: the arc's best proof that this music's citizenship is now planetary. Koopman, Herreweghe, the Netherlands Bach Society's free-to-all All of Bach film archive — completeness, once a scholars' dream, is now a listener's starting point.

And one object extends the arc past the planet: in 1977, the Voyager Golden Record left Earth carrying three Bach works — more than any other composer — including the Brandenburg No. 2 as track one. The biologist Lewis Thomas, asked what humanity should send, gave the arc its epigraph: "I would vote for Bach, all of Bach... we would be bragging, but it is surely excusable." The ethereal streams of this timeline's design — music flowing into the forever — are, since 1977, not a metaphor.
