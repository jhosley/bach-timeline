Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

1717–1723

For six years, Bach had the job he later said he intended to keep for the rest of his life — and for once, the reason wasn't piety or duty. It was that his employer was a musician.

Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Cöthen was twenty-three when Bach arrived in December 1717: a ruler of a tiny principality who played violin, viola da gamba, and harpsichord, sang a respectable bass, and had spent his grand tour buying music in Italy. When the King of Prussia dissolved his own court orchestra in 1713, Leopold hired its best players — so this minor court, in a town of a few thousand people, suddenly possessed one of the finest small ensembles in Germany. Bach came in as Kapellmeister — director of the prince's music, the most prestigious title he would ever hold — at roughly double his Weimar salary, second-highest paid official at court.

One fact shaped everything he wrote here: Cöthen was Calvinist. Reformed worship wanted no elaborate church music — no cantatas, no concerted Masses, just plain psalm-singing. For the first and only time in his life, Bach's job had almost nothing to do with church. The result was an explosion of instrumental music that has no parallel in his career: the six Brandenburg Concertos, the six cello suites, the six sonatas and partitas for unaccompanied violin, the violin concertos, the sonatas for violin and harpsichord — and, because his own household was filling with children and students, the great teaching books: the Clavierbüchlein for nine-year-old Wilhelm Friedemann, the Inventions and Sinfonias, and the first book of the Well-Tempered Clavier.

The pattern to notice, as a student: Cöthen is where Bach thinks in sets of six. Six Brandenburgs, six cello suites, six violin solos, six sonatas with harpsichord, six French suites. Each set is an encyclopedia — a deliberate, complete survey of what a genre can do — and that encyclopedic instinct is the same one that later produces the Well-Tempered Clavier's tour of all twenty-four keys and, at the very end of his life, The Art of Fugue. The habit of mind starts here, where he had the freedom to follow it.

The idyll broke twice. In July 1720, returning from a spa trip to Carlsbad with the prince, Bach learned that his wife Maria Barbara — mother of his seven children, four living — had died suddenly and was already buried. He was thirty-five. Seventeen months later he married Anna Magdalena Wilcke, a gifted twenty-year-old court soprano who became his copyist, musical partner, and mother of thirteen more children. And in December 1721 — one week after Bach's wedding — Leopold married a cousin whom Bach later described, in a rare unguarded letter, as an amusa: a woman indifferent to the muses. The prince's attention drifted from his orchestra, the musical budget tightened, and Bach began looking for the exit.

The exit was Leipzig — a step down in title (from court Kapellmeister to town cantor, a fact his friends remarked on and Bach himself felt) but a step toward what he now wanted: a university city for his sons' education, and a return to the sacred music that Cöthen could not use. He auditioned in February 1723, was chosen after two more famous candidates fell through, and moved his family in May. Leopold released him graciously; the affection was real on both sides. Bach kept his Cöthen title, and when Leopold died young in 1728, Bach returned one last time to perform funeral music for the prince who had loved and known music — built, fittingly, on movements from the St. Matthew Passion.

Study anchor: if you are working on any Bach instrumental piece, odds are its roots are in this room. Start with the Sei Solo and the Brandenburgs, and read the Capelle event to understand the specific players he was writing for — much of this music is portraiture of an orchestra he knew by name.
