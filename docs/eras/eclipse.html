The Eclipse: 1750–1829

1750–1829

The timeline's ribbon dims here, and the design is honest: for roughly eighty years after his death, Johann Sebastian Bach was not a famous composer. He was a famous organist, dead; a pedagogue, useful; and, above all, a father — because through the second half of the eighteenth century, "the great Bach" meant Carl Philipp Emanuel, and "Bach" without qualification might mean Emanuel, or the fashionable "London Bach" Johann Christian, before anyone thought of old Sebastian at all. The sons the timeline watched being born in the Weimar nursery genuinely eclipsed him — Emanuel shaping the emerging classical style that Haydn and Mozart built on, Christian charming London (and personally influencing the eight-year-old Mozart). The era's taste had ruled, and it ruled for the sons: clarity, feeling, one melody at a time. Scheibe had merely said early what the century concluded.

The physical legacy fared worse than the reputation. The estate divided, the manuscripts scattered with the heirs: Emanuel, a careful curator, preserved his share (the Passions, the Mass); Friedemann — brilliant, unstable, increasingly poor — sold his off piecemeal, and much of what this timeline mourns as lost (cantata cycles, Passions) vanished through his hands. Anna Magdalena's decade of poverty ended in an alms-grave in 1760; the Art of Fugue's plates were nearly melted for copper. The vocal music — the weekly miracles of the cantata machine — went silent almost totally: unpublished, liturgically obsolete, textually Lutheran in an Enlightenment century. What survived in use was exactly what this timeline flagged along the way: the motets, sung continuously at the Thomasschule; the chorales, collected as textbooks; the keyboard works Bach himself had published; and the WTC.

Because here is the eclipse's great secret: it was never total, and where it wasn't, it glowed. The Well-Tempered Clavier circulated for eighty years in manuscript copy — professional to student, hand to hand, a samizdat classic — and its copyists were the whole future: the young Beethoven made his Bonn reputation playing it; Mozart, ambushed by the counterpoint at van Swieten's Vienna salon and then by a motet in Leipzig itself, spent his last years folding Bach's polyphony into his own late style; Haydn owned the scores; the Berlin school of Bach's own students (Kirnberger, Agricola) codified his teaching into the theory books the next century learned from. The eclipse, examined closely, is a relay: the public forgot; the professionals never did. Every composer who built the classical style had Bach's counterpoint somewhere in the basement — which is why, when the revival came, it felt less like discovery than like the basement being opened.

The era ends with its own instruments of return: Forkel's 1802 biography — the first, written with the sons' help and an openly nationalist thesis (this music as German patrimony); the swelling Romantic appetite for the old, the deep, the Gothic; and, in Berlin, a choral society quietly rehearsing from a copyist's score of a forgotten Passion. The ribbon is about to reignite — one hundred years, almost to the season, after the music first sounded.

Study anchor: the Mozart motet card — one documented scene that contains the whole arc: the era's greatest living composer, ambushed by the dead cantor's counterpoint, demanding the parts.
