Lüneburg: the northern education

Singing for his bread: the Mettenchor scholarship

1700-04-03 · lueneburg
documented

By April 1700 the account books of St. Michael's list the two Thuringian arrivals — Erdmann and Bach — receiving their Mettenchor allowances: free tuition and board plus small cash fees, in exchange for singing matins and the church's liturgy. The Mettenchor took roughly fifteen boys, selected for voice and poverty alike; it was the standard machine by which Lutheran Germany converted musical talent into education for boys with neither family nor money — the same machine, note, that Leipzig's Thomasschule ran, whose boarders Bach would later command as cantor. He spent his adolescence inside the institution he would spend his last twenty-seven years directing; when he argues with Leipzig about choir conditions in 1730, he is describing a life he lived.

The scholarship's premise failed fast: his soprano broke soon after arrival (the Obituary says he briefly spoke and sang in octaves at once, then lost the treble entirely). The school kept him anyway — as violinist, as keyboard accompanist, possibly as prefect drilling the younger boys. The lesson he could not have missed: the voice was rented, but instrumental skill was capital.

Why it matters. Positions-track context at its clearest: Bach's entire secondary education was a job — meals contingent on performance, employment continuous from age ten. When later cards show him bargaining ferociously over salary, fees, and titles, remember that music and livelihood were fused for him from childhood; he never had the luxury of learning them separately.
