The Living Bach: 1850–present

Schweitzer's Bach: the tone-poet of the chorales

1905 · strasbourg
documented

Before Lambaréné and the Nobel Peace Prize, Albert Schweitzer was an Alsatian organist-theologian whose 1905 Bach study (French first, then the expanded German of 1908) became the twentieth century's founding Bach book. Its thesis: Bach is a tone-poet — his music built from a consistent pictorial-symbolic vocabulary, legible above all in the chorale preludes, where one figure per piece depicts the hymn's idea: falling motives for the Fall, wave-figures for water, the "grief" chromatics, the "joy" rhythms. Read the figures, Schweitzer argued, and the "abstract" instrumental Bach dissolves into a preacher with motifs for words.

The scholarship has aged the way strong theses do: the core observation is period-true — the figures are a rhetoric, and Schweitzer taught generations to hear them — while the system overreaches (not every third is a Trinity; this timeline's layer-model exists partly to keep his insight without his excess). His practical legacy aged better: as a leading voice of organ reform, he campaigned against the orchestral monster-organs of his day and for the clear, singing instruments Bach knew — the first major turn of the Romantic portrait toward what would become the historical movement. His own recordings survive: slow by modern taste, and moving precisely for their preacher's deliberateness.

Why it matters. Arc III's first interpretive revolution — Bach re-read rather than re-arranged — and a biographical emblem the timeline can't resist: the century's great humanitarian formed himself on the Orgelbüchlein's theology of figures, then lived it. Reception history occasionally produces saints.
