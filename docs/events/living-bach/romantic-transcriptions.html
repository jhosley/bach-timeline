The Romantic Bach: transcription as devotion

1871 · europe
documented

The Romantic century loved Bach the way it loved cathedrals — and renovated him accordingly. Its instrument of devotion was the transcription: Liszt carried the great organ preludes and fugues onto the concert piano; Busoni's version of the Chaconne turned one violin's implied polyphony into full-orchestral pianism (still a repertoire staple, and still an argument: is it revelation or replacement?); the violinist Wilhelmj dropped the Air onto the violin's lowest string and accidentally renamed it; Gounod floated an Ave Maria over the C-major prelude; Stokowski's orchestral Toccata and Fugue waited at the century's far end. Meanwhile the Passions swelled to festival choirs of hundreds, and organists cultivated the "Bach style" as sacred thunder.

It is easy — the twentieth century found it very easy — to catalogue the distortions: scale inflated, textures thickened, dance-feet buried under velvet. The fairer reading, which this timeline takes: transcription was how a century without recordings circulated music it loved — every arrangement a delivery vehicle, and the distortions were the postage. Brahms studying the Gesellschaft volumes and writing a left-hand Chaconne study "to feel what Bach felt"; Liszt bringing organ counterpoint to audiences a thousand miles from a great organ — this is reception as metabolism, not vandalism.

Why it matters. First portrait in Arc III's gallery, and the baseline the twentieth century's historians and modernists each rebelled against — in opposite directions. When you compare a 1900s-style Matthew Passion with Gardiner's, you are hearing this card argued with.
