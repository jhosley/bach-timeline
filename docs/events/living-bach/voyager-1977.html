1977: Bach leaves the solar system

1977-08-20 · cape-canaveral
documented

When Carl Sagan's committee assembled the Voyager Golden Record — the gold-plated disc of Earth's sounds and music launched aboard both Voyager probes in 1977, built to survive a billion years of interstellar travel — they gave Bach three of the twenty-seven music tracks, more than any other composer: the first movement of the Second Brandenburg Concerto (Karl Richter's recording) as the record's opening music; the Gavotte en rondeaux from the E-major violin Partita (Arthur Grumiaux); and the Prelude and Fugue in C from WTC Book II — played by Glenn Gould: the 1955 revolution, aboard.

The era's best joke about it belongs to the biologist Lewis Thomas, asked what humanity should send to the stars: "I would vote for Bach, all of Bach, streamed out into space, over and over again. We would be bragging, of course, but it is surely excusable for us to put the best possible face on at the beginning of such an acquaintance." The committee, in effect, agreed — and hedged only on quantity.

Both probes have since crossed into interstellar space: the discs — carrying a court entertainment for a margrave who never played it, a violin piece from the widower year, and a prelude from the old cantor's teaching book — are the most distant human-made objects in existence, outbound forever.

Why it matters. This timeline's design brief — the ethereal, never-ending streams flowing from Bach's creations into the forever — stopped being a metaphor on 20 August 1977. The app should end its ribbon here, literally: the music, leaving.
