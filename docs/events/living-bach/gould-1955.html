The Living Bach: 1850–present

June 1955: Gould records the Goldbergs

1955-06-01 · new-york
documented

Columbia's producers thought the repertoire choice was commercial suicide: an unknown twenty-two-year-old Canadian insisting his debut LP be the Goldberg Variations — in 1955 a connoisseur's curiosity, "harpsichord music," associated with Landowska's grand priestess manner. Glenn Gould arrived at the East 30th Street studio in June with his sawed-down chair and his humming, and recorded the performance that changed Bach's twentieth-century address: fast, feather-light, staccato-etched — every contrapuntal line audible as a separate living thing — the canons not solemn but exhilarating. The record became a genuine bestseller, and a generation discovered that counterpoint could hit like jazz.

Gould's whole career then ran on Bach (the WTC, the partitas, his beloved II/22) and on a modernist creed: concert halls were obsolete; the recording was the artwork — splices, close mics, and all. He quit the stage at thirty-one and made the studio his instrument, a decision that looks more prophetic every streaming decade. Then the frame closed with uncanny symmetry: in 1981 he re-recorded the Goldbergs — slower, inward, x-rayed — and died months after its release, at fifty. The two recordings, twenty-six years apart, are the modern era's own two-books-of-the-WTC: one artist, one work, youth and late style, permanently comparable.

Why it matters. The modernists' Bach at maximum reach — structure as ecstasy, no cathedral required — and the single recording most likely to have brought any living listener (possibly you) to this timeline's composer. Track one of the case that Bach is contemporary music.
