The Living Bach: 1850–present

The HIP revolution: Harnoncourt, Leonhardt, and the complete cantatas

1971 – 1989 · vienna
documented

Historically informed performance (HIP) asks a deceptively simple question: what did this music sound like in its own rooms — its instruments, pitches, tempos, forces, bows? The movement's founding monument is the project Nikolaus Harnoncourt (Vienna) and Gustav Leonhardt (Amsterdam) began in 1971: recording every sacred cantata — eighteen years, forty-five volumes — on period instruments, with boys' choirs and boy trebles where Bach's own forces would have had them. Early volumes sound effortful even now — valveless horns cracking, trebles straining — and that was the point: the sound of the music being difficult again, the cantata machine's weekly labor made audible after two centuries of upholstery.

The revolution won so completely that its victory is invisible: dance tempos, transparent textures, singing bows — the step-feel this timeline's craft essays assume — are simply how Bach is played now, on modern or old instruments. And the movement kept radicalizing itself: in 1981 the American scholar Joshua Rifkin argued from the surviving performing parts that Bach's normal "choir" was one singer per part — the great choruses as vocal chamber music — igniting the era's best scholarly brawl (Parrott's The Essential Bach Choir makes the case; the counter-case holds the podium elsewhere; recordings now exist both ways, and the debate is genuinely unresolved — this timeline flags it disputed and recommends hearing a one-per-part motet before taking sides).

Why it matters. Every recording this timeline recommends — Gardiner, Suzuki, Herreweghe, Koopman — is this event's direct descendant. HIP is Arc III's Gesellschaft: where the nineteenth century restored the texts, the twentieth restored the sound — and, like all restorations on this timeline, it is an interpretation wearing the authority of the past. Which is exactly why it stays flagged, lovingly, as one more portrait in the gallery.
