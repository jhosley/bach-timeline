The Living Bach: 1850–present

The completeness era: Suzuki, Koopman, and All of Bach

1995 – 2025 · worldwide
documented

Every other card on this arc has an evening attached — a session in a New York studio, a launch from a Florida pad, a year of pilgrimage with a fixed beginning and end. The arc's final revolution has none. It happened gradually, across the last generation, and its content can be stated in three words: completeness became normal. There was no announcement, because the change was distributed across dozens of projects and two decades; there was only, at some point, the quiet fact that a listener could hear everything — every cantata, every keyboard suite, every fragment — performed beautifully, and increasingly for free. No previous century of this music's history, including Bach's own, could have imagined the condition.

The era's emblematic project comes from Kobe. Masaaki Suzuki and his Bach Collegium Japan recorded every sacred cantata for BIS across eighteen years, 1995 to 2013, with the secular works following — a cycle polished and luminous on its own terms, and profoundly moving as a fact before a note is heard. Leipzig's weekly Lutheran bread, perfected in Japan, by a conductor for whom this music's theology is personal rather than inherited: if the arc needed a single exhibit that Bach's citizenship is now planetary — that this music has slipped every boundary of nation, confession, and culture that defined its birth — the Suzuki edition is that exhibit. Around it, the completeness projects multiplied: Ton Koopman's Amsterdam cycle ran in parallel; Herreweghe deepened the choral core with recordings of extraordinary inwardness; and every one of them stands, explicitly, on the 1971 revolution's shoulders — the sound world Harnoncourt and Leonhardt rebuilt is the sound world these cycles refined.

Then the internet finished the thought. In 2013 the Netherlands Bach Society — the world's oldest Bach ensemble — began All of Bach: every work, filmed in performance, released free online, the bibliography's standing recommendation for watching this music rather than merely hearing it. It is worth seeing this as the completion of the Gesellschaft's logic, begun in 1850: first every note collected and printed, for scholars who could afford the volumes; now every work performed and streamed, for anyone with a connection. Consider the gradient this closes. A Leipzig burgher heard a given cantata once, on its Sunday, and in all likelihood never again. Mozart, wanting to study a motet, had to spread the manuscript parts on his knees because no other access existed. Today any student anywhere summons three rival performances of anything before breakfast. The three-century story of custody that this timeline has traced — the estate scattered among heirs and dealers, the texts collected by the century of editors, the sound rebuilt by the century of performers — ends here, in universal access. The music that once survived by a diplomat's luggage and a widow's sales now cannot be lost by anything short of civilizational collapse.

And yet the era's gift carries the era's problem, and honesty about it is the right way to close the arc. This is the era you study in, and its abundance is a genuinely new kind of difficulty. Scarcity organized every earlier listener's attention automatically: you heard what your city performed, studied what your library held, and the limits did the curating. When everything is available at once, nothing is sequenced; the two hundred cantatas arrive as an undifferentiated wall, and the beginner drowns precisely where the burgher starved. When everything is available, the scarce resources are context and sequence — which is, frankly, why this timeline exists. The eras, cards, and craft essays here are one answer to the completeness era's problem: an order to encounter things in, a reason to care about each thing when you reach it. The completeness era supplies the recordings; this site supplies a path through them; your practice — listening, comparing, returning — supplies the rest.
