The Living Bach: 1850–present

The completeness era: Suzuki, Koopman, and All of Bach

1995 – 2025 · worldwide
documented

The arc's quiet final revolution has no single evening attached: sometime in the last generation, completeness became normal. Masaaki Suzuki and his Kobe-founded Bach Collegium Japan recorded every sacred cantata for BIS across eighteen years (1995–2013, with the secular works following) — polished, luminous, and profoundly moving as a fact: Leipzig's weekly Lutheran bread, perfected in Japan by a conductor for whom this music's theology is personal, the definitive proof that Bach's citizenship is now planetary. Ton Koopman's Amsterdam cycle ran in parallel; Herreweghe deepened the choral core; and every one of them stands on the 1971 revolution's shoulders.

Then the internet completed the Gesellschaft's logic: the Netherlands Bach Society — the world's oldest Bach ensemble — began All of Bach in 2013: every work, filmed in performance, released free online (the bibliography's standing recommendation for watching this music). Where a Leipzig burgher heard a cantata once and never again, and Mozart had to spread the parts on his knees, any student anywhere now summons three rival performances of anything before breakfast. Custody's three-century story — scattered, collected, sounded — ends in universal access.

Why it matters. This is the era you study in, and its abundance is a new kind of problem — which is, frankly, why this timeline exists: when everything is available, the scarce resources are context and sequence. The eras, cards, and craft essays here are one answer; the completeness era supplies the recordings; your practice supplies the rest.
