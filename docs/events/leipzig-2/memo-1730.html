Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

'Incorrigible': the council's rebuke and the great memorandum

1730-08-23 · leipzig
documented

In August 1730 the council's frustrations boiled into the minutes: the cantor "does nothing," shirked his teaching, left town without leave; he was — the burgomaster's word — "incorrigible"; income was voted withheld. Seven years of jurisdictional friction, from both sides' perspectives, in one ugly session.

Bach's answer, dated 23 August, is the era's central document: the Short but Most Necessary Draft for a Well-Appointed Church Music — his Endzweck of 1708 translated into an auditor's tables. Proper concerted music requires, he itemizes, at minimum twelve to sixteen singers (three to four per part, four choirs' worth for four churches) and eighteen to twenty instrumentalists; against this he lists his actual forces by name and competence — a handful able, the rest middling or unfit, the town's professional instrumentalists aging and undermanned ("modesty forbids me to speak... of their quality"). The cause is economic, and he says so with a sociologist's clarity: musicians must live; Dresden's are paid to master their instruments; Leipzig's scramble for bread. The memo received, as far as the record shows, no reply.

Two months later, to Erdmann, the private sequel: the post was less lucrative than promised, living costly, the authorities "odd and little interested in music"; he lived amid "almost constant vexation, envy, and persecution" — would Danzig have anything?

Why it matters. For working conditions — a core theme of this whole app — no document in the century says more: the greatest music director alive, itemizing to his employer why excellence requires funding, and being ignored. Every subsequent institutional battle (Dresden title, prefects) is this memo continued by other means.
