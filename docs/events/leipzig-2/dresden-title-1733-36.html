The Dresden gambit: a Missa for a title

1733-07-27 – 1736-11-19 · dresden
documented

Elector Augustus II ("the Strong") died in February 1733; the succession brought months of official mourning — no figural church music, the cantata machine's only extended silence — and Bach used the pause like a strategist. On 27 July 1733 he presented to the new Elector, Augustus III, in Dresden a set of beautifully prepared performing parts: a Missa — Kyrie and Gloria of what the world now knows as the B minor Mass, music of staggering scale and Dresden-tailored brilliance (the court was Catholic; a Latin Missa served both confessions) — attached to a petition, whose language repays study, begging the royal protection of a court title against the "one insult or another" he endured in Leipzig.

The logic was armor, not vanity: a Hof-Compositeur answered, in dignity, to a king — local councils and rectors harass such a man at their peril. (The Cöthen and Weissenfels titles he already held were expiring assets of small courts.) The pursuit took three years, renewed petitions, and probably the advocacy of Count Keyserlingk, the Russian envoy in Dresden (whose name returns with the Goldbergs). The decree arrived 19 November 1736: Royal Polish and Electoral Saxon Court Composer. Two weeks later Bach gave a celebrated two-hour recital on the new Silbermann organ of Dresden's Frauenkirche — the title sung in, publicly.

Around this run of years, family strategy pointed the same direction: Friedemann installed as organist of Dresden's Sophienkirche (1733); the sons launched toward court careers their father never had.

Why it matters. Politics and posterity in one act: the title did its local work (see the prefect war's endgame) — and the Missa written to win it became, fifteen years later, the seed crystal of the final summa. Bach's shrewdest career maneuver and his greatest work share a birth certificate.
