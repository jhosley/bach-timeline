Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Scheibe's attack: 'turgid' — the future files its complaint

1737-05-14 · leipzig
documented

In May 1737 Johann Adolph Scheibe — twenty-nine, Hamburg-based critic (and son of the Leipzig organ builder whose university organ Bach had examined in 1717; he had also once failed an audition Bach judged) — published, anonymously, the most consequential review of the century. The greatest keyboard master alive, he wrote, "would be the admiration of whole nations" if he had more agreeableness and less bombast (Schwulst — turgidity): he "darkened the beauty of his works by an excess of art"; wrote out every ornament, smothering melody; demanded that singers and players "do with their throats and instruments whatever he can play on the clavier" — an unnatural, laborious style whose effort "is vain, since it conflicts with Nature."

The defense arrived in January 1738 over the name of Johann Abraham Birnbaum, a Leipzig rhetoric lecturer — its inside knowledge betraying Bach's collaboration: art does not oppose nature but completes it; the fullest harmony is nature raised to its perfection; the "excess" is the music's substance. The pamphlets volleyed for years; partisans (Mizler's learned society among them) chose sides.

Read past the insults and the exchange is the eighteenth century's aesthetic fault line in miniature: the galant generation — melody, ease, sentiment, the listener sovereign — against learned counterpoint's claim that musical truth is structural. Scheibe spoke for the future's taste; time reversed only the verdict, not the description — what he called turgid, posterity calls inexhaustible.

Why it matters. The essential context for late Bach: after 1737 the encyclopedic works (Clavier-Übung III, the Goldbergs, Art of Fugue) read as conscious answers — counterpoint's case stated for a posterity he could not know would come. Arc II opens with his sons half-agreeing with Scheibe; Arc III ends with nobody remembering Scheibe for anything else.
