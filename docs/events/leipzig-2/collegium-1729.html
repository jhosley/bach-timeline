Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Director of the Collegium Musicum: Bach goes public

1729-03-01 · leipzig
documented

In March 1729 Bach assumed direction of the Collegium Musicum, the concert society of university students and town professionals Telemann had founded in 1702 — an ensemble of forty-odd capable players giving weekly public concerts: Friday evenings at Zimmermann's coffee house in winter, Wednesday afternoons in the coffee garden in summer, extra series during the trade fairs. No church, no court: admission and coffee — the bourgeois public concert, decades before "concert hall" existed as a building type in Germany.

Why would the overworked cantor volunteer? Every reading fits: an ensemble worth directing (compare the 1730 memo's inventory of his church forces); repertoire freedom; extra income; his university-age sons needing a stage; and — after the council's treatment — an audience that simply wanted him. For a decade (1729–37, and again 1739–41) this was where Bach's non-liturgical energy lived.

The repertoire consequence was epochal almost by accident. Needing weekly material with himself, Friedemann, and Emanuel at the keyboards, Bach rebuilt his Cöthen and Weimar concertos as harpsichord concertos (BWV 1052–1065, including multi-keyboard spectaculars) — effectively founding the public keyboard-concerto genre that runs through Mozart to every piano concerto since; Brandenburg No. 5 had planted the seed. Around it: orchestral suites, chamber music, guest virtuosi, and Picander's comic secular cantatas — above all the Coffee Cantata (c. 1734), a miniature opera about a daughter's caffeine addiction, performed in the coffee house — Bach's sense of humor, documented.

Why it matters. The era's proof that Bach's art was never church-bound by temperament — give him a public and he entertained it brilliantly. And a preview of music's coming economy: the court and church models that employed him all his life were already yielding to the ticket-buying public that would, a century on, resurrect him.
