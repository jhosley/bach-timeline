Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Christmas 1734: the Oratorio, and the parody workshop at full power

1734-12-25 – 1735-01-06 · leipzig
documented

Across the Christmas season of 1734–35, on the six feast days that run from Christmas morning to Epiphany, the congregations of Leipzig heard something new arrive in installments: a unified, six-part Christmas Oratorio. Each part was, outwardly, a cantata like any other Sunday's — but a single Gospel narrative threaded through all six, carried by an Evangelist, so that the season itself became the performance and the church calendar the work's true medium. Trumpets blaze at the corners of the design; at its center sit the cradle songs, Schlaft wohl among them the tenderest lullaby Bach ever wrote. A listener who attended all six feasts heard one work, told across twelve days, in the only concert hall Leipzig's sacred music possessed: time.

The engine room of this beloved work is the timeline's best classroom for parody technique — the craft of re-texting existing music, which Bach had practiced since the Cöthen serenatas. The majority of the Oratorio's great choruses and arias were not newly composed. They were re-texted from secular congratulatory cantatas written in the previous two years for the Saxon royal family — the same Dresden court Bach was courting for his title in exactly these years. Tönet, ihr Pauken! — "Sound, ye drums!," written for the queen's birthday — became Jauchzet, frohlocket, "Shout, rejoice!," for Christmas morning, its timpani fanfares now announcing another kind of majesty entirely. Occasional music for mortal princes, harvested for the eternal one.

To modern instincts this can look like corner-cutting, and the parody question has hung over the Oratorio like a mild scandal ever since it was noticed. The evidence points the other way, and the point matters for understanding how Bach thought. Scholars believe the transfer was planned from the start: the court works were written with their sacred afterlife in mind — first-class music deliberately parked in short-lived occasions, a birthday here, a coronation there, awaiting its true and permanent text. A royal birthday ode would be performed once and shelved; a Christmas oratorio would return every year. Bach, who never wasted anything, appears to have used the court commissions as a proving ground for music he had already destined for the manger. Recycling was not fatigue; it was architecture in time — composition conducted across occasions, with the church year as the final draft.

For the student of craft, the Oratorio offers something almost no other masterpiece does: both versions survive. Set the queen's birthday music beside the Christmas music and you can watch the composer's hands at work in the space between — declamation re-fitted syllable by syllable to the new words, affect re-aimed from courtly homage to sacred jubilation, instrumentation retouched where the new frame demanded it. Just as instructive is what he does not change. The parts he carries over untouched are the parts he evidently considered a movement's substance — its structure, its contrapuntal life — while the parts he re-tailors are its clothing. No treatise Bach never wrote could tell us more precisely where, in his own judgment, the essence of a piece of music resided. The R3 card will map every borrowing, movement by movement.

There is a final turn to the story, and it doubles the lesson. This most festive and now most-beloved of Bach's large works — the one piece of his sacred music many households meet every December — vanished after his death along with the rest of the vocal music, which stayed in manuscript while the keyboard works went to press. It slept for over a century, and was first revived, fittingly for a work built on returns, in 1857 Berlin, deep into the revival era. The work that argued music could be written for permanence and merely staged in the occasional had to wait for history to agree. It did; the Oratorio's afterlife is now the longest-running annual performance tradition Bach owns, and the queen's drums still sound every Christmas morning, announcing the majesty they were always meant for.
