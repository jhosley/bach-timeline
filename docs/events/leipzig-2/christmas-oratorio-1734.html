Christmas 1734: the Oratorio, and the parody workshop at full power

1734-12-25 – 1735-01-06 · leipzig
documented

Across the Christmas season of 1734–35 — six feast days from Christmas Day to Epiphany — Leipzig heard a unified six-part Christmas Oratorio: one Gospel narrative threaded by an Evangelist across six cantatas, trumpets blazing at the corners, the cradle songs at the center (Schlaft wohl the tenderest lullaby Bach wrote).

Its engine room is the timeline's best classroom for parody technique (introduced at Cöthen): the majority of the great choruses and arias were re-texted from secular congratulatory cantatas written in the previous two years for the Saxon royal family — Tönet, ihr Pauken! ("Sound, ye drums!") for the queen's birthday becoming Jauchzet, frohlocket ("Shout, rejoice!") for Christmas morning, its timpani fanfares now announcing another kind of majesty. Occasional music for mortal princes, harvested for the eternal one — planned as such, scholars believe, from the start: the court works were written with their sacred afterlife in mind, first-class music parked in short-lived occasions awaiting its true text.

For the study of Bach's craft, comparing the paired versions (both survive) is uniquely instructive: watch what he adjusts — declamation re-fitted syllable by syllable, affect re-aimed, instrumentation retouched — and what he doesn't, and you learn precisely what he considered a movement's substance versus its clothing.

Why it matters. The definitive answer to the parody "scandal": recycling was not fatigue but architecture in time — and the Oratorio's afterlife doubles the lesson, since this most-beloved of Bach's festive works spent a century forgotten with the rest of the vocal music (first revived, fittingly, in 1857 Berlin).
