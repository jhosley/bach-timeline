Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

The 250-mile walk: Buxtehude and the four-month leave

1705-10-15 – 1706-02-01 · luebeck
documented

In October 1705 the organist of Arnstadt's Neue Kirche requested four weeks' leave, installed a cousin at his organ, and started walking. His destination lay roughly 250 miles north: Lübeck, the Marienkirche, and Dieterich Buxtehude — the greatest living master of the northern organ school, then nearly seventy, the presiding wizard of a tradition Bach had tasted in Lüneburg and now wanted at the source. He went on foot, the length of Germany, to hear one man play.

The timing was not romantic accident but calculation. Advent brought Buxtehude's famous Abendmusiken — "evening musics," grand public concerts of sacred music with orchestra, financed by Lübeck's business community — the nearest thing Germany possessed to a civic concert institution: large-scale sacred art mounted for a whole town, outside the liturgy, on commercial money. And 1705 was an exceptional year even by Abendmusik standards. Emperor Leopold I had died, and Buxtehude was mounting extraordinary memorial and accession programs of unusual splendor. A twenty-year-old walking north that autumn was walking toward the most ambitious sacred-music spectacle the German world had to offer, timed to the season when it blazed brightest.

He had four weeks of permission. He stayed four months — through Advent, through Christmas, through the great performances — and returned to Arnstadt around February 1706 to face the consistory. Asked to account for the absence, he offered what may be the least apologetic justification in the annals of employment: he had gone, in the Obituary's phrasing of what the minutes paraphrase, "in order to comprehend one thing and another about his art." One thing and another. Four months. No regret is recorded anywhere in the file, and none should be inferred; the man the documents show us simply did not believe an explanation was owed beyond the true one.

One tantalizing sidelight hangs over the visit, and honesty requires flagging its status. Buxtehude was old and seeking a successor, and the Marienkirche post carried a customary condition: marriage to the incumbent's daughter. Handel and Mattheson had visited together in 1703 and famously declined the package. Scholars suspect the option was at least visible to Bach during four months in the great man's orbit — but this is inference, and the card flags it as such; the sources are silent on any offer, any consideration, any daughter at all. All we know is that he walked home to his Arnstadt trouble instead.

What did the truancy buy? Almost everything, is the honest answer. The Lübeck journey is the purest expression of the pattern that defines Bach's entire self-education: when he wanted knowledge, he walked to the source and overstayed — the moonlight copying, the Lüneburg excursions, and now the longest walk of all. And it was the most consequential instance of the pattern. The northern stylus phantasticus — the free, dramatic, quasi-improvisatory manner of which Buxtehude was the supreme master — flows at full voltage straight into the great early organ works; the era essay calls this pilgrimage the source of the organ music's northern fire, and the single most consequential act of self-education in Bach's life. Beyond the style, there was the institutional vision: the Abendmusik model — sacred music on the grandest scale, addressed to an entire town, organized and funded as a civic undertaking — arguably seeded the ambition that would one day become the Passions. The young man who stood in the Marienkirche through those memorial programs had seen what "a well-regulated church music" could look like when a city actually paid for it, and the sentence he would write to Mühlhausen's council three years later reads differently once you know what he had witnessed here.

Arnstadt saw only the empty organ bench and the cousin substituting at it. The reckoning that followed belongs to the next card. But the timeline's verdict on the episode is not in suspense: four weeks of permission became four months of formation, and posterity sides with the truant.
