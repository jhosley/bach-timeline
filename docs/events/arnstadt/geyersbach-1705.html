Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

The 'Zippel Fagottist' affair: swords in the market square

1705-08-04 · arnstadt
documented

On the night of 4 August 1705, the organist of Arnstadt's Neue Kirche was crossing the market square when six men came at him out of the dark. Five were companions; the sixth, carrying a stick, was Johann Heinrich Geyersbach — a student six years Bach's senior who played bassoon in the church's student ensemble, and who had a question he had evidently been nursing for a while. Why had Bach insulted him? Had he not called him a Zippel Fagottist — a "greenhorn bassoonist," and the German is if anything ruder than that? Geyersbach struck Bach in the face. Bach drew his sword. The two grappled, blade and stick, in the middle of the town's public square, until the companions pulled them apart.

We know all of this in remarkable detail because the consistory investigated, and its minutes preserve everything — including Bach's own testimony, which is the document's real treasure. He admitted the remark. His defense, as the record's careful phrasing has it, was that he had "insulted the bassoon," not the man — he had merely observed that the sounds Geyersbach produced on the instrument were unfit for polite description, and if the player chose to take that personally, the fault lay with the playing. The consistory's finding is a small masterpiece of institutional even-handedness: Geyersbach was certainly wrong to assault him, but Bach "might very well have refrained from calling him a Zippel Fagottist," since "such gibes lead in the end to unpleasantness." And then the committee added, pointedly, a broader observation: the organist did not get along well with the students generally.

Two details in the scene repay attention before the moralizing starts. The first is the sword. Bach was not carrying a weapon out of bravado; organists ranked as gentlemen-professionals, and a gentleman wore one. The twenty-year-old whom the town had hired at a salary above his senior colleagues' walked its streets as a member of the professional class, and when struck, he responded as that class was entitled to respond. The scuffle is, among other things, a status document. The second detail is what the insult was actually about. Bach had not mocked Geyersbach's family, his person, or his prospects; he had mocked the sound of his playing. Bad execution physically offended him — this is the first documented appearance of a trait that resurfaces in every era of his life, the flat intolerance for mediocre performance that no amount of prudence ever taught him to conceal. The famous 1730 Leipzig memorandum on the incompetence of the musicians provided to him is this market-square scene grown up, aged twenty-five years, and gone to paperwork: the same offended ear, the same refusal to pretend otherwise, merely redirected from a student's face to a town council's files.

The affair also belongs to the larger Arnstadt pattern the era essay traces. The town had hired a teenager over the heads of his seniors, paid him better than they were paid, and expected him to lead students older than himself; the resentments that arrangement seeded were bound to surface somewhere, and they surfaced here, at night, with a stick. The consistory's parting remark about not getting along with the students was not gratuitous — it was the institution noticing that its brilliant young hire was accumulating enemies, and it foreshadows the choir-rehearsal grievance that would dominate the hearings a year later.

Why does the timeline dwell on a street fight? Because this is the most human single document we possess of the young Bach: twenty years old, mouthy, armed, absolutely certain that his standards were the point and everyone else's feelings were not, and willing to say so under oath to the body that employed him. The nineteenth century, needing a saint, would have no use for this scene. The timeline needs it badly — the plaster saint composed nothing, and the man who drew a sword over the sound of a badly played bassoon is recognizably the man who spent the next forty-five years at war with every institution that tolerated bad music.
