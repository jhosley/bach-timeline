Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

The 'Zippel Fagottist' affair: swords in the market square

1705-08-04 · arnstadt
documented

Crossing the market square at night on 4 August 1705, Bach was accosted by Johann Heinrich Geyersbach, a student six years his senior who played bassoon in the church's student ensemble, with five companions and a stick. Geyersbach demanded to know why Bach had insulted him; had he called him a Zippel Fagottist — a "greenhorn (or prick of a) bassoonist"? He struck Bach in the face; Bach drew his sword; they grappled; the others separated them.

The consistory investigated, and the minutes preserve everything — including Bach's admission that he had made the remark (his defense: he had only said that Geyersbach's bassoon playing made sounds unfit for description in church records — the minutes are more delicate: he had "insulted the bassoon," and its player with it). The consistory's finding is a small masterpiece of institutional even-handedness: Geyersbach was wrong to assault him, but Bach "might very well have refrained from calling him a Zippel Fagottist," since "such gibes lead in the end to unpleasantness" — and, they added pointedly, he did not get along well with the students generally.

Note the sword: organists ranked as gentlemen-professionals and wore one. Note also what the insult was about: the sound of bad playing physically offended him — the first documented instance of the intolerance for mediocre execution that resurfaces in every era (the 1730 Leipzig memo on incompetent musicians is this scene grown up and gone to paperwork).

Why it matters. The most human single document of young Bach: twenty years old, mouthy, armed, and absolutely certain his standards were the point. The timeline needs him this way — the plaster saint composed nothing.
