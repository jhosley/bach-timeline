Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

First job: court violinist (and lackey) at Weimar

1703-01-15 · weimar
documented

Early in 1703, in the household books of a small court at Weimar, the future supreme composer of European music makes his first appearance as a salaried adult — among the lackeys. The word deserves to be read without flinching, because the clerks meant it plainly: a lackey was a liveried servant, and that was the administrative category into which a seventeen-year-old violinist fell when he joined the private Capelle of Duke Johann Ernst III. School was finished, university was unaffordable, and this was the first respectable offer. Bach took it. Europe's future master began his career, on paper, as a footman who could play the violin.

The entry is a permanently useful corrective, which is why this card lingers on it. Nothing romanticizes the eighteenth-century musician's life faster than hindsight, and nothing dispels the romance faster than the actual books: a court musician was a servant among servants, entered in the ledgers by rank, and the entry rank was low. There was no insult intended and none, as far as we know, taken — this was simply the normal door through which a young player entered court service. But it fixes the baseline from which everything later must be measured, including the ferocity with which the mature Bach defended his titles and his dignity. A man who starts in livery learns exactly what titles are worth.

One more detail of the arrangement deserves a flag, because it detonates fourteen years later. Johann Ernst III was the junior of Weimar's two co-ruling dukes — the duchy was governed, awkwardly, by an uncle and nephew at once — and Bach's first employer was the lesser of the pair. Remember the arrangement: when Bach returns to Weimar in 1708 and is eventually caught between the two courts' rivalries, the mechanism that ends with him [in the senior duke's jail in 1717] is already visible here in miniature. The timeline files this card as prologue, and the prologue is load-bearing.

The job itself was real work in a real orchestra, and it lasted only about six months — because in June 1703 an engagement to examine a new organ in Arnstadt turned into a far better job. But brief as it was, the Weimar interlude planted flags that the rest of the career would march past. He had now been inside a working court Capelle, and had seen its French and Italian repertoire not from the organ bench but from a violinist's chair — a detail worth holding onto, because the ensemble-writing fluency of the later concertos starts in ensemble chairs, not at keyboards. A composer who has sat in the section, counting rests and watching the bowing, writes for players differently than one who has only directed them. And he had made himself known to the Weimar establishment, which did not forget: five years later the same court would call him back, this time at officer rank.

Read this card, finally, as the positions track's baseline. Bach's career was a fifteen-year climb through every rung of German musical employment — lackey-violinist, town organist, court organist, Konzertmeister, Kapellmeister, cantor-director — and he is very possibly the only composer of his stature who knew the trade's whole ladder personally, from the bottom rung up. That knowledge is audible in the later music-directing: the memos about what players need, the exact calibration of what an ensemble can be asked to do. It is also audible, less charmingly, in his lifelong contempt for incompetent superiors — the certainty of a man who had held every job beneath theirs and knew precisely how each one should be done. Both the craft and the contempt begin here, in the household books of a junior court, filed under servants.
