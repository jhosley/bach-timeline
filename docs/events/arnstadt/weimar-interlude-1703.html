Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

First job: court violinist (and lackey) at Weimar

1703-01-15 · weimar
documented

School finished, university unaffordable, Bach took the first respectable offer: a place in the small private Capelle of Duke Johann Ernst III at Weimar — the junior, less powerful of Weimar's two co-ruling dukes (remember the arrangement; it detonates over Bach's head in 1717). The court books list him among the lackeys — liveried servants — a normal entry rank for a young court musician and a permanently useful corrective to any romantic picture of the era's working conditions: Europe's future supreme composer began his career, administratively, as a footman who could play the violin.

The post was real work in a real orchestra, and it lasted only about six months — because in June 1703 the examination engagement in Arnstadt turned into a far better job. But the Weimar interlude planted flags: he had now been inside a court Capelle, seen its French-Italian repertoire from a violinist's chair (the ensemble-writing fluency of the later concertos starts in ensemble chairs, not at keyboards), and made himself known to the Weimar establishment — which would call him back, at officer rank, five years later.

Why it matters. The positions track's baseline reading: Bach's career was a fifteen-year climb through every rung of German musical employment — lackey-violinist, town organist, court organist, Konzertmeister, Kapellmeister, cantor-director. No composer of his stature ever knew the trade's whole ladder so personally, and his later music-directing (and his later contempt for incompetent superiors) is the knowledge talking.
