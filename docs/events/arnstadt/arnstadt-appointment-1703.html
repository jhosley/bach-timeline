Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

Organist at eighteen — on his own terms

1703-08-09 · arnstadt
documented

In June 1703 Bach was engaged to examine the Neue Kirche's newly completed Wender organ — extraordinary in itself for an eighteen-year-old; organ examination was a senior expert's trade, and the invitation says how far his reputation had already traveled through the clan network. He tested the machine, then played it for the town. Six weeks later, on 9 August 1703, the consistory issued his appointment as organist.

The terms tell the story: a combined salary and allowances of some 80-plus florins — more than either of the town's two senior organists earned — for duties officially confined to organ service at a handful of weekly services. The appointment certificate survives, sermon-like in its charges ("be true, industrious and faithful... appear promptly... take good care of the organ"), each line of which the coming cards will show him testing.

The light duty load was the post's hidden treasure. An organist with a new instrument, spare time, and no superior musician for miles: Arnstadt was, functionally, Bach's composition studio years. The early organ repertoire — toccatas, chorale partitas in Böhm's manner, the first fully characteristic preludes — takes shape here (dating individual early works is notoriously soft; the R3 catalog will flag each).

Why it matters. Compare the ladder's rungs: six months ago, a lackey; now, at eighteen, the best-paid organist in a proud town, hired over seniors' heads on raw demonstrated ability. Both facts — the meteoric rise and the resentments it must have seeded around him — are load-bearing context for the trouble ahead.
