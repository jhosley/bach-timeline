Arnstadt: the difficult young genius

'Strange variations... foreign tones': the consistory hearings

1706-02-21 · arnstadt
documented

The hearing of 21 February 1706 opened with the absence (four months on four weeks' leave) and moved swiftly to the deeper grievance — his playing. The minutes' complaint has become the most quoted job-performance review in music history: Bach had been making "many curious variations in the chorale, and mingled many foreign tones in it, so that the congregation has been confused by it." Subsequent admonitions fill out the picture: told to stop preluding at such length, he had responded by playing absurdly short; and he still refused to rehearse with the student choir, considering (he let it be known) no proper director had been provided.

Read musically, the charge is priceless: we are hearing, through scandalized committee prose, the young Bach's harmonic imagination hitting a provincial congregation — the chromatic passing tones, the unexpected reharmonizations, exactly the vocabulary that (tamed and perfected) makes the mature chorale settings the textbook of Western harmony. The congregation wasn't wrong that something strange was happening; they were the first audience of it.

A November session added the era's romantic footnote: the organist had "made music" in the choir loft with an "unfamiliar maiden" (frembde Jungfer). Tradition identifies her — plausibly but without proof — as Maria Barbara Bach, his cousin, then living in Arnstadt, whom he married the following year. (The identification is flagged tradition; the citation itself is documented.)

Why it matters. The Craft and positions tracks cross here exactly: the same independence that made him ungovernable made the music unprecedented, and the two would never be separable. Every subsequent employer bought both.
