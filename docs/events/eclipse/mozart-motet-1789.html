The Eclipse: 1750–1829

'Now, there is something one can learn from!': Mozart at the Thomaskirche

1789-04-22 · leipzig
documented

In April 1789, passing through Leipzig on a journey to Berlin, Mozart visited the Thomaskirche — Bach's church, where the dead cantor's successors and choir had never stopped singing his motets. The aged cantor Doles, a Bach pupil himself, arranged a welcome he knew the guest would not get anywhere else on earth: he had the Thomanerchor perform Singet dem Herrn ein neues Lied — eight voices, two choirs, the old master's counterpoint at full sail, in the room it was written for.

The account we have was published nine years later by Rochlitz, who knew the witnesses, and the timeline handles it the way it handles all such treasures: the scene's core is accepted, its dialogue was polished in the retelling, and the exact words carry a tradition flag. With that said, here is what Rochlitz reports. A few bars in, Mozart sat up, startled. A few more, and he cried out — "Was ist das?" — what is that? — and then, as the motet ended, the sentence that gives this card its title: "Now, there is something one can learn from!" Told that the school possessed more Bach motets, he demanded to see them. There were no scores — only the performing parts, the working papers of a choir that sang this music as routine — so the parts were brought, and Mozart spread them around himself: on his knees, on the chairs nearest him, pages held in both hands. He did not rise, the account says, until he had read through everything there. Before he left, he asked for a copy, and received one.

It is worth slowing down over each layer of this scene, because each one carries a piece of the whole arc. Start with the choir: the music was there to be heard at all only because one institution had kept singing it, week upon week, through four decades in which the wider world had filed its composer under "learned, obsolete." The eclipse never reached inside the Thomasschule; custody, here, was simply habit. Then the visitor: Mozart was prepared — van Swieten's Vienna Sundays had trained him on the fugues for seven years — and he was ambushed anyway. Reading counterpoint in a library is one kind of knowledge; eight-part polyphony alive in a stone room is another, and the gap between them is exactly what the era's manuscript culture could not transmit. The paper had survived; the sound had to be met in person, and almost nobody could.

And then the reaction itself, which may be the most telling layer of all. Mozart's response was not reverence, not antiquarian curiosity, not the pious nod one gives a grandfather's portrait. It was a craftsman's lunge: something to learn from — the parts on his knees, the pages in both hands, the professional stripping a colleague's work for technique. In that gesture the dead cantor was readmitted to living music history, not by sentiment but by the professional judgment of the highest court then sitting. The century had concluded that Bach's counterpoint was yesterday's learning; the century's greatest composer, given twenty minutes with the actual sound, ruled otherwise on the spot.

That is why this small, half-polished anecdote is the emotional hinge of the entire reception arc, and the timeline's single most vivid proof that the eclipse was custodial, not qualitative. Nothing about the music had failed; only its distribution had. Put the parts in front of qualified eyes and the verdict reversed itself in minutes — but for forty more years, qualified eyes were the only ones that would get the chance. The servant Leipzig had buried in 1750 became, in one documented gasp, the teacher of the age's genius; in Berlin, forty years on, the public finally caught up with what Mozart knew before he rose from those chairs.
