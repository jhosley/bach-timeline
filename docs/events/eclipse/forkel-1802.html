The Eclipse: 1750–1829

Forkel's biography: the legend gets an author

1802 · goettingen
documented

Johann Nikolaus Forkel — Göttingen's university music director, pioneering music historian — had corresponded for years with Emanuel and Friedemann, harvesting their memories of their father. In 1802 he published the result: Ueber Johann Sebastian Bachs Leben, Kunst und Kunstwerke — the first biography, sixty pages that fixed the image of Bach for a century: the moonlight copying, the Marchand duel, the Goldberg insomnia story — much of this timeline's tradition-flagged material enters the record precisely here, family memory professionally embalmed.

Forkel's motive was explicit, and it built the revival's political engine. Writing with Napoleon's armies redrawing Germany, he framed Bach as national patrimony: this art is "an invaluable national heritage, with which no other nation has anything to be compared" — and the famous closing charge: "this great man... was a German. Be proud of him, German fatherland, but be worthy of him too." A fragmented nation with no unified state was assembling a unified culture, and Bach — learned, deep, unfashionably serious, safely dead — was conscripted as its foundation stone. (Applegate's study, this arc's modern authority, traces how completely the 1829 revival ran on this fuel.)

Read today, the book is double: genuinely precious (the sons' firsthand testimony exists nowhere else — Bach's practicing habits, his teaching sequence, his verdicts on other composers) and genuinely tendentious (the cosmopolitan synthesizer of French and Italian styles recast as essentially, exclusively German). Both truths belong on the timeline.

Why it matters. Every revival needs a story, and Forkel wrote it — accurate, embellished, and weaponized in one small volume. The nationalist frame he built carried Bach's return for a century, did real interpretive damage that scholarship still unwinds, and stands as this timeline's cleanest warning: reception is never neutral; someone is always using the past.
