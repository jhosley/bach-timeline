Eisenach: born into the guild

Luther's school, Luther's chorales

1693 · eisenach
documented

From about age seven Bach attended Eisenach's Latin school — the same school Martin Luther had attended two centuries earlier — and sang in its chorus musicus, the choir that led congregational singing at the Georgenkirche and sang through the streets for alms. Above the town stood the Wartburg, where Luther, in hiding, had translated the New Testament into German.

The theological geography is the point. Luther's Reformation was uniquely musical: he wrote hymns himself, declared music "next to theology" in honor, and built worship around the chorale — the congregational hymn in German, meant for every voice in the room. By Bach's childhood, Lutheran Germany possessed a repertoire of hundreds of chorales that everyone — cobbler, duchess, schoolboy — knew by heart, the shared musical vocabulary of an entire civilization.

Bach learned that repertoire here, from the inside, as a singer. It became the load-bearing material of his life's work: the cantatas are built on chorales; the Passions pause on them at every emotional summit; the organ works elaborate them; the 371 four-part settings became (and remain) the textbook of Western harmony. When the Craft section's essay on the chorale-as-classroom arrives, its story starts in this schoolyard.

Why it matters. You cannot study Bach's sacred music without the chorales any more than you can study Shakespeare without the English Bible. This card marks where he — like his future congregations — absorbed them: by singing, before he could have analyzed a note.
