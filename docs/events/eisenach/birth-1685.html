Eisenach: born into the guild

Birth of Johann Sebastian Bach

1685-03-21 · eisenach
documented

The baptismal register of the Georgenkirche in Eisenach carries an entry for 23 March 1685: a son of the town musician Johann Ambrosius Bach and his wife Elisabeth, née Lämmerhirt, born two days earlier, christened Johann Sebastian. The page survives, and it repays a slow look, because the eighteenth century wrote its social order into such documents the way geology writes eras into rock. The godfathers who stood at the font were a town musician and a ducal forestry official — men of trades and offices, solid, guild-respectable, nowhere near gentry. That is the exact social station into which the most consequential composer in the Western tradition was born: the working middle of a small Thuringian town, closer to the huntsmen than to the court.

A word about the date, because it is less simple than it looks. Protestant Germany in 1685 still kept the old Julian calendar, ten days behind the reformed Gregorian one; by modern reckoning Bach was born on 31 March. This timeline follows the sources and gives old-style dates for events inside Germany before 1700, flagging conversions where they matter. It is a small discipline, but it is the right one to learn at the very first card: we take the past on its own terms, calendar and all.

He was the eighth and youngest child, and the household he entered was not merely musical but professionally so — music as a trade, inherited, taught, and guarded like any craft. His father directed Eisenach's town music, the Stadtpfeifer, the civic band of winds and strings that sounded fanfares from the tower and played the town's weddings and ceremonies, and he served as a court trumpeter besides. In Thuringia the family name was so completely fused with the profession that town musicians were sometimes called "the Bachs" whether or not any actual Bach was among them. The boy's first classroom was the family workshop, strings and brass by day, and his first inheritance was the clan itself — six generations of organists, cantors, and town musicians whose networks and training methods stood ready for him before he could walk.

The place mattered as much as the family. The church where he was baptized was the church where Martin Luther had once preached, and above the town stood the Wartburg, the castle where Luther in hiding had translated the New Testament into German. Sebastian would attend the Latin school Luther had attended and sing in the same choir tradition, absorbing a Lutheranism in which — by Luther's own formulation — music stood next to theology itself. Luther's town shaped him as surely as the family trade did. And at the organ of that baptismal church sat a third omen: his father's cousin Johann Christoph Bach, the most gifted musician the family had yet produced, a composer Sebastian would later call "profound" — living proof, at the very font, that a Bach could be more than a craftsman.

There is one more frame worth setting around the register's plain entry. 1685 is the great vintage year of the Baroque: the two exact contemporaries whose paths make Bach's so illuminating by contrast, Handel and Domenico Scarlatti, were born the same year, and the world-track card on the class of 1685 follows their careers outward across Europe. Bach's ran the other way — he alone of the three never left Germany, never wrote an opera — and the contrast will matter again and again, because it shows the narrowness was chosen, not imposed.

So the one-line entry holds three forces in embryo: craft, faith, and region. To be born a Bach, in Luther's town, in musical Thuringia, was to inherit all three at once, and this timeline's tracks spend the next sixty-five years following what he built from them.
