Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

A month in the Duke's jail

1717-11-06 – 1717-12-02 · weimar
documented

On the sixth of November, 1717, a clerk at the ducal court of Weimar dipped his pen and recorded, in the flat language of administrative routine, one of the strangest sentences in the history of music: the concertmaster and court organist Bach was to be confined to the district judge's place of detention "for too stubbornly forcing the issue of his dismissal." He would sit there for twenty-seven days. When the door opened on the second of December, the same ledger noted his release "with notice of his unfavorable discharge" — fired, in disgrace, with a jail record. He was thirty-two years old, the most gifted musician in Germany, and he had just spent nearly a month in a cell for the crime of trying to change jobs.

To modern eyes the episode reads as absurdity; to Bach's contemporaries it read as order. A court musician in the Holy Roman Empire was not an employee in any sense we would recognize. He was a bound servant of his sovereign, listed in the household books somewhere between the huntsmen and the pastry cooks, and he could no more resign at will than a soldier could walk out of a garrison. Departure required the ruler's gracious consent — a demission — and Duke Wilhelm Ernst, a man who fined his musicians for missteps and quizzed his servants on the content of Sunday's sermon, was not in a consenting mood. Bach had a signed appointment waiting for him at the court of Cöthen; the young Prince Leopold there had been paying his salary since August, as the account books show. None of that mattered. What mattered was that a duke's servant had presumed to insist.

The stubbornness was not new, and that is what makes the document so valuable. The consistory records of Arnstadt had already preserved a twenty-year-old Bach brawling in the market square with a bassoonist he had insulted, and overstaying a four-week leave by three months. The Leipzig town council would later fill its minutes with decades of the same immovability, wielded over choir prefects and funeral fees. The Weimar jailing sits in the middle of that long documentary arc like a keystone: the fullest price he ever paid for the trait, and the clearest proof of how deep it ran. John Eliot Gardiner, reading these records with a conductor's eye for character, sees in them the temperament of a man who had buried both parents before his tenth birthday and learned early that no one else would defend his worth. Whatever its source, the pattern is beyond dispute. Bach fought his employers his whole life — not for luxury, but over the terms and dignity of his work — and he never once flinched first.

What gives the episode its retrospective glow is what stood on the other side of the cell door. The post Bach was so stubbornly forcing his way toward was the Kapellmeistership of Cöthen — the highest title of his career, a musician-prince who treasured him, and the six years in which the Brandenburg Concertos, the cello suites, the solo-violin Sei Solo, and the first book of the Well-Tempered Clavier would pour out in an unbroken stream. Every note of that instrumental golden age was, in a sense, purchased in advance by twenty-seven days of detention. There is no evidence Bach composed in the cell, though biographers have never resisted imagining it; the honest record gives us only the dates, the charge, and the discharge.

It also gives us a corrective that this timeline returns to again and again. The nineteenth century, needing a saint, sanded Bach's documents into piety; the actual paper trail preserves a proud, difficult, self-certain professional who knew exactly what he was worth and said so to dukes. The music did not come from meekness. It came from the same unbending will that a Weimar clerk, one November morning, filed under insubordination.
