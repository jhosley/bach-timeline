Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

The Leipzig audition — third choice for the job of the century

1723-02-07 · leipzig
documented

Leipzig's cantor Johann Kuhnau died in June 1722, opening one of the most important musical jobs in Protestant Germany: Cantor of the Thomasschule and director of music for the city's principal churches. The town council's search is unusually well documented in its minutes, and the documents are merciless to posterity's sense of justice.

The council's first choice was Georg Philipp Telemann — Germany's most famous composer, a Leipzig favorite — who used the offer to extract a raise from Hamburg and withdrew. The second was Christoph Graupner, Kapellmeister at Darmstadt, who could not obtain release from his prince. Only then did the field settle on the remaining candidates, Bach among them. On 7 February 1723 (Quinquagesima Sunday) he auditioned with two cantatas — Jesus nahm zu sich die Zwölfe, BWV 22, and Du wahrer Gott und Davids Sohn, BWV 23. In April the council voted for him, one member, Councilman Platz, recording the era's verdict with immortal wrongness: since the best men could not be obtained, "mediocre ones would have to be accepted."

Bach had his own hesitations, later confessed in the Erdmann letter: "it seemed strange to me at first that I should become a cantor after having been a Kapellmeister" — a real step down in rank. What decided him: the fading of Cöthen's music, the University of Leipzig for his sons, and the return to sacred music on the largest stage available.

Why it matters. Every student should sit with this: the composer of the St. Matthew Passion was the committee's third choice, hired reluctantly, and the surviving paperwork says so in plain German. It is the timeline's standing caution about how institutions judge genius in real time — and the perfect gateway into the Leipzig eras, where the same council and this same stubborn man will fight for twenty-seven years.
