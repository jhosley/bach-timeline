Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Working conditions: the Capelle, the salary, the freedom

1718 · cothen
documented

This entry is not a single dated moment but the standing conditions of Bach's Cöthen employment — worth its own card because no other period of his life looked like this.

The ensemble. Leopold's Capelle numbered about seventeen or eighteen. Its core was ex-Berlin: when King Friedrich Wilhelm I of Prussia — the "soldier king," who had no use for opera or orchestras — dissolved the royal Capelle in 1713, the teenage Leopold hired several of its finest players. These were virtuosi with names Bach knew and wrote for individually: the violinist Joseph Spiess (likely first player for the violin concertos), the gambist Christian Ferdinand Abel (whose son later founded London concert life with Bach's own son Johann Christian — the web of this world is small), the cellist Christian Bernhard Linigke, and others. Much of the Cöthen instrumental music is best understood as portraiture of specific colleagues.

The pay. Roughly 400 thalers plus allowances — about double Weimar, making Bach the second-highest-paid person at court. For calibration: a Thuringian schoolmaster might earn 40 thalers a year; Bach's later Leipzig cantorate nominally paid less in fixed salary but far more in fees. Cöthen was, in straightforward money terms, an excellent job.

The duties. Chamber and orchestral music for the court's evenings, serenatas for the prince's birthday (10 December) and New Year, keeping the harpsichords maintained, teaching. Crucially, no liturgical calendar: the Reformed (Calvinist) court wanted no concerted church music, so the weekly cantata treadmill of Weimar and Leipzig simply did not exist here.

Why it matters. Conditions are visible in the music. Write for a court of virtuosi with no liturgy, and you get the Brandenburgs' six different instrumental line-ups, solo-string monuments, and teaching books. This card is the timeline's clearest demonstration of its core thesis: the job explains the works.
