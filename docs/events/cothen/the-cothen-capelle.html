Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Working conditions: the Capelle, the salary, the freedom

1718 · cothen
documented

Every biography of Bach eventually asks the same question of the Cöthen years: how did one man, in six years, produce the Brandenburg Concertos, the cello suites, the solo-violin Sei Solo, the violin concertos, the first book of the Well-Tempered Clavier, and the Inventions — while also running a court's music, raising four children, burying a wife, and remarrying? Part of the answer is unknowable, the part we shelve under genius. But a surprisingly large part sits in plain sight in the Cöthen payment registers, and it can be stated as a working professional would state it: for the only time in his life, Bach had a great ensemble, great pay, and almost no obligations. This card is about those three conditions, because the music is unimaginable without them.

Begin with the ensemble, because Bach certainly did. Leopold's Capelle numbered about seventeen or eighteen players, and its core had an aristocratic pedigree of its own: when Friedrich Wilhelm I — Prussia's "soldier king," a man who regarded opera houses roughly as he regarded lace — dissolved the Berlin royal orchestra in 1713, the teenage Prince of Anhalt-Cöthen quietly hired its best men. So the band that greeted Bach in 1717 was, in effect, the salvaged heart of one of Europe's great orchestras, transplanted into a town of three thousand. These were not anonymous section players but virtuosi Bach knew by name and wrote for by name: Joseph Spiess, the concertmaster, almost certainly the first voice of the violin concertos; Christian Ferdinand Abel, the gambist, whose son would one day found London's concert life in partnership with Bach's own youngest son — the eighteenth century folding in on itself, as it constantly did; Linigke the cellist; Rose the oboist. Much of the Cöthen instrumental music is best heard as group portraiture — the Brandenburgs especially, six concertos that read like an affectionate inventory of what each colleague could do when dared.

The pay told Bach what he was worth, in the only language courts spoke plainly. Roughly four hundred thalers a year plus allowances made him the second-highest-paid official in the principality, behind only the marshal of the court. Double his Weimar income; ten times a village schoolmaster's. He was thirty-two, had been working for wages since boyhood, and had never been valued like this — would never be again, in fact, in quite so unambiguous a form.

But the third condition was the miracle, and it was theological. Anhalt-Cöthen was a Calvinist state, and Reformed worship — plain psalms, plain walls, deep suspicion of ornament — wanted nothing from a Kapellmeister. No weekly cantata. No liturgical calendar with its remorseless deadlines. No consistory complaining about strange harmonies confusing the congregation. Bach's employment, for the only stretch of his life, had nothing to do with church: the job was chamber and orchestral music for the court's evenings, serenatas for the prince's birthday in December and for New Year's Day, the instruments kept in repair, the young prince partnered at the gamba. For a man whose declared life's purpose was church music, it should have felt like exile. What it actually produced was the least constrained, most exploratory music he ever wrote — the sets of six, the encyclopedic surveys, the teaching books — as if the absence of the weekly sermon-clock let a lifetime of instrumental thinking pour through the opened gate all at once.

The lesson generalizes, and this timeline leans on it everywhere: the job explains the works. Not the inspiration, not the depth — no payroll ledger accounts for the Chaconne — but the shape, the genre, the sheer direction of the output. Weimar's duke wanted organ music and got a decade of it; Leipzig's council would want cantatas and get five annual cycles; and a musician-prince with a virtuoso band and no liturgy got the greatest instrumental portfolio ever assembled. Conditions are never the whole story. They are always the first chapter of it.
