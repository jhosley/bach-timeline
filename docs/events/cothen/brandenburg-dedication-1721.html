Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Six concertos posted to Brandenburg

1721-03-24 · cothen
documented

On the twenty-fourth of March, 1721, Bach signed his name to a letter written in the elaborately submissive French that European court etiquette required, and attached it to the most consequential parcel in the history of orchestral music. The letter addressed Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg — the Prussian princeling who had heard Bach play in Berlin two years earlier and asked, in the way great men asked, to be sent some of his compositions. The parcel contained Six Concerts avec plusieurs instruments — six concertos with several instruments — copied out in Bach's finest calligraphic hand, a fair copy so beautiful it functions as an artwork in its own right. The world would eventually call them the Brandenburg Concertos, a name coined by a biographer a hundred and fifty years later; their composer knew them only as a gift, two years late, to a man who had asked politely.

The dedication letter rewards reading in full, because it is a primary document of what "working conditions" meant at the very top of the eighteenth-century musical profession. Bach begs the Margrave "not to judge their imperfection by the standards of the refined and delicate taste" His Highness is known to possess, and describes himself with "the very humble and very obedient servitude" the genre demanded. None of this was sincerity; all of it was grammar — the standard self-abasement of the patronage system, executed by a man who privately knew his own worth to the thaler. Read plainly, the parcel was a job application. Cöthen's musical budget had begun to look mortal, and a Hohenzollern connection was worth cultivating; the six concertos were Bach advertising, to a court an order of magnitude grander than his own, precisely what his Capelle could do — six different instrumental line-ups, six different answers to the concerto's central question, a portfolio disguised as a compliment.

Nothing came of it. No summons from Berlin, no fee, no recorded acknowledgment of any kind. The Margrave's modest household band could not have covered the works' exotic instrumentations — the high clarino trumpet, the pair of violas da gamba, the echo flutes — and there is no evidence the presentation score was ever once played from. When Christian Ludwig died in 1734, the inventory of his estate lumped the manuscript in with a job lot of concertos by many hands, valued at a few groschen. It passed through private collections and archives, survived the century, survived the next one, survived evacuation in the Second World War, and rests today in the Berlin State Library as one of the most treasured musical manuscripts on earth — which is history's little joke, since its neglect is what preserved it. An inattentive dedicatee turned out to be an immaculate archivist.

Bach, ever practical, lost nothing by the silence. The concertos were not written for Brandenburg; they were assembled from music composed over years for his own virtuosi, and the performing materials stayed home in Cöthen, where the works went on earning their keep at the prince's evenings. The dedication copy was always a duplicate — a message in a bottle, as it turned out, addressed to the wrong prince and delivered, two centuries late, to everyone.

That trajectory — masterpiece ignored, shelved, nearly lost, then resurrected into universal glory — is worth holding in mind, because it is the entire architecture of this timeline in miniature. What happened to one parcel between 1721 and 1734 is what happened to Bach's whole legacy between 1750 and 1829: the same eclipse, the same accident of custody, the same afterlife out of all proportion to the reception. The Brandenburgs simply ran the experiment first.
