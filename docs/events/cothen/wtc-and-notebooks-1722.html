Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

1722: the Well-Tempered Clavier fair copy and Anna Magdalena's first notebook

1722 · cothen
documented

Two manuscripts carry the date 1722, and together they map the full range of Bach's keyboard imagination in the last full year of the Cöthen idyll — one addressed to posterity, the other to the woman across the parlor.

The first is among the most consequential objects in Western music: the calligraphic fair copy of Das Wohltemperirte Clavier — "The Well-Tempered Keyboard" — a prelude and fugue in every major and minor key, all twenty-four, ascending chromatically from C major into regions of the tonal map where no composer had seriously built before. The title itself was a technological manifesto. Well-tempered referred to the newer systems of tuning keyboard instruments that distributed the mathematics of intonation cleverly enough to make every key usable — where the older temperaments had left the remote keys howling — and Bach's collection was simultaneously the demonstration, the advertisement, and the colonization of that new world. F-sharp major and E-flat minor were not exotic effects here; they were homes, furnished. His title page states the intended audience with the same double vision as the Inventions' manifesto: "for the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning, and especially for the pastime of those already skilled in this study." A beginner's course and a master's recreation, in one binding — the whole Bach pedagogy in a single phrase. And characteristically, the monument had domestic foundations: many of its preludes had begun life two years earlier as teaching pieces in nine-year-old Friedemann's notebook. The workshop fed the cathedral.

The second manuscript of 1722 answers the first the way a hearth answers a spire. In the wedding year, Bach began a Clavierbüchlein for his new wife — the first of two notebooks that would carry her name — and into it went, among other things, the earliest versions of five of the French Suites, the most intimate and songful of his keyboard dance sets. Consider what the gift actually was. Anna Magdalena was a professional musician; a notebook of new keyboard music from her husband was shop talk, courtship, and curriculum at once, and the pieces' parlor scale tells us exactly what room they were written for. Where Friedemann's book taught a child to think in voices, Anna Magdalena's furnished a marriage with repertoire. Domestic life and high art were, in this household, a single continuous activity — the fact about Bach that the monuments most easily hide and the notebooks most plainly show.

The Well-Tempered Clavier's afterlife deserves its coda here, because no object on this timeline traveled a stranger road to immortality. Bach never published it. For eighty years it existed only in manuscript copies, passed hand to hand through the professional underground — the samizdat classic of the eclipse era, studied by Mozart at a Vienna salon, drilled into a boy named Beethoven in Bonn, hoarded by connoisseurs while the public forgot its author's first name. When it finally reached print in 1801, it emerged not as a novelty but as scripture already canonized by three generations of the only readers who had mattered. Chopin practiced from it before every concert; Schumann called it the musician's daily bread; a Prelude and Fugue from its second volume now rides aboard the Voyager probes, outbound past the edge of the solar system. All of which lay unimaginably far from the Cöthen parlor in 1722, where the ink was drying on two title pages — one facing eternity, one facing his wife — and the composer, for one more year, had every reason to think life had settled at last.
