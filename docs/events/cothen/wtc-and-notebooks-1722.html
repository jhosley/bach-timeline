Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

1722: the Well-Tempered Clavier fair copy and Anna Magdalena's first notebook

1722 · cothen
documented

Two manuscripts date from 1722, and together they show the two registers of Bach's keyboard mind — the encyclopedic and the domestic.

The first is the calligraphic fair copy of Das Wohltemperirte Clavier — "The Well-Tempered Keyboard": a prelude and fugue in every major and minor key, all twenty-four, an itinerary no one had completed at this level before. Bach's title page states the audience in his own words: "for the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning, and especially for the pastime of those already skilled in this study." Note what the title advertises: well-tempered — the newer tuning systems that made every key usable (older keyboard temperaments left remote keys sourly out of tune). The collection is thus simultaneously a technological demonstration, a teaching course, and a compositional encyclopedia — the fullest early statement of the encyclopedic instinct that rules Bach's later life. Many of its preludes had begun two years earlier as teaching pieces in Friedemann's notebook: the domestic workshop feeding the monument.

The second manuscript is the first Clavierbüchlein for Anna Magdalena — a wedding-year gift-book into which went, among other things, early versions of five of the French Suites. A notebook for a bride who was herself a professional musician: teaching material, dance suites, domestic repertoire.

Why it matters. 1722 is the pivot where Bach's Cöthen pedagogy crystallizes into permanent form — and the WTC will become the single most influential unpublished work in music history: circulated in manuscript for eighty years, the training text of Mozart, Beethoven ("it should be every pianist's daily bread"), and Chopin, and a main artery of the Arc II rediscovery story. When the timeline's ribbon dims after 1750, the WTC is one of the veins that keeps glowing.
