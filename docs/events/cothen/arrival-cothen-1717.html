Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Arrival in Cöthen — and a detour to test Leipzig's new organ

1717-12-10 · cothen
documented

Released from Weimar's jail on 2 December 1717, Bach moved fast. Within days he was in Cöthen, in time for the festivities around Prince Leopold's birthday on 10 December, and the court's books record its new Kapellmeister at work.

That same month he accepted an outside engagement that reads, in hindsight, like foreshadowing: Leipzig's university church (the Paulinerkirche) had just completed a large new organ, and Bach was invited to give it its formal examination. Organ examination was one of his most trusted sideline trades — a mix of engineering inspection and virtuoso demonstration, testing wind supply, voicing, and action, then improvising to show the instrument off. His written report survives: thorough, technical, fair to the builder while noting defects. It was Bach's first professional appearance in Leipzig, five years before the cantorate fell vacant.

Why it matters. Two things. First, the organ-examiner side of Bach is a window into his mind that students often miss: he was a machine-minded craftsman — obsessed with winding, temperament, and action — not only a composer, and his writing for every instrument shows that mechanical empathy. Second, note the geography: Weimar, Cöthen, Leipzig are all within a couple days' travel of each other. His whole world was this small; the timeline's map view makes the point visually.
