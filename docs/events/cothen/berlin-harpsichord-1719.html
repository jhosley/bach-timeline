Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

To Berlin for a harpsichord — and a fateful introduction

1719-03-01 · berlin
documented

In March 1719 the Cöthen accounts show Bach paid for a journey to Berlin, sent to collect an expensive new instrument for the court: a large two-manual harpsichord by Michael Mietke, the Berlin court builder. Leopold's willingness to spend on this scale — and to send his Kapellmeister personally, trusting his examiner's ear — says much about the court's musical seriousness.

While in Berlin, Bach played before Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg, uncle of the Prussian king, who kept his own small Capelle. According to Bach's later dedication, the Margrave asked him to send some compositions. Bach took two years to comply — and what he finally sent, in March 1721, was the collection the world now calls the Brandenburg Concertos.

The Mietke harpsichord had its own compositional consequence: scholars connect it with the extraordinary harpsichord part of the Fifth Brandenburg Concerto, where the keyboard erupts out of the continuo section into a long, unaccompanied cadenza — effectively the birth certificate of the keyboard concerto, plausibly written to show off the court's glamorous new machine (and its Kapellmeister's hands).

Why it matters. A neat case study in how practical circumstances — an instrument purchase, a courtesy call — sit underneath monuments of the repertoire. No Berlin errand, no Brandenburg Concertos, at least not under that name and possibly not in that form.
