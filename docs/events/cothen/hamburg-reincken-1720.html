Hamburg: Reincken's blessing, and the job that went to the highest bidder

1720-11-01 · hamburg
documented

In November 1720 — four months after Maria Barbara's death — Bach traveled to Hamburg, the great free commercial city of the north. The organist post at the Jacobikirche was vacant, and it is hard not to read the trip as a widower testing whether a new life, back at a great organ, might help.

Two things happened. The famous one: before an audience including Johann Adam Reincken — patriarch of the north-German organ school, whom Bach had walked to hear as a Lüneburg schoolboy two decades earlier — Bach improvised on the chorale An Wasserflüssen Babylon ("By the waters of Babylon") for nearly half an hour, in the sprawling north-German fantasia manner that was Reincken's own signature. The old man's recorded response is one of the great torch-passing lines in music history: "I thought this art was dead, but I see that it lives on in you." (The scene comes from the Obituary — vivid family tradition, plausible but polished.)

The unfamous thing: Bach did not get, or in the end pursue, the job. The Jacobikirche's practice — not unusual — expected the successful candidate to make a substantial "voluntary" payment to the church; the post went to one Johann Joachim Heitmann, who offered 4,000 marks. A Hamburg pastor, Erdmann Neumeister, thundered from the pulpit that if one of the angels of Bethlehem came down and played divinely but had no money, he might as well fly away again — a contemporary's disgust, preserved in print.

Why it matters. In one month you get the two poles of Bach's professional world: the deepest artistic lineage (Reincken's blessing linking him back through Sweelinck to the Renaissance) and the crassest employment reality (jobs sold to the highest bidder). Both belong on a timeline about conditions, not just achievements.
