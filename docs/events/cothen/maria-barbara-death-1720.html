Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

The death of Maria Barbara

1720-07-07 · cothen
documented

In May 1720 Bach left for Carlsbad, the Bohemian spa, in Prince Leopold's traveling entourage — the second such trip; the prince took a core of his Capelle along so the music would not stop. Maria Barbara was healthy when he left.

She died suddenly in early July, cause unrecorded, and was buried on 7 July. Travel being what it was, there was no way to reach him. The Obituary — written after Bach's death partly by their son Carl Philipp Emanuel — records the scene as family memory kept it: Bach returned to Cöthen to find that his wife was dead and buried, and that he heard it first at his own door. (That doorstep detail is the family's telling, decades later — the timeline marks the death and burial as documented, the scene as tradition.)

He was thirty-five, now sole parent to four children between five and eleven — Catharina Dorothea, Wilhelm Friedemann, Carl Philipp Emanuel, and Johann Gottfried Bernhard. His widowed status lasted seventeen months.

A note on the Chaconne. The autograph fair copy of the solo-violin Sei Solo, whose D-minor Chaconne is often heard as a tombeau — a memorial — for Maria Barbara, is dated 1720. The dating makes the association possible; no document makes it fact, and some of the music certainly predates her death. The timeline presents the connection as the tradition it is: irresistible, unprovable, and in performance often unforgettable.
