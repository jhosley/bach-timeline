Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Appointed Kapellmeister to Prince Leopold

1717-08-05 · cothen
documented

In August 1717 the account books of a very small court in a very small principality began carrying a very large name. Anhalt-Cöthen — a Calvinist statelet on the flat country between Magdeburg and Halle, its capital a town of perhaps three thousand souls — started paying a salary to one Johann Sebastian Bach, Kapellmeister. The man himself was still a hundred miles away in Weimar, still legally bound to a duke who had no intention of releasing him, and the collision between those two facts would land him in a jail cell before the year was out. But on paper, from that August forward, Bach held the grandest title German musical life could bestow on a working musician.

The word deserves a moment, because Bach cared about it intensely and his whole era did too. A Kapellmeister was not an organist, however famous, and not a cantor, however learned; he was the director of a sovereign's music — an officer of a court, entitled to deference, precedence, and a gentleman's sword. In the finely graded hierarchy of the Holy Roman Empire's musical trades, the title outranked everything else available to a commoner. Bach had just been passed over for exactly this rank at Weimar, where the old Kapellmeister's death had sent the job to the dead man's unremarkable son while the most gifted musician in Germany looked on from the organ loft. Cöthen's offer was, among other things, an answer to that insult, and Bach would sign himself with some version of the Kapellmeister title for the rest of his life — clinging to honorary renewals of it long after he had moved on, the way a professor emeritus keeps a letterhead.

The deeper attraction, though, was the man doing the hiring. Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Cöthen was twenty-three years old and that rarest of things in the German patronage system: a ruler who was himself a genuine musician. He played violin, viola da gamba, and harpsichord; he sang a respectable bass; he had spent his grand tour buying music in Italy rather than merely portraits; and when the King of Prussia dissolved his own court orchestra in 1713, the teenage Leopold had pounced on its best players, assembling in his miniature capital one of the finest small ensembles in Europe. Hiring Bach was the final acquisition. The salary said everything about the priority: roughly four hundred thalers with allowances — double Weimar, and enough to make the new Kapellmeister the second-highest-paid official in the entire principality. For calibration, a Thuringian schoolmaster of the day might see forty thalers in a year.

Years later, in the frankest letter he ever wrote, Bach would tell his old school friend Georg Erdmann what the post had meant to him: "There I had a gracious Prince, who both loved and knew music, and in his service I intended to spend the rest of my life." Musicians rarely wrote such sentences about their employers, and Bach never wrote another one. The intention, of course, did not survive — a princely marriage, a shrinking budget, and the pull of Leipzig would end the idyll within six years — but the sentence stands as the emotional high-water mark of his entire professional biography.

There is a pattern worth carrying away from this appointment, one this timeline traces from Arnstadt to the grave: with Bach, the job always predicts the music. Organist posts produced organ music and cantatas; a cantorate would produce the great sacred cycles; and a Kapellmeistership at a Calvinist court — where Reformed worship wanted no elaborate church music at all — was about to produce the most concentrated outpouring of instrumental masterworks in Western history. In August 1717, none of that music existed. What existed was a line in an account book, a title, and a young prince waiting for his investment to get out of jail.
