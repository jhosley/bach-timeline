Leipzig III: the summing-up

'Old Bach is here': Frederick the Great and the royal theme

1747-05-07 – 1747-05-08 · potsdam
documented

On the evening of 7 May 1747, Frederick the Great was preparing his nightly chamber concert at Potsdam. The ritual was fixed: the philosopher-warrior-king played serious flute, by candlelight, with Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach at the harpsichord — and Emanuel's father, the old cantor of Leipzig, had just arrived in town to visit his son. The list of newcomers was brought in, and the king looked up from it and announced to his musicians: "Gentlemen, old Bach is come." The anecdote descends through Wilhelm Friedemann via Forkel, and its core is corroborated by the Berlin newspapers. The concert was cancelled on the spot. The sixty-two-year-old visitor, still in his traveling clothes, was walked through the palace rooms to try the king's collection of Silbermann fortepianos — the newfangled hammer-action instruments Frederick collected by the dozen — improvising as he went from room to room.

Then came the test. The king played him a theme — long, chromatic, sinking through a diminished world — and asked for a fugue on it, extempore. Whether the theme was Frederick's own or planted for the occasion is genuinely uncertain; Emanuel's fingerprints have been suspected, and the timeline flags the question disputed. Bach delivered a fugue in three voices on the spot, to general astonishment. Asked next for a fugue in six voices on the royal subject, he declined — not every theme, he said, will bear six voices — and improvised a six-part fugue on a theme of his own instead. The refusal is as characteristic as the feat: a craftsman's honesty about material, delivered to a king's face, followed immediately by proof that the limitation lay in the theme and not the man. The next day, the papers report, he gave an organ recital at Potsdam's Heiliggeistkirche.

Home in Leipzig, honor alone did not satisfy him. He spent two months building the answer he could not improvise: the Musical Offering, the royal theme worked into an encyclopedia of what counterpoint could do with it. Two ricercars anchor it — one in three voices, and one in six, the very thing he had declined at Potsdam, now the summit of his keyboard counterpoint. Around them stand ten canons, several notated as riddles for the court's cleverness to solve, and a trio sonata written in the king's own galant taste with the royal theme smuggled through it — the old master paying the new style the sly compliment of infiltration. The whole was engraved and dedicated to Frederick in July 1747. No reply from the king is recorded.

The scene has become the timeline's richest single tableau, and it earns the attention. Here is the Enlightenment's future — the modern prince, the fashionable galant idiom, the hammer-action instruments that would conquer the coming century — meeting counterpoint's past in one candle-lit room, and setting it a challenge meant to be charming. The past answered twice: once on its feet, improvising, and once from the workshop, with a dedication whose elaborate courtesies enclose an object no court in Europe could have produced or, perhaps, fully read. Old counterpoint bowed to new power, and then quietly outlasted it. Frederick's flute evenings are a footnote of cultural history; the six-voice ricercar crossed into the modern canon, and the third arc of this timeline finds it again aboard Webern's orchestration, two centuries on.

It is also the last great public scene of the life. The decade around it was inward work — the Goldbergs behind, the Mass and the Art of Fugue ahead — and Potsdam supplied the legend's final image of the working musician among the powerful: summoned, tested, equal to it, and back on the road to Leipzig within the week, carrying a theme home the way he had carried commissions and obligations home for forty years. This time the patron was incidental. The theme was the employer now.
