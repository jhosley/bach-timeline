Leipzig III: the summing-up

The B minor Mass assembled: a life's sacred music in one summa

1748 – 1749 · leipzig
documented

In 1748–49, in failing health and a deteriorating hand (the autograph's later pages show it), Bach completed his largest single work: the Mass in B minor — the 1733 Dresden Missa (Kyrie and Gloria) extended with a Credo (Symbolum Nicenum), Sanctus (from 1724), and the closing complex — a complete Latin Mass on the grandest scale, from a Lutheran cantor with no occasion, no commission, and no prospect of performing it entire.

Its construction method is its meaning: the Mass is substantially parody at summa scale — his life's finest sacred movements selected, re-texted, and perfected: the Crucifixus is the lament chorus of Weimar's BWV 12 (1714), thirty-five years reconsidered; other movements distill Leipzig cantatas of two decades; the Credo opens in Renaissance stile antico over a walking bass — the Lüneburg library's old church style — while the Et incarnatus stands with his last and most modern writing. Old style and new, 1714 and 1749, chant and gallantry: a curated retrospective of sacred music itself, his own above all. Why Latin, why a full Mass? Dresden practicality, universal permanence, pure summa-instinct — the scholarship debates (the R3 card will map it); the object outruns every explanation.

He never heard it complete; no full performance is documented before the nineteenth century (Emanuel mounted the Credo in 1786). The autograph passed down the family; today it sits in Berlin beside the Brandenburg dedication score — the two poles of his art, shelved together.

Why it matters. The Endzweck of the 1708 letter — "a well-regulated church music to the glory of God" — delivered at last, to no employer, beyond any liturgy, addressed (as far as evidence shows) to posterity and to God. If Arc I has a final chord, this is it.
