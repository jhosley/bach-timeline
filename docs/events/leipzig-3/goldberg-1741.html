Leipzig III: the summing-up

The Goldberg Variations published — and the insomnia legend

1741-10-01 · leipzig
documented

The fourth Clavier-Übung volume, published in Nuremberg in autumn 1741: an Aria — a serene sarabande over a thirty-two-bar bass — followed by thirty variations built not on the melody but on that bass foundation (the Chaconne's principle, domesticated and vastly extended). The design is crystalline: variations in groups of three, every third one a canon at successively wider intervals — at the unison, the second, the third... to the ninth — strictness tightening as the wheel turns, until variation 30 breaks pattern for a quodlibet: two folk songs (one about cabbage and turnips) braided in counterpoint over the sacred bass — the old Bach-clan party trick crowning his most learned structure. Then the Aria returns, unchanged, changed utterly.

The legend (Forkel, 1802): Count Keyserlingk — the Dresden envoy who helped secure Bach's court title — sleepless, commissions variations "of such a gentle and somewhat lively character" for his house harpsichordist, the Bach-trained prodigy Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, to play from the next room; rewards Bach with a goblet of a hundred louis d'or. Problems: the print bears no dedication (unthinkable for a real commission), and Goldberg was about fourteen. Verdict: tradition — likely garbling a true kernel (Keyserlingk owned a copy; Goldberg surely played it), but the world's favorite Bach anecdote is not a document.

A coda in Bach's own hand: his personal copy of the print, rediscovered in 1974, has fourteen additional canons on the Aria's bass (BWV 1087) penned inside the back cover — the composer, post-publication, still mining the same eight notes.

Why it matters. The gateway to late Bach — and, via Gould's 1955 recording, the work that made "Goldberg" a household word two centuries on. The R3 card gives a variation-by-variation listening guide.
