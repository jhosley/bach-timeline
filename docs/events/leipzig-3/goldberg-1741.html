Leipzig III: the summing-up

The Goldberg Variations published — and the insomnia legend

1741-10-01 · leipzig
documented

By the autumn of 1741 the shape of Bach's last decade was becoming visible. Zimmermann, whose coffeehouse had housed the Collegium's concerts, died that year and the ensemble faded with him; the weekly cantata obligation had long since built its repertory and ran on revivals; the institutional wars had cooled into coexistence. Into the space those duties vacated came a project no employer had assigned — the complete statement of his art, genre by genre — and its opening exhibit arrived from the Nuremberg printer Schmid that fall: the fourth volume of the Clavier-Übung. It begins with an Aria, a serene sarabande floating over a thirty-two-bar bass, and what follows is thirty variations of a design keyboard music had never attempted.

The variations are built not on the Aria's melody but on the bass beneath it — the principle of the Chaconne, domesticated for the chamber and vastly extended. The architecture is crystalline. The thirty variations march in groups of three, and every third one is a canon — one voice strictly imitating another — at a successively wider interval: at the unison, then the second, then the third, out to the ninth, the discipline tightening notch by notch as the wheel turns. Then, at the place where the series promises its next canon, variation thirty breaks the pattern for a quodlibet: two folk songs, one of them about cabbage and turnips, braided together in counterpoint above the sacred bass. It is the old Bach-clan party trick — the improvised song-tangles of the family reunions — set like a piece of home ground at the summit of his most learned structure. And then the Aria returns, note for note unchanged, and, after everything the listener now knows is living in its bass, changed utterly.

The story everyone tells arrived sixty years later, in Forkel's biography of 1802. Count Keyserlingk — the Dresden envoy who had helped secure Bach his court title — suffered sleepless nights, the story goes, and commissioned clavier pieces "of such a gentle and somewhat lively character" that his house harpsichordist, the Bach-trained prodigy Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, might play them from the next room through the bad hours; Bach's reward was a goblet holding a hundred louis d'or. It is a perfect anecdote, and the timeline's honesty policy requires saying plainly what is wrong with it. The original print carries no dedication — unthinkable for a genuine aristocratic commission — and Goldberg, in 1741, was about fourteen years old. The verdict is tradition: probably the garbling of a true kernel, since Keyserlingk did own a copy and Goldberg surely played from it, but the world's favorite Bach anecdote is not a document. The work carries the name of a teenager who may have been no more than its most convenient performer.

There is a coda in Bach's own hand, and it waited two hundred and thirty-three years to be read. His personal copy of the print — the Handexemplar, rediscovered in 1974 — holds fourteen additional canons on the Aria's bass, BWV 1087, penned inside the back cover. Publication, in other words, settled nothing. After the engraver was paid and the volume was public property, the composer went on privately turning the same eight notes, finding canon after canon still folded inside them — a small, datable image of the whole late decade, in which finishing a thing and being finished with it were entirely different states.

This is the gateway to late Bach. The Goldberg Variations open the sequence the era essay lays out like a curriculum — variation raised to cosmology, with the second book of the Well-Tempered Clavier, the Musical Offering, the B minor Mass, and the Art of Fugue still to come — and they wear the late style's signature: encyclopedic rigor carried as delight. Their afterlife belongs to the timeline too. Two centuries on, it was Glenn Gould's 1955 recording that made "Goldberg" a household word — the name from a doubtful Dresden anecdote becoming, of all the words Bach left behind, one of the most famous.
