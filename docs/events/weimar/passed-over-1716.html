Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Passed over: the Kapellmeister job goes to the mediocre son

1716-12-01 · weimar
documented

Kapellmeister Johann Samuel Drese died on 1 December 1716, and with him died the polite fiction that had organized Weimar's music for years. Drese had been elderly and long ailing, his duties largely carried by others — not least by the Konzertmeister whose 1714 promotion had quietly priced him above the Kapellmeister he nominally served under. Now the top post stood open, and the succession should have been obvious. The court's own Konzertmeister was, by any measure then or since, the outstanding church musician in Germany: the man who had rebuilt the chapel's organ, supplied its gallery with two years of cantatas, and made Weimar's name among connoisseurs. Bach evidently thought the case obvious too, and he made it in the only medium he trusted. That Advent he produced cantatas on three consecutive Sundays — triple his contracted rate, an audible application for the post, composed at what the timeline can only call Leipzig-preview pace. It is one of the most poignant documents of the era precisely because it is not a document: a job application written entirely in music, by a man who believed the work would speak.

Duke Wilhelm Ernst appointed Drese's son. The vice-Kapellmeister, a man music history remembers for nothing else, stepped into his father's place, and the greatest church musician in Germany remained third chair at a minor court. Why? The honest answer stacks motives without being able to weigh them. Seniority — the son held the deputy post, and courts honored succession. Economy — a Drese came cheaper than a Bach who already out-earned the title. And, scholars suspect, displeasure: the inference (flagged as inference) that the elder duke was settling scores over Bach's well-known loyalties to the rival Rote Schloss household, choosing this moment to teach his organist the price of befriending the wrong palace. There had even been an intervening humiliation, for those keeping count: the post was reportedly first dangled toward Telemann — the godfather of Bach's own son — who did not pursue it. Weimar, offered the outstanding church musician in Germany, had shopped elsewhere first and then promoted the mediocrity anyway.

Bach's response is one of the loudest silences in the documentary record: from January 1717, his Weimar cantata production essentially ceases. No letter of protest survives, no petition, no recorded scene — just the output stopping, month after month, the contracted cantatas simply not appearing. A professional had taken precise inventory of what his employer valued and withheld exactly that. It is a strike in everything but name, conducted with the same wordless eloquence as the Advent application it answered, and it marks how completely the relationship had inverted: the court that could not imagine promoting him could now discover what his presence had been worth by experiencing his absence. He spent the silence usefully. Within months he had secured the Kapellmeistership at Cöthen — the title Weimar had refused him, from a prince who wanted him by name; within the year, having pressed his release too stubbornly, he was in the duke's jail; by Christmas 1717 he was gone.

For the positions track, this is the third firing of a pattern the timeline has been tracking since Arnstadt, and the most consequential. Arnstadt's consistory had answered his ambitions with reprimands; Halle had answered with terms he judged beneath the work; now Weimar answered the plainest merit case of the age with a mediocre insider. Institutions, offered the obvious, repeatedly chose the comfortable — and Bach repeatedly answered by leaving, which is the engine that moved him down the map: Arnstadt to Mühlhausen to Weimar to Cöthen and at last to Leipzig, each move powered by an employer's failure of nerve. It is tempting to read the pattern as tragedy, but the record suggests something drier: the eighteenth century's institutions were simply not built to recognize what they had, and Bach learned to treat their blindness as a fact of the terrain, like weather. Six years after Weimar's snub, a Leipzig councilman named Platz would sigh that since the best men could not be had, mediocre ones would have to do — the famous verdict of 1723. Platz merely said aloud what Weimar's appointment had already enacted. The difference is that by then, Bach was the mediocrity in question — and took the job.
