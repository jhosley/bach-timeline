Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Two dukes, one Capelle: the feud that framed everything

1712 · weimar
documented

Saxe-Weimar suffered a constitutional curiosity: two dukes ruled jointly — the elder, pious Wilhelm Ernst from the Wilhelmsburg palace, and his nephew Ernst August from the Rote Schloss a few streets away — and they loathed each other. The uncle held most of the power and used it pettily; the nephew nursed grievances and built his own cultural circle. The court musicians, paid mainly by the uncle but traditionally serving both households, lived in the crossfire: Wilhelm Ernst at various points forbade the Capelle to perform at the Rote Schloss, on pain of fines.

Bach was structurally disobedient. The Rote Schloss circle was the musically interesting one — above all the nephew's half-brother, the teenage Prince Johann Ernst, a genuinely gifted composer whom Bach (with Walther) taught, and whose Amsterdam music-shopping detonated the Vivaldi conversion. Bach's ties to the junior court were close and known; scholars read the trail of small salary slights and the fatal 1716 succession snub partly as the elder duke's displeasure at his organist's loyalties — and the ferocity of the 1717 jailing as its final expression. (The connecting motive is inference — flagged as such; the fines, the feud, and the friendships are all documented.)

Why it matters. The timeline's clearest case that court patronage was a political ecosystem, not a paycheck: the same structure that funded Bach's organ decade could destroy his prospects over a quarrel he didn't start. Musicians of his era navigated sovereigns' family feuds the way modern professionals navigate mergers — and sometimes lost.
