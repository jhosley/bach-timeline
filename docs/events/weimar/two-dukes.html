Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Two dukes, one Capelle: the feud that framed everything

1712 · weimar
documented

Every story of the Weimar years runs, sooner or later, into a fact of constitutional plumbing: Saxe-Weimar had two dukes at once. The duchy was ruled jointly — the elder, pious Wilhelm Ernst from the Wilhelmsburg palace, and his nephew Ernst August from the Rote Schloss a few streets away — and the two men loathed each other with the durable, well-fed hatred available only to relatives who cannot escape each other. The uncle held most of the actual power and used it pettily; the nephew nursed his grievances and built a cultural circle of his own in the junior palace. Neither could dissolve the arrangement, so the feud simply became the weather of the town: permanent, ambient, and hardest on whoever had to move between the two households.

That meant the musicians. The court Capelle was paid mainly by the uncle but by long tradition served both courts, and the players lived in the crossfire of a quarrel they had no part in making. Wilhelm Ernst at various points went so far as to forbid the Capelle to perform at the Rote Schloss at all, on pain of fines — a sovereign taxing his own employees for pleasing his own nephew. A musician in Weimar was thus never merely doing his job; every engagement was legible as a declaration of loyalty, and the elder duke was the kind of man who read the declarations carefully.

Bach was structurally disobedient, and it is worth being precise about why: the Rote Schloss circle was the musically interesting one. Its brightest light was the nephew's half-brother, the teenage Prince Johann Ernst — a genuinely gifted composer whom Bach, together with his cousin Walther, taught — and it was this prince whose music-shopping trip to the Netherlands detonated the Vivaldi conversion, the stylistic event on which the whole era turns. The most important artistic relationship of Bach's Weimar decade lived in the wrong palace. His ties to the junior court were close and they were known, which placed the duke's best musician, year after year, on the wrong side of the duke's dearest grudge.

What did it cost him? Here the card must be honest about where the documents stop and the reading begins. The fines, the feud, and the friendships are all documented; the connecting motive is inference, and it is flagged as such. But scholars read the record's trail of small salary slights, and above all the fatal succession snub of 1716 — when the Kapellmeister post went to a mediocrity rather than to the obvious candidate — as bearing the fingerprints of the elder duke's displeasure at his organist's loyalties. And when the era finally ended in a month in the duke's jail, the sheer ferocity of that response — a sovereign imprisoning a musician for asking too stubbornly to leave — reads plausibly as the feud's final expression: not anger at a resignation, but the settling of a long account. None of this is provable. All of it fits.

The reason this card sits on the shelf, though, is not the gossip but the lesson underneath it, because Weimar is the timeline's clearest demonstration that court patronage was a political ecosystem, not a paycheck. The same structure that funded Bach's organ decade — a pious duke's deep pockets, a standing Capelle, a rebuilt instrument — could destroy his prospects over a quarrel he didn't start and couldn't influence. There was no version of the job that stood outside the feud; taking the salary meant living inside the family. Musicians of Bach's era navigated their sovereigns' domestic wars the way modern professionals navigate mergers and boardroom coups: by reading signals, hedging loyalties, and hoping the reorganization lands gently. Sometimes it did not. Bach played the game imperfectly — or, more charitably, chose music over prudence, cultivating the palace where the interesting scores were rather than the palace where the power sat — and the two-duke trap closed on him by degrees: first the slights, then the snub, finally the cell. When he left for Cöthen at the end of 1717, he was exchanging, among other things, two quarreling masters for one who played the gamba. It is not hard to see the appeal.
