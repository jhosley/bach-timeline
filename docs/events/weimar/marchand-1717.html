The duel that wasn't: Marchand flees Dresden

1717-09-15 · dresden
tradition

In the autumn of 1717 — between the Cöthen appointment and the Weimar jail — Bach visited Dresden, the Saxon electoral capital and the most brilliant musical establishment in Germany. Also visiting: Louis Marchand, the famous French organist-harpsichordist, exiled from Versailles and trailing celebrity. Court musicians proposed the obvious entertainment: a keyboard contest — improvisation, sight-reading, the era's competitive disciplines — before the assembled nobility.

The story, as the Obituary and the Birnbaum defense tell it: Bach accepted; the appointed hour arrived; the company assembled — and Marchand did not. Inquiry found he had left Dresden that morning by express coach, having (the tradition insists) contrived to hear Bach practice the day before. Bach performed alone to general astonishment; the promised 500-thaler purse was allegedly embezzled by a court servant, so the victor of Germany's most famous keyboard non-duel went home unpaid.

Provenance, honestly. No Dresden document confirms the episode; every detail comes from Bach's circle, twenty-plus years later, deployed both times to defend his honor in print. Marchand's side was never heard. The scaffolding is plausible (both men verifiably in Dresden; the contest custom real), the dramaturgy suspiciously perfect — flagged tradition, with the note that even skeptical Williams grants a kernel.

Why it matters. True or embroidered, the story did historical work: it made Bach a national champion — German thoroughness besting French elegance — decades before nationalism claimed him in earnest (Forkel's 1802 biography, Arc II, leans on exactly this note). And it fixes his contemporary reputation precisely: to his own age Bach was, above all, the most terrifying performer alive. The composer's supremacy is posterity's discovery.
