Leipzig I: the cantata machine

The household in the school: family as workshop, and its costs

1725 · leipzig
documented

The cantor's apartment in the Thomasschule — expanded during the 1723 renovation, Bach's composing study adjoining the boarders' quarters (imagine the ambient noise the Passions were written against) — housed an enterprise as much as a family. Under one roof: the four surviving children of Maria Barbara; the growing brood of Anna Magdalena; private pupils, some boarding; visiting relatives on clan business; the copying workshop that turned scores into Sunday's parts; and one of Germany's great private music libraries (Bach's estate inventory proves its range).

In Leipzig Anna Magdalena bore thirteen children over some nineteen years — and buried seven of them, most in infancy, several in these very years of the cantata machine's full production. The juxtaposition is the era's hardest fact and the family track states it without ornament: while the outer cards show a masterwork a week, the parish registers show, again and again, a small coffin from the Thomasschule. (Biography resists causal claims and so does this card; but no one should study the Leipzig funeral music — Ich habe genug above all — ignorant of the address it came from.)

To Erdmann, Bach reckoned the household in a businessman's sentences with a father's pride at the center: the children are "born musicians," and — his one boast — he could already form a concert, vocal and instrumental, within his own family, his wife singing "a fine soprano."

Why it matters. The Leipzig output is inseparable from this domestic machine: unpaid skilled labor (copying, rehearsal), living quality control, and the emotional weather of constant birth and frequent death. The timeline's family track is not a sidebar to the works track here; it is its infrastructure.
