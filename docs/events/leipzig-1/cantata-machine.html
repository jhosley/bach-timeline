Leipzig I: the cantata machine

The cantata machine: a masterwork a week

1723-05-30 – 1725-06-01 · leipzig
documented

The first Sunday announced the scale of the ambition. On 30 May 1723, Bach's first cantata as the new cantor — Die Elenden sollen essen, BWV 75 — unfolded in fourteen movements, a double-length statement of intent. Then the calendar took over, and for roughly two years it never let go: a new cantata nearly every week, composed, copied, rehearsed, and performed inside seven days, then another, and another. Of perhaps three hundred written, some two hundred survive — the largest concentrated body of great music ever produced by one person, and it was made on a schedule that would break a modern film composer.

The week itself can be reconstructed from the surviving performing materials, and it is worth walking through slowly, because it is the invisible context of every cantata anyone will ever study. First the text, chosen or received, tied to the prescribed Gospel reading for that particular Sunday. Then composition — realistically Monday to Wednesday. Then the score passed to the household copying shop, where Anna Magdalena, the elder sons, and senior students extracted the performing parts. One rehearsal, likely Saturday. Performance Sunday morning, in the Thomaskirche or the Nikolaikirche in strict alternation, within a liturgy that began at seven o'clock and ran toward noon — the cantata standing as the sermon's musical twin, expounding the same Gospel the preacher would take up. Then Monday came, and the next text. Feast days added more; Christmas week could demand several new works within days. Dürr's chronology — built forensically from paper types, copyists' hands, and ink — demonstrates what the Obituary only asserts: cycle upon cycle, week upon week, there was no pause.

The first annual cycle, 1723–24, mixed new composition with revivals of his Weimar cantatas — the veteran housebuilder reusing sound timber, and no one should think less of him for it. The second cycle, running from Trinity 1724 to the spring of 1725, is the summit: the chorale cantata cycle, in which every work is built upon a single hymn — its melody anchoring the opening chorus, usually a vast chorale fantasia; its inner verses paraphrased into arias and recitatives; its final verse closing the work plainly, as the congregation knew it from the pews. Some forty of these, each structurally individual, each produced inside the same seven-day machine. By common consent it stands among the supreme sustained achievements of Western art, and — the fact that resists comprehension — it was workflow.

That is why this card asks to be read before any single cantata. Inside the machine, the categories we prefer to keep separate — craft and inspiration, formula and invention — simply dissolve. The deadline forbade the distinction. The formulas, the models, the parody adaptations, the chorale's ready-made scaffolding: these were not shortcuts around genius but the conditions under which genius could deliver by Sunday. John Eliot Gardiner, who performed the cantatas on their own liturgical calendar across a single year — the Bach Cantata Pilgrimage of 2000 — and so felt the cycle's rhythm from the inside as few musicians ever have, calls the second cycle's consistency simply beyond explanation. He is a witness worth crediting: he tried living at the tempo, with modern resources, and came away astonished.

Hold the picture, then: a composing study beside a boys' dormitory, a household bent over copying tables, a Saturday rehearsal, a Sunday liturgy — and inside that ordinary municipal machinery, week after week for two years, and at high intensity for five, half-hour masterworks emerging like harvest. The catalog will eventually walk the cycle cantata by cantata; this card exists so that when it does, the reader never mistakes any single work for a solitary act of inspiration. Each one was made inside the machine — and the machine itself, more than any single Sunday's product, may be the most astonishing thing Bach ever built.
