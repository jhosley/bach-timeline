Leipzig I: the cantata machine

Good Friday 1724: the St. John Passion

1724-04-07 · leipzig
documented

The tradition Bach inherited was younger than most of his students: concerted Passion music at Leipzig's Good Friday Vespers had existed for barely a decade when, on 7 April 1724, ten months into the job, he mounted his first — the St. John Passion. Even the venue came with a lesson in his new life. He had planned the performance for the Thomaskirche and said so in print; the council corrected his announcement and the work sounded at the Nikolaikirche instead — a small humiliation, an early taste of the jurisdictional weather in which everything he did in Leipzig would be made.

The form itself was a high-wire act. John's Passion narrative is sung complete by an Evangelist in recitative; the crowd's words erupt from the choir; and at chosen moments the action is suspended for contemplative arias and chorales — the congregation's own hymns folded into the drama. These are opera's tools, and Bach's pledge to the council had expressly forbidden music "operatic in character"; the Passion genre lived exactly on that line, deploying the theater's machinery on the central story of the faith while never being permitted to look like theater. Part of the John Passion's permanent controversy — it has been argued over ever since — begins right there.

John's Gospel gave Bach a specific drama, and it is worth being precise about which one. This narrative is swift and confrontational, its Christ sovereign even in suffering, and the music matches it at every level. The opening chorus, Herr, unser Herrscher, churns over an orchestral surface of extraordinary turbulence — nothing serene about it, a vortex rather than a portal. The crowd choruses of the trial scene, the turba movements, are set with a ferocity nothing else in the era approaches, the mob's voices interlocking with terrible energy. And the whole is engineered as an arch of keys and reprises pivoting on the trial — architecture doing the work that staging would do in an opera house, invisibly.

Bach never stopped rebuilding the work. At least four versions survive — the 1725 revival already wore a different frame, later performances restored and re-revised the score — and the last revision was left partially complete at his death, which tells you the piece never left his workbench or his mind. And one part of its afterlife must be named plainly, because the timeline does not look away from it: the trial scene sets John's words about "the Jews" verbatim, and the work's modern performance carries a necessary reckoning with that text's long and painful history. This card flags it; the catalog card treats it honestly and at length, as the subject requires.

What Good Friday 1724 demonstrated, ten months into the cantorate, was something Leipzig could not yet have known: that the weekly cantata machine could scale. The same workshop that delivered a half-hour work every Sunday could, when the one open evening of the liturgical year arrived, deliver monumental drama — an evening-filling structure of narrative, commentary, and congregational song, produced by the same household of copyists on the same unforgiving calendar. It was the opening of the Passion thread that runs through this whole timeline: culminating three years later in the St. Matthew Passion, falling silent through decades of neglect, and then, in 1829, providing the very music whose revival would resurrect its composer's name. The tradition was barely ten years old when Bach joined it; he is the reason anyone remembers it existed.
