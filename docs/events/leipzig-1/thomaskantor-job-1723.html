Leipzig I: the cantata machine

The job: cantor, teacher, director, tenant, servant of the council

1723-06-01 · leipzig
documented

Bach was formally installed as Cantor of the Thomasschule on the first of June, 1723, and with that ceremony began the strangest employment arrangement of his life. A month earlier he had signed a fourteen-point pledge to the Leipzig town council, and the document repays reading clause by clause, because everything that happened over the following twenty-seven years is latent in it. He promised to live soberly. He promised to treat the schoolboys kindly. He promised not to leave town without the burgomaster's permission — a clause worth pausing over, since the man signing it had lately been a prince's Kapellmeister. He promised to keep the church music "not too long, nor... operatic in character, but rather incite the listeners to devotion." And he promised to teach, including the Latin instruction that came with the post — which he immediately, with the council's consent, hired a deputy to cover out of his own pocket. The greatest composer alive began his Leipzig tenure by paying a colleague to do part of his job, and the council let him, and both facts tell you something about the arrangement.

What had he actually signed on to be? Not one thing but four, and the four never stopped colliding. He was a schoolmaster — fourth master of a boarding school of some fifty-five scholarship boys, who were also his singers, admitted by his examination and drilled in his rehearsals: the same choral-scholarship machinery that had once fed and educated the young Bach himself at Lüneburg, now seen from the other side of the desk. He was director musices of Leipzig, responsible for music in the city's four churches every Sunday and feast day. He was a civic official, supplying music for council elections and university occasions. And he was, not least, his employer's tenant: the family lived in the school building itself, renovated around them, so that the cantor raised his children, wrote his masterpieces, and fought his battles under the council's own roof.

The finances had the same layered oddity. The fixed salary was around a hundred thalers — modest for the work — while the real income, something near seven hundred, came from Accidentien, the fees attached to weddings and funerals. Bach's livelihood thus depended, quite literally, on Leipzig's mortality, an arrangement he described to his friend Erdmann years later with mordant precision: when the air was healthy, the fees fell. It is the grimmest joke in his surviving correspondence, and it was also plain accounting.

Above him sat not one authority but three — town council, church consistory, university — their jurisdictions tangled together, so that every reform he might attempt required a fight, and every fight came ready-supplied with a venue. That structure explains the next quarter-century better than any theory of temperament does. Bach was not simply a difficult employee, whatever the minutes would later say; he was a man of enormous purpose installed in a post engineered for friction, a prince's officer turned town servant, answerable everywhere and sovereign nowhere.

This is the baseline document of the whole Leipzig story, and the reader should hold its shape in mind through everything that follows. The great memorandum of 1730, in which Bach itemized for the council what his art actually required; the two-year war over the choir prefects; the patient campaign for a Dresden court title to hold as armor against local harassment — every one of them unfolds from the terms signed in May 1723. The plainest way to say it is the truest: the greatest composer alive had taken a job as a middle manager, with three boards above him and a dormitory of teenagers below, and both parties would spend three decades discovering, memo by memo, exactly what that meant.
