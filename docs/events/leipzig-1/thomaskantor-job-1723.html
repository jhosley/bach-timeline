Leipzig I: the cantata machine

The job: cantor, teacher, director, tenant, servant of the council

1723-06-01 · leipzig
documented

Bach was formally installed as Cantor of the Thomasschule on 1 June 1723, having signed a fourteen-point pledge whose clauses repay reading: he would live soberly, treat the boys kindly, not leave town without the burgomaster's permission, keep the music "not too long, nor... operatic in character, but rather incite the listeners to devotion," and teach — the Latin instruction he immediately, with council consent, hired a deputy to cover from his own pocket.

The structure of the post explains the next twenty-seven years of friction. He was simultaneously: schoolmaster in a boarding school of ~55 scholarship boys (his singers — admitted, examined, and drilled by him, the same Mettenchor machine he had sung in at Lüneburg); director musices responsible for four churches every Sunday and feast; civic official supplying council-election and university music; and tenant of his employer, raising his family in the school building. His fixed salary was around 100 thalers — the ~700-thaler real income came from Accidentien, fees for weddings and funerals, a dependency he described to Erdmann with mordant precision: when the air was healthy, fees fell.

Above him sat not one authority but three, jurisdictionally tangled — council, church consistory, university — a design guaranteeing that every reform required a fight and every fight had a venue.

Why it matters. The baseline document of the whole Leipzig positions track. Every later card — the 1730 memo, the prefect war, the Dresden title-hunting — unfolds from this pledge's structure: the greatest composer alive had signed on as a middle manager, and both parties spent three decades discovering what that meant.
