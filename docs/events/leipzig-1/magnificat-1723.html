Leipzig I: the cantata machine

First Christmas: the Magnificat

1723-12-25 · leipzig
documented

For Vespers on his first Leipzig Christmas Day, Bach produced his most jewel-cut large work: the Magnificat — Mary's canticle from Luke, in Latin (Leipzig kept Latin for high feasts), for five-part choir, festival orchestra with trumpets and drums, and soloists, in twelve compact movements with not a wasted bar. The first version (E-flat, BWV 243a) interleaved four Christmas interpolations — little carols nested between the canticle's movements, a Leipzig tradition — which Bach removed when he revised the work into its familiar D major about a decade later.

For the congregation, the piece announced what the new cantor meant by festival music: text-painting of almost theatrical vividness (omnes generationes — "all generations" — piling up in relentless fugal entries; the proud dispersit superbos scattered across the choir; Suscepit Israel's ancient chant floating over the trebles), massive architecture in miniature spans, and the five-voice richness he reserved for the grandest statements (the B minor Mass will make the same choice).

Why it matters. The Magnificat is the study-sized door into Bach's large choral art — every technique of the Passions and the Mass present in a thirty-minute frame — and its two versions make a tidy case study in his lifelong revising habit (the R3 card will compare them). Biographically it marks the first Christmas of the Leipzig pattern: the machine's ordinary weekly output punctuated, at the year's hinges, by works of deliberate monumentality.
