Ohrdruf: the brother's apprentice

Top of the class: the Lyceum record

1697 · ohrdruf
documented

The Ohrdruf years are the dimmest stretch of Bach's biography, and in the middle of them one documentary lamp burns steadily: the Lyceum kept registers, and the registers survive. They record that Sebastian, arriving as a grieving ten-year-old within months of burying both parents, ranked fourth in his class almost at once — and thereafter stood first or second, year after year, while remaining consistently among the youngest boys in each class, two to three years junior to the classmates he was outperforming and promoted faster than the norm. This is the only hard evidence we possess of Bach's general education, and it quietly demolishes a lazy picture: the boy was not a musical savant with a neglected mind. By the one set of school records history left us, he was an excellent student of everything.

What "everything" meant is worth spelling out, because the curriculum was serious. Latin, taught through Cicero and the formal discipline of rhetoric; the Greek New Testament; Lutheran theology by way of Leonhard Hutter's dense doctrinal compendium; arithmetic; history. A provincial town school, but an ambitious one, drilling its boys in the classical machinery of learned argument — and this particular boy absorbed it near the top of every class he entered.

Two consequences reach far beyond Ohrdruf. The first is practical. Decades later the Leipzig cantorate would require its holder to teach Latin, and Bach would spend years negotiating — and quarreling — with a town council and a rector as a man of learning among the university-trained; the era essay notes he even dueled Leipzig's rector over Latin teaching duties. The confidence for all of that was banked here, in a classroom where he had proven he could hold his own with anyone on academic ground. The second consequence is deeper, and it runs straight into the music. The rhetorical training he received — inventio, dispositio, elaboratio: finding an idea, arranging it, developing it — is precisely the vocabulary Bach's own circle later reached for when they tried to describe how he composed, and the title of the Inventions is borrowed straight from it. He was taught to build arguments before he was taught to build fugues, and the fugues show it. When the Craft essays in this timeline describe a fugue as an argument — a proposition found, arranged, and developed to its conclusion — that is not a metaphor imposed from outside. It is the education talking.

One more line in the record deserves its weight. Throughout these years Sebastian earned money as a chorister: the school choir sang for fees and alms, and he sang in it for his keep. From age ten to the end of his formal education he was a working student, paid for musical service — which means that music as livelihood, as a trade with wages and obligations, was never for a single year of his life an abstraction. The man who would later negotiate salaries, perquisites, and fee schedules with the precision of a guild craftsman learned the economics of the profession at the same desks where he learned his Cicero.

Christoph Wolff titled the standard modern biography The Learned Musician, and the whole argument of that title starts here, in a provincial classroom's Latin drills and theology recitations. The learned counterpoint of the later eras — the music that struck contemporaries as the work of a scholar as much as a performer — did not appear from nowhere in Weimar or Leipzig. It was seeded in the one period of Bach's life for which we hold actual report cards, and the report cards say: first or second in his class, years younger than everyone around him, and singing for his supper the entire time.
