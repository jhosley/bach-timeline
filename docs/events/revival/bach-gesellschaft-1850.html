The Rediscovery: 1829–1850

The centenary act: founding the Bach-Gesellschaft

1850-07-28 · leipzig
documented

On 28 July 1850 — one hundred years to the day after the death in the Thomasschule apartment — a society constituted itself in Leipzig with a single stated object: to publish the complete works of Johann Sebastian Bach, critically edited from the sources, without arrangement or "improvement." Among the founders and first subscribers: Robert Schumann (whose journal had campaigned for exactly this for a decade), Moscheles, Otto Jahn; Mendelssohn, the natural president, had died three years earlier — his revival work (and the Leipzig Bach monument he personally financed in 1843) was the enterprise's visible foundation.

The undertaking consumed fifty years and forty-six volumes (1851–1899), and it is hard to overstate what it changed. The manuscripts scattered in 1750 were hunted, collated, and fixed in print; the two hundred surviving cantatas — all but one unpublished in Bach's lifetime — entered the public record; the B minor Mass and the unfinished Art of Fugue took their places as monuments. The edition's opening page gave Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern its immortal number, and its completion moved Brahms — a subscriber who studied each volume as it arrived — to rank it with the founding of the German Empire among his era's great events (a comparison that says everything about the national frame the whole project ran inside).

Every later instrument of Bach scholarship descends from this act: the BWV catalog numbers this timeline uses, the Neue Bach-Ausgabe that re-edited everything in the twentieth century, Bach Digital's manuscript database — and every complete-cantata recording of Arc III is the Gesellschaft's shelf, performed.

Why it matters. Arc II ends where custody becomes institution: what one family lost, one nation's scholarship rebuilt — and from 1850 forward, Bach's survival never again depended on any single heir, attic, or accident. The timeline's ribbon, from here, only brightens.
