The Rediscovery: 1829–1850

The centenary act: founding the Bach-Gesellschaft

1850-07-28 · leipzig
documented

On 28 July 1850 — one hundred years to the day after the death in the Thomasschule apartment — a society constituted itself in Leipzig with a single stated object: to publish the complete works of Johann Sebastian Bach, critically edited from the sources, without arrangement or "improvement." Every word of that charter was a quiet manifesto. Complete: not the famous works, not a selection for practical use — everything. From the sources: the scattered manuscripts themselves, hunted down and compared, not the corrupted copies in circulation. Without improvement: after a century of arrangers helpfully modernizing whatever Bach they touched, the music would finally appear as written. The centenary of the composer's death became the founding date of the institution that would end, permanently, the era in which his survival was left to chance.

The founding roster tells the story of the revival's second generation taking up the first's unfinished work. Among the founders and first subscribers stood Robert Schumann, whose critical journal had campaigned for exactly this edition for a decade; Moscheles; the scholar Otto Jahn. The natural president was missing: Mendelssohn had died three years earlier, and the enterprise rose visibly on the foundation he had built — the 1829 resurrection and its long Leipzig aftermath, down to the Bach monument near the Thomaskirche that he personally financed in 1843. The Gesellschaft was, among other things, the revival outliving its hero and becoming a system.

The undertaking consumed fifty years and forty-six volumes, from 1851 to 1899, and it is hard to overstate what those volumes changed. The manuscripts scattered among the heirs in 1750 — the dispersal through which so much had already been lost — were hunted, collated, and fixed in print where no further accident could reach them. The two hundred surviving cantatas, all but a single one unpublished in Bach's lifetime, entered the public record at last; the B minor Mass and the unfinished Art of Fugue took their places as public monuments rather than connoisseurs' rumors. The edition's opening page gave Wie schön leuchtet der Morgenstern its immortal number — BWV 1, the first entry in what became the catalog this whole timeline navigates by. And the project found its ideal reader as it went: Brahms, a subscriber who studied each volume as it arrived, ranked the edition's completion with the founding of the German Empire among the great events of his era — a comparison that says everything about the national frame, built by Forkel half a century before, inside which the entire enterprise ran. Even at its scholarly summit, the Bach revival was still a national project, and knew itself as one.

Every later instrument of Bach scholarship descends from this act. The BWV numbers on every card here; the Neue Bach-Ausgabe, which re-edited everything in the twentieth century against the Gesellschaft's baseline; Bach Digital's manuscript database, the old society's hunt continued online — each is a direct descendant. So, in performance, is more than we usually notice: every complete-cantata recording of Arc III is the Gesellschaft's shelf, performed — a repertoire that exists as a performable whole only because forty-six volumes made it one.

This is where Arc II properly ends, and the ending has a shape worth naming: custody became institution. For a hundred years Bach's survival had depended on individuals — an heir's carefulness or fecklessness, a collector's salon, a grandmother's gift — every link in the chain a single point of failure, and several links had already failed, taking cantata cycles and a Passion with them. From 28 July 1850 forward, that was over. What one family lost, one nation's scholarship rebuilt, and never again would the music's existence hang on any single heir, attic, or accident. The timeline's ribbon, dimmed in 1750 and reignited in 1829, from here only brightens.
