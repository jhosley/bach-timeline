The Rediscovery: 1829–1850

A birthday present: the copyist's score of the Matthew Passion

1824 · berlin
documented

The revival's founding object is a birthday present. Around 1823–24, Bella Salomon — Felix Mendelssohn's grandmother, of the banking family whose Berlin salon culture had kept "ancient music" alive for a generation — commissioned a complete manuscript copy of the St. Matthew Passion (from the materials in Zelter's Singakademie orbit) and gave it to her grandson: fourteen or fifteen years old, already a prodigious composer, already Zelter-trained in the strict old counterpoint.

Consider what the gift assumes: the greatest sacred work of the previous century existed only in manuscript, its reproduction a bespoke luxury purchase — and a Jewish-descended banking matriarch of Berlin was the one commissioning it, for a boy. (The Mendelssohn family's complex position — Moses Mendelssohn's grandchildren, converted to Protestantism, at once inside and outside the culture claiming Bach as national patrimony — sits inside this scene, and Felix's later wry remark about "an actor and a Jew's son" reviving the great Christian music shows he knew it precisely.)

Felix lived with the score for five years — studying it, playing it at the family's Sunday musicales, gathering around it a cell of enthusiasts: above all Eduard Devrient, the baritone (and actor) whose memoirs preserve the campaign — the two young men in 1829 talking the skeptical old Zelter into lending his choir, his hall, and his blessing to the performance the next card records. Devrient sang Jesus; Felix conducted from memory.

Why it matters. Arc II's relay — students to salon, salon to prodigy — reaches its terminal handoff: from custody to performance. And the object lesson stands for the whole reception story: revolutions of taste begin as private objects — a copied score, a gift, a room of friends — years before they have a public date.
