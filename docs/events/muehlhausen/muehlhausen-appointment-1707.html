Mühlhausen: the mission statement

Organist of Divi Blasii — negotiating like a Bach

1707-06-15 · muehlhausen
documented

The post came open the way posts usually opened in Bach's Germany: the organist of Divi Blasii died in late 1706, and the city went looking. Mühlhausen was worth wanting — a free imperial city answering to no duke, its council sovereign — and the bench at St. Blasius was one of Thuringia's venerable seats. At Easter 1707 the twenty-two-year-old organist of Arnstadt's Neue Kirche came to audition. What he played is not recorded, though scholars have long suspected Christ lag in Todes Banden, BWV 4 — that stark masterpiece of the old chorale-variation type, stylistically perfect for the date and the place; the association is inference, not document, and should be held as such. What the surviving council proceedings preserve instead is, in its way, just as revealing as any cantata: the sound of a very young man negotiating without a flicker of deference.

The council had deliberated in his favor, and asked his terms. He required the same 85 gulden he made in Arnstadt — itself a raise on what the Mühlhausen post had previously paid — and then the perquisites, itemized with the unhurried precision of a man who knew exactly how a household ran: three measures of grain, two cords of wood, six times threescore faggots of kindling, three pounds of fish a year, and a wagon to move his belongings. The council agreed to everything. On 15 June 1707 he signed.

The scene deserves more than a sentence, because it repeats, with variations, at every job change for the rest of his life. Bach knew his market value with unsentimental precision, stated it plainly, and — this is the leverage of demonstrated excellence — generally received it. He was twenty-two, with no reputation beyond what the clan's name and his own playing supplied; but the playing had evidently settled the question before the bargaining began, and he negotiated like a man who knew it. In an economy where a musician's pay came partly in grain and firewood, the itemized list is not pettiness but fluency: he understood what a position was actually worth, down to the kindling. The one great exception to the pattern came at the far end of the road, in Leipzig, whose fee-dependent package he would grumble about to Erdmann for years; everywhere else, the asking and the getting align with almost comic reliability.

Notice, too, who sat across the table. Not a duke, not a prince, but a town council — sovereign, committee-minded, budget-anxious — and this was Bach's first contact with the employer-type of his destiny. Everything about how he handled Mühlhausen's burghers previews the long Leipzig marriage to come: firm on money, meticulous on the organ project (he would design the St. Blasius rebuild himself, in an engineering memo that survives stop by stop), and gracious in exit. The preview lacks only the acrimony, which this once never arrived. A free city's politics were a different weather system from a court's — decisions reached by minutes and votes rather than a patron's mood — and at his first exposure Bach navigated them cleanly.

He closed out Arnstadt as correctly as he had opened Mühlhausen, returning his keys honorably; and the clan machinery engaged as it always did, for he arranged his own succession — his cousin Johann Ernst Bach took over the Neue Kirche bench behind him. The family had been staffing Thuringia's organ lofts for generations, and a Bach vacancy was, by long custom, a Bach opportunity; Sebastian simply operated the system from the inside, as he had been raised to do.

So the appointment card earns its place not for what happened afterward at Divi Blasii — the cantata and the resignation have their own cards — but for what it establishes about the man at the threshold of his adult career: a musician who could measure his own worth in a market with no formal way of measuring it, and who treated a sovereign council as a negotiating partner rather than a benefactor. The confidence was not bravado; it was an accurate self-assessment, backed by the one currency that never failed him. Asked his price, he named it — in gulden, grain, wood, and fish — and the free imperial city paid.
