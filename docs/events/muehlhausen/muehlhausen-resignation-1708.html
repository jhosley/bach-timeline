Mühlhausen: the mission statement

The Endzweck letter: 'a well-regulated church music to the glory of God'

1708-06-25 · muehlhausen
documented

In June 1708 Bach played for Duke Wilhelm Ernst at Weimar and came away with an offer: court organist and chamber musician at roughly double his Mühlhausen package. His letter of resignation to the council, dated 25 June 1708, survives complete — and it is the closest thing to a personal manifesto Bach ever wrote.

He thanks the town; he itemizes, without naming names, the obstruction — he had sought to advance church music "without vexation," but had met "hindrance," and conditions gave no sign that a better state of affairs was coming (the orthodox–Pietist friction, in careful civil-service prose). Then the sentence around which biographers build the man: he had always kept in view one Endzweck — final purpose — namely "a well-regulated church music to the glory of God" (eine regulirte Kirchenmusik zu Gottes Ehren), and where he could not pursue it, he must go where he could.

Unpack "well-regulated": not a mood but a program — competent professional forces, adequate funding, systematic repertoire for the whole church year, the institutional machinery of excellence. It is the same demand he makes of Leipzig in the famous 1730 memorandum, a lifetime later, nearly word for word. The letter announces both the mission and, with eerie precision, the shape of every conflict to come: Bach's employers repeatedly hired the organist or cantor and got, unbargained-for, the program.

The council released him with honor, kept him as consultant on the organ rebuild he'd specified, and invited him back to perform the following year's council cantata. No Bach departure was ever cleaner — a fact worth registering before Weimar, where the leaving ends in a jail cell.

Why it matters. If the timeline had to keep a single sentence of Bach's prose, it keeps this one. The Endzweck is the thesis of Arc I; the B minor Mass is its final proof.
