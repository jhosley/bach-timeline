Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Six Partitas (Clavier-Übung I)

BWV 825–830 · B-flat, c, a, D, G, e · 1731
documented
Forces — Harpsichord
Occasion — Clavier-Übung I — the publishing debut
Dating — Issued singly 1726–30, collected 1731 as Bach's self-declared Opus 1.
Recommended recording — Murray Perahia, Bach: Partitas (Sony)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The suite's third and final ascent — after the French Suites' parlor and the English Suites' concert manner, the Partitas are the genre's public summit: each opens with a differently-titled portal (Praeludium, Sinfonia, Fantasia, Ouverture, Praeambulum, Toccata — six doors, no two alike, the encyclopedic tic even in the table of contents), and the dances within stretch their frames toward character-piece freedom. Bach chose this as Opus 1 — his printed introduction to the world, at forty-one.

Listen for. No. 1 in B-flat: the charmer (its Praeludium every pianist's first Partita page). No. 2 in C minor: the dark drama — its closing Capriccio replacing the gigue with leaping wit. No. 4 in D: grandeur — the French-overture opening and the long, aristocratic Allemande, possibly the most beautiful he wrote. No. 6 in E minor: the summa — a Toccata with fugue built in, a Sarabande dissolved past dance into pure rhetoric, a gigue in fierce dotted counterpoint. A contemporary (Forkel's report) said these pieces made "as brilliant an effect in the musical world as ever" — Bach's one fashionable success in print.

Craft. The R4 dance essay's terminal exhibit: what remains of a dance when every convention has been transcended and the step still, somehow, holds.

Why it matters. The composer's own opening statement to posterity — start the keyboard suites here or end here; either way, this is the destination.
