What it is. Bach's last organ publication: six chorale settings engraved by his sometime student Schübler — and five of them are transcriptions of his own cantata movements, the church-year's vocal treasures repackaged for any organist's Sunday. The most famous: BWV 645 — the Wachet auf tenor-chorale, its striding string countermelody now an organ right hand, on its way to becoming the best-known organ chorale in the world.

Listen for. With the cantata originals beside them (all on this timeline), the transcriber's decisions: what survives the loss of words, which voice gets which manual, where idiom bends. BWV 645 barely changes a note — Bach knew that movement was already perfect furniture for any room.

Craft. Parody's late, public face: where the Christmas Oratorio re-texted secular into sacred, here sacred vocal becomes instrumental — the same conviction that music's substance survives its medium.

Why it matters. A shrewd old man's bet on posterity: the cantatas were unpublishable (economics, again), but six of their best minutes could ride out into the world on organ paper — and did: through the eclipse, organists kept Wachet auf alive without knowing the cantata existed. A message in a bottle that arrived.
