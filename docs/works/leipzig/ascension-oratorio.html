Ascension Oratorio (Lobet Gott in seinen Reichen)

BWV 11 · D major · 1735
documented

What it is. The compact third oratorio — Ascension Day 1735, Evangelist narration in the Christmas Oratorio's manner, festival D-major frame — a two-part, half-hour work whose center of gravity is one aria.

Listen for. That aria: Ach, bleibe doch, mein liebstes Leben — the alto begging the departing Christ to stay, over a bass that keeps slipping away underneath. Bach returned to this music at the end of his life and re-carved it as the Agnus Dei of the B minor Mass — and comparing the two versions, note for note, is among the most intimate views available of his revising mind: the later version removes material, tightening grief into prayer. (The catalog treats it as the third great long-range self-borrowing, beside BWV 12 → Crucifixus.)

Craft. The closing chorale fantasia sets the hymn against an orchestral texture of almost concerto brilliance — the late-1730s Bach letting the congregation's tune ride an instrumental machine at full gallop.

Why it matters. Small door, large rooms: half an hour that opens onto the parody workshop, the Mass's final pages, and the question the R4 essays keep asking — what did Bach consider a piece of music to be, that it could serve a Leipzig feast day and eternity with the same notes?
