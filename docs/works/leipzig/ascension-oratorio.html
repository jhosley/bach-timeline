The Ascension Oratorio, Lobet Gott in seinen Reichen, is the compact third member of Bach's oratorio family — written for Ascension Day 1735, with Evangelist narration in the Christmas Oratorio's manner and the same festival D-major frame, three trumpets and drums included. It is a two-part work lasting about half an hour, and by the standards of its siblings it can look like a footnote: no six-evening span, no operatic experiment, just a feast-day oratorio done supremely well. What earns it a card of its own is that its center of gravity is a single aria — and what happened to that aria afterward.

The aria is Ach, bleibe doch, mein liebstes Leben: the alto begging the departing Christ to stay, over a bass line that keeps slipping away underneath. The scoring enacts the theology — the voice reaches, and the ground it reaches from keeps receding — and on the feast of the Ascension the point could not be missed: this is what it feels like to watch something beloved rise slowly, irreversibly, out of reach. Heard on its own terms, on Ascension Day 1735, it is one of the great arias of the Leipzig years, complete and sufficient. But Bach was not done with it. At the end of his life he returned to this music and re-carved it as the Agnus Dei of the B minor Mass — the final aria of his final monument.

That transformation makes this half-hour work one of the most valuable study objects in the catalog. Comparing the two versions note for note is among the most intimate views available of Bach's revising mind, and the direction of the revision is the surprise: the later version removes material. Where we might expect a composer monumentalizing his life's work to elaborate, Bach cuts — tightening grief into prayer, paring the aria's pleading toward something sparer and more inward. The catalog treats this as the third great long-range self-borrowing, standing beside the journey of BWV 12 into the Mass's Crucifixus: music written for a specific Leipzig occasion, carried across years, and found worthy of the summa — but only after the knife.

The rest of the work rewards attention too. The closing chorale fantasia sets the hymn against an orchestral texture of almost concerto brilliance — the late-1730s Bach letting the congregation's tune ride an instrumental machine at full gallop. The technique is the Christmas Oratorio's chorale-thinking pushed to a festive extreme: the plainest, most communal layer of Lutheran music-making — the hymn everyone knew — mounted on top of the most virtuosic thing an orchestra of the day could do, neither element yielding anything to the other. It makes a fitting frame for the work's central grief: the oratorio that contains Bach's tenderest music of departure ends with the congregation's own tune carried upward on full orchestral wings.

This card is a small door into large rooms. Half an hour of music opens onto the parody workshop and its ethics of reuse; onto the final pages of the Mass, where this aria's second life awaits; and onto the question the craft essays keep circling back to — what did Bach consider a piece of music to be, that it could serve a Leipzig feast day and eternity with the same notes? The Ascension Oratorio suggests his answer was untroubled: the music written for the departing Christ of Ascension Day 1735 was, with revision and re-texting, the right music for the Lamb of God, full stop. Occasion was where a piece started, not what it was. A listener who takes this work first, then follows the alto aria into the Agnus Dei with the score's differences in ear, will understand Bach's late relationship to his own past work better than any essay can teach it.

Follow the aria to its destination: the Mass in B minor — and the narrative manner back to its source: the Christmas Oratorio.
