Leipzig III: the summing-up

The Great Eighteen (Leipzig Chorales)

BWV 651–668 · · 1745

What it is. In the 1740s, alongside the Mass and the Art of Fugue, Bach fair-copied a legacy collection of his great Weimar chorale fantasias, revised — the large-scale settings the Orgelbüchlein's miniatures deliberately weren't: expansive, multi-verse, the hymn examined at cathedral length.

Listen for. An Wasserflüssen Babylon (BWV 653) — the very chorale the teenager copied at Böhm's and the master improvised on for Reincken, here fixed in final form: one melody, a whole life's thread. The two Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland settings — the ornamented one dissolving Advent's hymn into arabesque grief. Schmücke dich — Mendelssohn wept over it; Schumann reported it. And last in the manuscript, BWV 668, Vor deinen Thron tret ich hiermit ("Before thy throne I now appear") — the deathbed-dictation legend's object, entered in another hand when Bach's eyes failed: the collection ends, literally, at the throne.

Craft. The definitive revision corpus: Weimar originals survive for comparison — thirty years of second thoughts, visible note by note. The R4 essays mine it for what "better" meant to late Bach (almost always: barer).

Why it matters. The organ thread's true summa — youth's material, age's judgment, one manuscript — and the most moving object in the instrumental catalog.
