What it is. The mightiest of the keyboard concertos — dark, driving D minor on a symphonic scale that made it the nineteenth century's favorite (Mendelssohn and Brahms both championed it): the one Bach concerto that sounds like it knows Beethoven is coming.

Listen for. The opening ritornello's unison growl — the whole orchestra as one fist — and the solo part's escalating fury, including near-cadenza eruptions over drumming pedal points. The slow movement: a grave ground-bass aria, the keyboard singing ornament-laden grief above it. The finale's momentum — and everywhere, the reconstruction game: violinists play a back-converted "violin concerto in D minor" from this music; hear both and judge the scholars' case yourself.

Why it matters. Its movements also served as organ-solo cantata sinfonias (BWV 146/188) — one music, three costumes (church organ, coffee-house harpsichord, hypothetical violin): the timeline's tidiest proof that for Bach a concerto was an idea, not an instrumentation.
