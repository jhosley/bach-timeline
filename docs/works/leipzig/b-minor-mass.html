Leipzig III: the summing-up

Mass in B minor

BWV 232 · B minor / D major · 1749
documented
Forces — Five-part choir (to eight), festival orchestra, soloists
Occasion — None documented — the summa
Dating — Assembled 1748–49 from the 1733 Dresden Missa, the 1724 Sanctus, and movements parodied across three decades; the earliest source (Crucifixus) reaches back to 1714.
Recommended recording — John Eliot Gardiner, Monteverdi Choir & English Baroque Soloists, Mass in B minor (SDG)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The complete Latin Mass Ordinary at maximal scale — two hours, twenty-seven movements — assembled in Bach's last working years from the finest sacred music of his whole life, re-texted and perfected (the event card tells the biographical story). Not written for any service he could attend; never heard whole in his lifetime.

Listen for. The opening: a five-bar B-minor cry of Kyrie — then the vast fugue that spends eleven minutes earning it. The architecture of styles: Renaissance stile antico (Credo's opening, Confiteor) beside high-Baroque concerto choruses (Gloria, Et resurrexit) beside the most modern galant duets — a deliberate museum of sacred music's whole history, curated by its master. The Crucifixus: a chaconne on a grinding chromatic ground (born in Weimar, 1714), sinking at its end into burial's E minor — out of which Et resurrexit detonates without a breath. And the ending: Dona nobis pacem re-uses the Gratias fugue — the Mass closes by giving thanks and asking peace with the same music, the parody principle elevated into theology.

Craft. Every R4 essay lands here eventually: fugue, canon (Confiteor's chant-in-canon), chorale-thinking without chorales, parody as transfiguration, number and symmetry (the debates dealt with soberly).

Reception thread. Emanuel performed the Credo in 1786; the nineteenth century built it into "the greatest work of music of all ages and all peoples" (the Swiss publisher Nägeli, announcing the first edition); today it is the work by which everything else — Bach's included — gets measured.
