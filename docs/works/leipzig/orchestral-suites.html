Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

The Four Orchestral Suites (Ouvertüren)

BWV 1066–1069 · C, b, D, D · 1730
disputed
Forces — Various — from strings-and-oboes to full trumpets-and-drums festival band
Occasion — Collegium Musicum (as transmitted)
Dating — Long assumed Cöthen works; surviving sources are Leipzig (Collegium era, c. 1725–39), and modern scholarship dates much of the music there — No. 2 likely latest (c. 1738–39).
Recommended recording — Jordi Savall, Le Concert des Nations, Bach: Ouvertures (Alia Vox)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)
Listen
  • All of Bach — the official Netherlands Bach Society film
  • Search YouTube — Jordi Savall

What it is. Four Ouvertüren — the French genre (Celle's legacy): a grand dotted overture, then a chain of dances — at four different orchestral weights, from the B-minor's chamber intimacy (solo flute and strings) to the D-major pair's trumpets-and-drums civic blaze.

Listen for. Each suite's persona: No. 1 (C major, oboes and bassoon prominent) the courtly conversationalist; No. 2 (B minor) effectively a flute concerto-suite (own card); No. 3 (D major) home of the Air (own card); No. 4 (D major) pure festival — its overture also serving, re-armed with chorus, as the opening of Christmas cantata BWV 110: the parody membrane again.

Craft. The dance essay's orchestral wing: the same allemande-courante-sarabande grammar as the keyboard and cello suites, publicly costumed — compare a gavotte across all three media and the step is identical.

Why it matters. With the Brandenburgs, Bach's complete orchestral testament — Italy there, France here — and coffee-house repertoire that became concert-hall bedrock.
