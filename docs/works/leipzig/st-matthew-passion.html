The St. Matthew Passion is the largest musical structure of Bach's life: roughly three hours of music for Good Friday Vespers at the Thomaskirche, calling for two complete choirs and two orchestras set in spatial dialogue, two organs, a company of soloists, and a ripieno choir of trebles floating above the whole. Scholarly consensus places the premiere on Good Friday of 1727; Bach returned to the score in 1736 for a major revision, writing out a calligraphic fair copy — the surviving autograph — with a care that tells you something about what the work meant to him. The Passion interleaves three kinds of music, which is to say three kinds of time. There is Matthew's narrative itself — the Evangelist reciting, Christ speaking, the crowd erupting — moving at the pace of the Gospel. There are the contemplative arias, on texts by Picander, in which the action halts and a single conscience responds. And there are the chorales, hymns the Leipzig congregation knew by heart, in which the whole community answers back. Everything the work achieves, it achieves by braiding those three strands.

Begin listening with the double-choir architecture, because it is not a sonic luxury: it is theology staged in space. From the first chorus onward the two choirs are cast as Zion and the Faithful, one questioning and one answering, and the exchanges start immediately — Sehet! — Wen? — Look! — At whom? That dialogue, announced in the opening minutes, is the engine of the entire evening; every event in the story becomes a conversation between the one who sees and the many who ask. Against the public drama runs a private radiance. Christ's words alone are haloed by sustained strings, a glow surrounding everything he sings — and the halo vanishes exactly once, at Eli, Eli, lama asabthani. The light goes out at the cry from the cross, and no description quite prepares a listener for how much the strings' silence can mean.

The arias are stations of private conscience along the narrative's road, and three of them mark the way. Buß und Reu — penitence and remorse — establishes the pattern: the story pauses, and one voice takes its weight personally. Erbarme dich, for alto and solo violin, comes directly after Peter's denial, and it is perhaps the most merciful page in music — mercy arriving in the violin's line before the voice can even ask for it. Mache dich, mein Herze, rein is the third of these stations, the same inwardness carried on. And threaded through the whole evening is the chorale O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden, which returns five times, harmonized darker at each return — the congregation's own hymn made to carry the narrative's descending weight. Leipzig listeners had sung that tune all their lives; Bach counted on it, letting them feel the harmony shift beneath a melody they knew like the floor of their own house.

For the craft essays elsewhere on this timeline, the Matthew is the inexhaustible quarry: chorale used as structural rebar; recitative as speech-made-pitch; the da capo aria — an operatic convention — bent to liturgical purpose; a key-architecture held coherent across three full hours. Whatever technique of Bach's vocal writing you want to study at maximum pressure, it is here, working.

Then comes the strangest part of the story. The work was performed a handful of times under Bach and then fell silent for a century — until 1829, when a twenty-year-old Felix Mendelssohn raised it in Berlin and restarted Bach's fame with this very score. That is why the Matthew Passion functions as the spine of this whole timeline: it appears in the first arc as the summit of achievement, in the second as the vehicle of resurrection, and in the third as the repertoire's Everest — the work by which choirs, conductors, and listeners still measure themselves, three hours at a time.

Follow the work into its second life: the revival.
