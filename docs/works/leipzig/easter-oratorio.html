Leipzig I: the cantata machine

Easter Oratorio (Oster-Oratorium)

BWV 249 · D major · 1725
documented
Forces — Choir, orchestra with 3 trumpets & drums, four solo 'characters'
Occasion — Easter Sunday
Dating — First heard Easter 1725, parodying a shepherd-cantata written weeks earlier for the Duke of Weissenfels (BWV 249a); revised into 'Oratorium' form in the 1730s.
Recommended recording — Masaaki Suzuki, Bach Collegium Japan, Easter & Ascension Oratorios (BIS)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The odd one out among the oratorios: no Evangelist, no Gospel narration — instead four named characters (Peter, John, the two Marys) racing to the empty tomb and reacting in aria. It began, weeks earlier, as a pastoral birthday entertainment for the Duke of Weissenfels — shepherds and shepherdesses re-costumed as apostles at speed (Easter 1725 fell during the chorale-cantata cycle's most punishing stretch; parody was survival).

Listen for. The opening pair — a trumpet-blazing sinfonia, then an adagio oboe lament, then the racing duet-turned-chorus Kommt, eilet und laufet ("come, hurry, and run" — the disciples' footrace made music): festival splendor with a smile in it. The tenor's Sanfte soll mein Todeskummer over recorder-cushioned strings — death as slumber — is one of Bach's gentlest arias anywhere.

Craft. Study it beside the Christmas Oratorio as the parody workshop's quick mode versus its planned mode — and note what the operatic character-form (his one quasi-dramatic sacred work) does and doesn't permit him.

Why it matters. Proof the sacred-secular membrane ran both directions on short notice — and a reminder that the Leipzig Easter of 1725, which also produced the second version of the John Passion, packed more first-rate music into one week than most careers.
