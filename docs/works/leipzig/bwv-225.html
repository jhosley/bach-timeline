What it is. The most exhilarating eight minutes in the choral repertoire: Psalm 149's "Sing to the Lord a new song" for two four-part choirs — jubilation engineered as antiphonal machine, and the motet Mozart heard at the Thomaskirche in 1789, demanding the parts on the spot.

Listen for. The opening's layered ignition — one choir chanting "Singet!" as rhythmic scaffolding while the other builds melismatic arcs, the roles swapping mid-flight. The central movement's masterstroke: choir II sings a chorale verse, and between its phrases choir I answers with a free "aria" — hymn and commentary interleaved in real time. The finale collapses eight parts to four for a whipping fugue on "Alles, was Odem hat" — everything that has breath — which, sung well, leaves neither choir nor audience with any.

Why it matters. The work that pierced the eclipse: what Mozart learned from (the Arc II card carries the scene), and the modern chamber choir's Everest.
