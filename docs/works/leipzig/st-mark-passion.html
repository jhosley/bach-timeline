St. Mark Passion (lost)

BWV 247 · · 1731
documented
Forces — Choir, orchestra, soloists (per the surviving libretto)
Occasion — Good Friday Vespers, Thomaskirche
Dating — Premiere Good Friday 1731. The music is lost; Picander's complete libretto survives.
Recommended recording — (reconstructions only — e.g. Jordi Savall's, among several rival versions)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. Bach's third documented Passion — Good Friday 1731 — and the timeline's most instructive absence: the music is lost, one of the major casualties of the estate's dispersal. What survives is Picander's complete printed libretto, and from it scholars deduce the work's design — and its economy: the metrical fingerprints show it was substantially parody, its great frame-choruses and several arias almost certainly drawn from the Trauer-Ode of 1727. Because those source movements survive, the Mark Passion is partially recoverable — hence the cottage industry of scholarly reconstructions (each guessing differently at recitatives and chorales), several recorded.

Listen for (in reconstruction). The Trauer-Ode's lute-and-gamba-shaded mourning re-aimed at Golgotha — and, as an exercise, the seams: knowing it's a reconstruction, ask where the joins show. There is no better training for understanding what, exactly, is Bach in a Bach work.

Why it's on the timeline. The Obituary credits Bach with five Passions; two survive whole, Mark survives as a shadow, and the others are titles at best. The catalog carries this card as the standing reminder that the corpus we study is the surviving corpus — perhaps two-fifths of the cantatas and most of the Passions are simply gone, and every generalization about "Bach's music" stands on that incomplete floor.
