Twenty years after Book I, Bach walked the twenty-four keys again. The second book of the Well-Tempered Clavier was assembled around 1739–42 from new work and revised older pieces — no single autograph title page survives, and the "Book II" framing is partly posthumous — but the result reads as a unified second traversal of the same terrain: a prelude and fugue in every major and minor key, the complete circuit walked end to end, made once by a man in his thirties and now remade, from a different vantage entirely, by a man near sixty. And the differences between the two journeys are a portrait of late style, which is what makes this book essential rather than redundant.

The preludes have grown. Many are now full two-part, sonata-like movements — rounded designs with the modern world's sense of balanced form. This is the galant style of the younger generation acknowledged, absorbed, and out-built: Bach takes the fashionable manner his sons' contemporaries were making their careers on and shows what it can carry when someone with his resources writes it. The fugues, meanwhile, are fewer-voiced on average but denser in thought — more chromatic subjects, more learned devices, and the learning worn more lightly. Where Book I demonstrates, Book II converses; the difficulty has moved inward, from the surface of the counterpoint into the substance of the ideas it carries.

A first shelf of listening, for orientation. The F-sharp minor fugue is a triple fugue — three subjects, the last a chain of sighs — and its ending combines all three with a valedictory calm that stands among the book's most affecting pages. The E-major fugue is stile antico: Renaissance polyphony visiting the modern book, the idiom of the Mass's Credo at keyboard size, the old church style folded into a collection that otherwise looks forward. The B-flat minor pair is Book II's answer to Book I's E-flat minor grief — the later book measuring itself against the earlier one's darkest station. And the D-major prelude puts a trumpet-and-drum orchestra inside two hands, festival brilliance conjured from a keyboard alone. Four pieces, four different answers to what a prelude and fugue can be — which is the book in miniature: the twenty-four keys are not a constraint Bach satisfies but a set of rooms he furnishes, each differently.

The deepest use of Book II, though, is comparative. Take a same-key pair — the C minor, say, or the B minor — and set the Book I and Book II versions side by side: the same mind, on the same terrain, twenty years apart. Everything is controlled in this experiment except time — same composer, same key, same two-movement form, same expressive neighborhood — so every difference you hear is development, not circumstance. The catalog can offer no cleaner window into how Bach changed, and the fugue essay uses exactly this before-and-after spine. A listener who works through even two or three such pairs will come away with a concrete, earned sense of what "late Bach" means — not a slogan but an audible set of choices.

Book II also takes its place in a larger constellation. With the Goldberg Variations and The Art of Fugue, it forms the late keyboard triptych — three summations, each taking one principle (the key-cycle, the variation, the fugue) to its limit. And its influence ran forward with peculiar force. Chopin practiced from it before every concert; Shostakovich answered it directly with his own twenty-four preludes and fugues, op. 87. This is the book that taught the future what preludes and fugues were for — not archaic exercises but a form in which a composer takes inventory of everything they know. Each of the twenty-four pairs has its own card, where the individual preludes and fugues get their due.
