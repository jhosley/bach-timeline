The Christmas Oratorio is six cantatas designed as one work — the Nativity narrative from the manger to the Magi, told by an Evangelist and performed across the six feast days from Christmas Day 1734 to Epiphany 1735. No audience heard it whole in Bach's Leipzig; it unfolded across nearly two weeks of services, an installment at a time. And yet Bach built it as a single span: the six parts are bound into one tonal arch, with D-major trumpets and drums blazing at both ends, so that a congregation hearing Part VI on Epiphany would close the circle opened twelve days before. It is a shape no concert work of its time attempts — architecture stretched across a calendar rather than an evening — and holding that span together is the work's quietest achievement. Each part is a complete cantata sized to its feast, with the Evangelist's narration carrying the story across the gaps between services; the modern listener who hears all six in one sitting experiences a compression the original congregation never did, and it is worth remembering, as the trumpets return in Part VI, that Bach designed that return to land across an interval of twelve days.

The scene-setting can be astonishingly economical. Part II opens not with voices but with the Sinfonia: the pastoral orchestra of the shepherds answered by an angel-choir of oboes, earth and heaven trading phrases. The entire tableau — fields, night, annunciation — is established before a single word is sung.

The famous fact about the work is that much of it is second-hand, and the famous surprise is how little that matters — or rather, how much it adds. The core choruses and arias were parodied from birthday odes of 1733–34 (the event card tells that story): the opening chorus Jauchzet, frohlocket began life as a drum-fanfare birthday ode, and knowing its origin makes the transformation more impressive, not less. Same notes, higher object. The festive machinery built to flatter a court proved, with new words, to be exactly the machinery of "rejoice, exult" — and part of the pleasure of repeated listening is hearing how completely the music commits to its second life. Whatever suspicion the word "parody" raises, the practice here is the opposite of laziness: it is a composer judging his own recent music worth preserving, and finding the texts that let it say more.

Against all that brilliance, the intimacies. The alto's lullaby Schlafe, mein Liebster rocks the cradle at the heart of the celebration. And the chorale Wie soll ich dich empfangen is sung to the same melody as the Matthew Passion's O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden — the Passion chorale planted in the cradle scenes. For every Leipzig ear that knew the tune — and they all did — Good Friday was audible at Christmas: the manger already shadowed by the cross, an association Bach could count on his congregation supplying the moment the melody began. It is the boldest instance of a technique that runs through the whole work, the chorale as connective tissue — familiar hymn-tunes threaded through the six evenings, binding the installments to each other and to everything the congregation already knew and believed. Between that and the parody workshop running at full sophistication, this is the catalog's master-class in both techniques at once.

The reception history runs the timeline's signature arc, but on its own clock. The Christmas Oratorio was forgotten with the rest of the vocal music after Bach's death, and its revival came late — first performed again in Berlin in 1857, a full generation after the Matthew Passion's famous return. From that slow start it has run further than anything: in German-speaking lands today it is the single most performed Bach work of all, an annual fixture woven into the culture's Christmas itself. Eclipse and return, the pattern this timeline traces everywhere — here run in slow motion, and then overshooting everything.

How the parody workshop assembled it, feast by feast: Christmas 1734. The tune the cradle borrows: the St. Matthew Passion.
