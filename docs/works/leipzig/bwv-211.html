Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Schweigt stille, plaudert nicht (the Coffee Cantata)

BWV 211 · G major · 1734
documented
Forces — Flute, strings, three singers (narrator, father, daughter), continuo
Occasion — Collegium Musicum entertainment, Zimmermann's
Dating — c. 1734, Picander text; performed by the Collegium Musicum — in the coffee house it gently satirizes.
Recommended recording — Masaaki Suzuki, Bach Collegium Japan, Secular Cantatas (BIS)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. A ten-movement comic miniature-opera for Zimmermann's coffee house: grumpy father Schlendrian ("Slowcoach") tries to break daughter Lieschen's coffee habit by escalating threats — no walks, no dresses, finally no husband — and she capitulates... while secretly resolving to marry only a man who'll write coffee into the wedding contract. Leipzig laughing at its own caffeine craze (then a genuine moral controversy), in the den itself.

Listen for. Lieschen's aria Ei! wie schmeckt der Coffee süße — "ah, how sweet the coffee tastes, lovelier than a thousand kisses" — a flute-wreathed love song addressed to a beverage, and genuinely beautiful: Bach refuses to write down to comedy. Schlendrian's growling basso frustration (the continuo stomping); the recitatives' sitcom timing; the closing trio shrugging that coffee-drinking is hereditary anyway — "die Katze läßt das Mausen nicht" (cats will chase mice).

Craft. The same aria-and-recitative machinery as the church works, retuned for wit — the era's proof (with the Peasant Cantata, its rustic sibling of 1742) that the "Fifth Evangelist" image is a nineteenth-century cropping of a fuller man.

Why it matters. Documented Bach humor — rare, precious, and performed today more than almost any secular cantata. Play it for anyone who thinks they don't like Bach; play it for anyone who thinks Bach didn't like fun.
