Leipzig I: the cantata machine

Magnificat

BWV 243 / 243a · D major (1733 revision); E-flat (1723 original) · 1723
documented
Forces — Five-part choir, festival orchestra, five soloists
Occasion — Vespers on high feasts
Dating — E-flat version with Christmas interpolations, Christmas 1723; revised in D major c. 1733.
Recommended recording — John Eliot Gardiner, Monteverdi Choir & English Baroque Soloists, Magnificat (Philips)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. Mary's canticle in twelve movements averaging under three minutes each — Bach's large-choral style at its most concentrated: no recitatives, no chorales, no da capo longueurs; every verse a distinct, complete design. Two versions survive: the 1723 E-flat original with its four Christmas insertions, and the familiar D-major revision (trumpets happier, details polished) of about a decade later.

Listen for. Text-painting as structure, not decoration: omnes generationes piling fugal entries into an overlapping crowd — "all generations," audibly arriving at once; deposuit potentes hurling the mighty down the scale while exaltavit lifts the humble; Suscepit Israel's trebles floating over the ancient Magnificat chant intoned by oboes — the old church singing inside the new. Finally Sicut erat in principio — "as it was in the beginning" — literally restating the opening music: the pun every composer since has envied.

Craft. The study-sized door to the big choral works (everything in the Mass at one-quarter length), and the catalog's cleanest two-version revision case: E-flat vs. D, side by side.

Why it matters. If you take one large vocal work into close analysis first, take this one — thirty minutes, every technique, no filler.
