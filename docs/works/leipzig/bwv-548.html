Prelude and Fugue in E minor ('the Wedge')

BWV 548 · E minor · 1732
documented
Forces — Organ
Dating — Leipzig, late 1720s–1730s; autograph partially in Bach's hand.
Recommended recording — Simon Preston, Bach: The Organ Works (DG)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The summit of the free organ works — Leipzig-Bach's answer to his own Weimar decade: bigger, stricter, more symphonic. The nickname is the fugue subject's shape: from a repeated central note it opens outward in widening leaps — a wedge drawn in sound, austere as a diagram and unforgettable as a face.

Listen for. The prelude as proto-symphony: a brooding ritornello structure (the Italian engine, fully Germanized) whose material develops with an intensity pointing straight at later centuries — Bach's grandest single organ movement. The fugue's shock is formal: its middle turns into a toccata — episodes of free, whirling figuration invading the strict form — before the fugue's opening section returns note for note: a da capo fugue, opera's ABA imposed on counterpoint's cathedral. Law, fantasy, law.

Craft. The R4 fugue essay's late-style exhibit: fugue no longer as texture but as dramatic architecture — form itself the expressive event. Compare the Wedge's return-as-triumph with the WTC's miniature closures.

Why it matters. Where the organ thread culminates before the late chorale summas: the instrument of Bach's youth, mastered past anyone's imagining, one last time at full civic scale.
