What it is. The dramatic Passion: John's swift, confrontational Gospel set with corresponding urgency — half the Matthew's length, twice its velocity. The opening chorus Herr, unser Herrscher is unlike anything else in Bach: swirling strings, grinding dissonant winds, the choir hammering "Herr! Herr! Herr!" — majesty invoked over chaos.

Listen for. The turba choruses — the crowd's interjections in the trial scene — set with documentary ferocity and structural cunning: Bach arranges them symmetrically around the scene's center (the same music returning with opposite texts), building a palindromic arch at the drama's heart, with the chorale Durch dein Gefängnis — freedom through captivity — placed at the exact keystone. Also the aria Es ist vollbracht: viola da gamba, the old instrument of mourning, breaking into a blaze of trumpet-figures at "Der Held aus Juda siegt" — death and victory in one page.

Craft. The comparison work: set the two Passions side by side and every parameter differs — narrative pacing, aria placement, crowd treatment. The R4 essays use exactly that comparison.

A necessary honesty. John's text has the crowd as "die Jüden," and the work's performance history intersects with the history of Christian antisemitism; modern performances and scholarship address this directly rather than around it. The card keeps that reckoning visible — the documented/tradition discipline applied to reception, not just anecdote.
