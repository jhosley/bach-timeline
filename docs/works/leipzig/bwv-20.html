Leipzig I: the cantata machine

What it is. The gateway to the summit: on the first Sunday after Trinity 1724 — one year to the day after his debut — Bach launched the chorale cantata cycle with this setting of the terrifying eternity-hymn ("O eternity, thou word of thunder"). The cycle's format, established here: the hymn's first verse as a vast chorale fantasia chorus, inner verses paraphrased into recitatives and arias, the last verse plain — the congregation's hymn swallowed whole and rebuilt as architecture.

Listen for. The opening: a French overture — the royal-entrance genre (compare BWV 61) — with the chorale blazing in long notes above dotted dread: eternity arriving in state. Bach marking the cycle's beginning with the ceremony-of-beginnings form: he knew what he was starting. Then the bass aria with trumpet, Wacht auf — "wake up!" — the alarm sounded at the sinners in the back pews.

Craft. The chorale-fantasia format this work inaugurates is, for the R4 essays, Bach's single greatest structural invention in vocal music — some forty realizations follow, none alike.

Why it matters. Stand here and look down the year ahead: BWV 20 is the trailhead of the most sustained feat in Western composition. Gardiner's Pilgrimage journals call this cycle the discovery of his conducting life.
