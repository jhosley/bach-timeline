Leipzig III: the summing-up

Mer hahn en neue Oberkeet (the Peasant Cantata)

BWV 212 · A major · 1742
documented
Forces — Horn, flute, strings (folk-band textures), soprano & bass
Occasion — Village homage festivity
Dating — 30 August 1742, homage for the new lord of the manor at Klein-Zschocher — Bach's last dated secular cantata.
Recommended recording — Masaaki Suzuki, Bach Collegium Japan (BIS), Secular Cantatas  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)
Listen
  • Search YouTube — Masaaki Suzuki

What it is. "We've got a new squire!" — in Saxon dialect: a village couple celebrates (and needles) the new lord of a manor near Leipzig, in Bach's most down-to-earth score: an overture that's a medley of rustic dance tunes, arias built on folk melodies and popular songs, drone basses, a tavern-band flute-and-fiddle world — the learned cantor, at fifty-seven, writing barn music well, with evident delight.

Listen for. The overture's deliberate patchwork (dances butting into each other mid-phrase — a village band's set list); the couple's flirt-and-grumble duets; the wink where the music briefly goes "high style" for the flattery aria — and drops it again.

Why it matters. Written the year after the Goldbergs: hold the two side by side — strict canon cosmology and dialect comedy, twelve months apart — and every remaining stereotype of Bach dissolves. The completest composer who ever lived, at both ends of the register, on his way into the late summa years.
