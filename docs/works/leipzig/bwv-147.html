What it is. A Weimar Advent cantata rebuilt, weeks into the Leipzig job, for the feast of Mary's visit to Elizabeth — two parts, ten movements, trumpet-led C-major festivity — and the accidental home of the most famous ninety seconds Bach ever wrote.

Listen for. Each part closes with the same chorale setting: the hymn Wohl mir, daß ich Jesum habe, floated in even quarter-notes inside a lilting triplet orchestral halo — the movement the world knows as "Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring" (the English title comes from the Victorian-era choral adaptation; Dame Myra Hess's piano transcription made it universal). Notice what the fame obscures: it is a congregational hymn with orchestral gilding, the plainest device in the Leipzig toolkit — and the whole planet responded to exactly that plainness.

Craft. A textbook Weimar-to-Leipzig expansion: the R3 comparison shows what adapting an old work for the new machine actually involved (new recitatives to fit the feast's Gospel, re-ordering, the chorale frames added).

Why it matters. The catalog's best case study in selective fame: one interior movement escaped into world culture while its nine siblings — including a superb trumpet aria — wait inside, unheard by the millions who can hum their neighbor. The timeline's job in miniature: reattach the famous fragment to its whole.
