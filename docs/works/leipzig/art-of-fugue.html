Leipzig III: the summing-up

The Art of Fugue (Die Kunst der Fuge)

BWV 1080 · D minor · 1745
documented
Forces — Open score, four voices — playable (and surely meant) at keyboard
Occasion — None — the discipline itself
Dating — Autograph c. 1742; expanded for engraving in the last years; published posthumously 1751; the final fugue unfinished in the sources.
Recommended recording — Grigory Sokolov (piano); alternatively Fretwork (viols), The Art of Fugue  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The event card tells the biography; here is the music: fourteen fugues and four canons, every one grown from a single plain D-minor subject — simple fugues, counter-fugues on its inversion, double and triple fugues, mirror fugues (two complete fugues, one the other's exact reflection), the strictness ratcheting movement by movement until the unfinished quadruple summit where B–A–C–H enters as the third subject and the music stops mid-air.

Listen for, as a journey. Contrapunctus 1: the subject bare, the rules stated. 4: the inversion singing on its own. 9: the racing double fugue everyone loves first. 11: the triple fugue's chromatic storm — the cycle's expressive extreme. The mirror pair (12–13): listen to both halves back to back; your ear learns what "inverted world" means. Then 14, and the silence.

Listen for, as sound. Choose your instrument-question: keyboard (the scholarly consensus — one mind, one sitting), string quartet or viols (the voices embodied), even orchestra. The open score invites the question; three centuries of answers are part of the work now.

Craft. The R4 fugue essay's final chapter — and the Craft section's whole thesis (one idea, total development) at terminal scale.

Reception thread. Sold ~30 copies; plates nearly melted for copper — then the twentieth century made it a monument of pure mind: the emblem of Bach-as-structure that Arc III's modernists claimed for their own.
