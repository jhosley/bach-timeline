Leipzig I: the cantata machine

What it is. State mourning at the highest craft: the Electress of Saxony — beloved precisely because she had refused to follow her husband's politically-motivated conversion to Catholicism, Lutheran Saxony's quiet heroine — died in 1727, and Leipzig's university commissioned Bach (over its own music director's furious protest; the paperwork survives) to set an ode by Gottsched, Germany's reigning literary arbiter, for the grand memorial in the Paulinerkirche.

Listen for. The sound-world first — Bach's most luxurious mourning orchestra: paired flutes, oboes d'amore, viole da gamba and lutes together, antique shadows inside modern strings. The opening chorus's ceremonial grief; the alto recitative where tolling bells are composed into the texture (lutes, gambas, and pizzicato tolling at different speeds — Leipzig's actual bells, notated); the closing chorus's consoling sarabande lilt.

Craft. Its afterlife makes it doubly essential: this music almost certainly became the backbone of the lost St. Mark Passion — so the Trauer-Ode is our best audible window into a vanished major work. The R4 parody essay treats the double identity in full.

Why it matters. Written months after the Matthew Passion and sharing its B-minor gravity: 1727 caught Bach at the summit of his mourning art, twice — once for scripture, once for a queen who chose her faith over a crown.
