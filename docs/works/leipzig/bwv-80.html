Leipzig II: coffee-house concertos and paper wars

Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott

BWV 80 · D major · 1730
documented
Forces — Oboes, strings, choir, soloists (trumpets & drums added posthumously by W. F. Bach)
Occasion — Reformation Day (31 October)
Dating — Evolved from a Weimar Lenten cantata (BWV 80a, 1715/16) into the Reformation festival work, final form c. 1728–31; the familiar trumpet parts are Friedemann's later addition.
Recommended recording — Masaaki Suzuki, Bach Collegium Japan, Cantatas (BIS)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)
Listen

What it is. Luther's battle-hymn — A mighty fortress is our God, the anthem of Bach's whole confessional world — built into the definitive Reformation Day cantata, assembled and perfected across fifteen years from a Weimar Lenten original.

Listen for. The opening chorus: no orchestral fantasia this time — instead a colossal chorale motet, every phrase of Luther's tune developed fugally by the voices while, at top and bottom of the texture, oboes and violone hammer the same tune in strict canon against them: the fortress built in counterpoint, walls above and below. It is the most learned chorus in the cantatas — and it sounds like granite, not learning. The second movement mounts the hymn's verse 2 (soprano) simultaneously over an aria-duet — two texts at once, the era's multitasking theology.

Craft. The R4 canon essay's sacred centerpiece; also a fine authenticity case: the blazing trumpets most recordings add are Friedemann's, for a later Halle performance — Bach's own festival scoring is leaner, and period performances now let you hear both.

Why it matters. Bach and Luther, maximally fused — the timeline's Eisenach beginnings (the school choir singing this very hymn) arriving at their monument.
