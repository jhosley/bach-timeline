The motets are six choral works, by the traditional count, written in the old Lutheran motet genre: scripture and chorale woven together for voices alone, in four to eight parts, with continuo and doubling instruments assumed in practice but nothing in the texture depending on them. The genre was already old when Bach took it up — this is the genre of the old masters whose works filled the Michaelis library at Lüneburg — and he wrote his own motets as that tradition's conscious heir, and its terminus. Mostly they were written for funerals and memorial services, which in Leipzig were the occasions that ordered the city's grandest choral craftsmanship — and Bach answered them with a corpus that closed the genre's books. The six were not conceived as a set: they accumulated across the Leipzig years, one perhaps earlier still, their datings varying work by work, and the traditional count of six — BWV 225 through 230 — is the shape posterity gave the corpus rather than a plan Bach announced.

The one to hear first is Singet dem Herrn ein neues Lied, BWV 225: two four-part choirs hurling jubilation across the aisle at each other, antiphonal writing of a physical, spatial excitement that no recording quite captures and no listener forgets. This is the work Mozart heard in 1789 when he visited Leipzig — heard, sat up, and demanded the parts of, exclaiming "now, there is something one can learn from!" One genius auditing another across four decades of supposed oblivion: the scene is one of the timeline's hinges, and this motet is what caused it.

Then Jesu, meine Freude, BWV 227 — eleven movements arranged in a perfect symmetrical arch, verses of the chorale alternating with passages of scripture, each scriptural movement answering its mirror-partner across the center. It is the funeral sermon as architecture: text and hymn interleaved the way a preacher interleaves doctrine and consolation, but with the proportions worked out to the movement. And Komm, Jesu, komm, which sets death as homecoming in the tenderest double-choir writing of the century — the eight voices not hurling jubilation now but passing weariness and welcome back and forth until they merge.

For the study of Bach's craft, the motets are the counterpoint essays' choral anchor, because everything here happens with no orchestra to hide behind. Fugue, chorale-fantasia, antiphony — every technique of the big concerted works appears, but the choir is the whole machine: no instrumental ritornello to frame a chorus, no continuo band to thicken a texture, no trumpet to crown a cadence. Voices carry the counterpoint, the color, and the architecture at once. Singers have always known these works as the ultimate test for exactly this reason; there is nowhere to stand except on the writing itself, and the writing, exposed completely, turns out to need no help.

And the motets hold a unique place in the reception story this timeline tells. Bach's vocal music went dark after his death — the cantatas scattered, the Passions shelved — but the motets are the one vocal corpus that never did. The Thomanerchor, Bach's own choir, sang them continuously from his death to this day: an unbroken performing tradition running straight through the decades when the rest of the vocal music slept in libraries and attics. That thread is what the 1789 Mozart scene hangs on — Mozart could only be ambushed by Singet dem Herrn because the choir in Leipzig had never stopped singing it — and it is the proof that the eclipse was never quite total where the manuscripts stayed home. Where custody and practice held together, Bach's music simply continued, waiting for the world to circle back. Each of the six motets has its own card, BWV 225 through 230, where the individual works get their due.

Where the genre's old masters lived: the Michaelis library. What the unbroken thread made possible: the eclipse — and its exceptions.
