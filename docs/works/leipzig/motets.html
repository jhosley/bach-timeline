Leipzig I: the cantata machine

The Motets

BWV 225–230 · · 1727
documented
Forces — Four- to eight-part choir (double choir), continuo (instruments doubling ad lib)
Occasion — Funerals, memorial services, special occasions
Dating — Composed across the Leipzig years (one perhaps earlier), mostly for funerals and memorials; datings vary by work.
Recommended recording — John Eliot Gardiner, Monteverdi Choir, Bach Motets (SDG)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. Six (by traditional count) unaccompanied-style choral works — the old Lutheran motet genre, scripture and chorale woven for voices alone (continuo and doubling instruments assumed in practice) — written mostly for funerals: the occasions where Leipzig ordered its grandest choral craftsmanship. This is the genre of the Lüneburg library's old masters, and Bach wrote them as the tradition's conscious heir — and terminus.

Listen for. Singet dem Herrn ein neues Lied (BWV 225): two four-part choirs hurling jubilation across the aisle — the work Mozart heard in 1789 and demanded the parts of, exclaiming "now, there is something one can learn from!" Jesu, meine Freude (BWV 227): eleven movements in a perfect symmetrical arch, chorale verses alternating with scripture — the funeral sermon as architecture. Komm, Jesu, komm: death as homecoming, in the tenderest double-choir writing of the century.

Craft. The counterpoint essays' choral anchor: fugue, chorale-fantasia, and antiphony with no orchestra to hide behind — the choir is the whole machine.

Reception thread. The one vocal corpus that never went dark: the Thomanerchor sang the motets continuously from Bach's death to this day — the thread on which the 1789 Mozart scene hangs, and the proof that the eclipse was never quite total where the manuscripts stayed home. Individual cards for all six arrive with the R3 vocal completion batch.
