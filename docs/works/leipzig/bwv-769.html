What it is. The membership exercise for Mizler's learned society: Luther's Christmas hymn Vom Himmel hoch run through five variations of escalating canonic strictness — canon at the octave, at the fifth, at the seventh, in augmentation, and finally a movement combining the chorale's own phrases against themselves in stretto, augmentation, and inversion at once. Counterpoint's doctoral defense, on a children's carol.

Listen for. The trick that makes it art rather than proof: the carol stays audible — pedal or soprano, plain and singable — while the canons weave around it; Christmas never stops being Christmas. The last movement's pile-up (every phrase of the tune combined) ends with Bach's name stitched in: a B–A–C–H figure in the final bars — the signature, as on the Art of Fugue, entering the finished cathedral.

Craft. The R4 canon essay's summit exhibit with the Goldberg canons: the same lesson — strictness as invisible discipline, felt as inevitability, not effort. Stravinsky, two centuries on, orchestrated it in homage (Arc III).

Why it matters. What Bach chose to prove himself with, at sixty-two, to a society of scientists: not brilliance — law, singing. The late style's credo in eleven minutes.
