Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Prelude and Fugue in A minor ('the Great')

What it is. The Weimar prelude-and-fugue ideal at full maturity: a prelude of serpentine chromatic figuration that keeps tightening its own spiral, and a long gigue-rhythm fugue whose subject dances for pages before the work detonates into a stylus phantasticus cadenza-coda — the north-German fire (Buxtehude) now running through Italian structural discipline (the Vivaldi conversion, audible as form).

Listen for. The pedal solo moments — Weimar-Bach's signature flex (contemporaries marveled most at his feet) — and the fugue's long paragraph-building: episodes that sequence outward, subject entries that land like pillars, the ending that abandons counterpoint entirely for sheer improvisatory weather. The two-stage form (free prelude, strict fugue, free coda) is the mature print of what BWV 565 sketches in crayon.

Craft. The pairing-of-opposites principle — fantasy then law — that governs the WTC's whole design, here at organ scale.

Why it matters. Liszt transcribed it for piano and made it a Romantic warhorse — one of the main channels by which the organ works reached the nineteenth-century concert public (Arc II's keyboard-first Bach again). For students: the single best Weimar prelude-and-fugue to know whole.
