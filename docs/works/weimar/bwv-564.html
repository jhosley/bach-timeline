What it is. The organ pretending to be an orchestra: uniquely among Bach's organ works, a three-movement concerto plan — fast, slow, fast — the Italian import (fresh from the transcription desk) rebuilt as native organ music.

Listen for. The opening's twin manifestos: first a manuals-only fanfare of scales, then the most famous pedal solo in the literature — a full paragraph for feet alone, the young virtuoso simply showing off the machine's (and his own) most spectacular capability. The Adagio is Italy distilled: a singing solo line over a walking pizzicato-style bass — a violin concerto slow movement scored for pipes — ending in strange, dark Grave chords that door into the fugue. The fugue itself is pure play: a subject full of rests and skips, counterpoint with a grin.

Craft. For the R4 essays, the cleanest specimen of style transfer — the concerto's dramaturgy (tutti/solo, cantilena, dance-finale) executed by one player. Compare the true transcriptions (BWV 592–596) to hear the difference between arranging Italy and speaking it.

Why it matters. Busoni's piano transcription made the C-major Toccata a Romantic recital monument — and the Adagio, in a hundred arrangements, one of Bach's most-loved slow movements. Weimar's sunniest big organ work.
