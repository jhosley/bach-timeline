What it is. Advent I, 1714: the church year opens, the King arrives — so Bach opens with a French overture, the musical genre invented to announce Louis XIV's entrance (absorbed at Celle), with Luther's ancient Advent chorale Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland threaded through its dotted pomp voice by voice. The oldest German hymn dressed as Versailles ceremony: the whole Weimar synthesis in one movement's concept.

Listen for. That opening — chorale phrases arriving inside the overture's slow-fast-slow frame — and then the bass recitative on Revelation's "Behold, I stand at the door and knock": Bach sets the knocking as pizzicato strings, Christ's knuckle on the door, one of his most famous literalisms and among his best. The soprano's answer (Öffne dich, mein ganzes Herze — open, my whole heart) scales the cosmic Advent down to one person's chest.

Craft. Exhibit A for the R4 essay on national styles as meaning: French = royalty; the choice is the exegesis. Compare BWV 62 (same chorale, Leipzig 1724, entirely different solution) when the chorale-cycle batch arrives.

Why it matters. Desert-island Weimar: fifteen minutes, and the door-knocking recitative alone teaches how Bach reads scripture — concretely, theatrically, and with total confidence that wit and reverence are compatible.
