What it is. The second Konzertmeister cantata and the most consequential twelve bars of ground bass Bach ever laid down: the opening chorus — "Weeping, wailing, worrying, quailing" — is a lament chaconne, the choir sighing its four words over a chromatically descending bass repeated twelve times (the lamento bass, the Baroque's universal figure of grief).

Listen for. How the words are dismembered: not sentences but four gasped nouns, each voice entering, breaking off, overlapping — grief as texture. Then the pivot the whole cantata argues: "Christians' tears are the vineyard's rain" — sorrow reframed as cultivation, and the music warming genre by genre until a trumpet threads the chorale Was Gott tut, das ist wohlgetan wordlessly above the final aria: consolation arriving as a tune everyone knows, before anyone sings it.

Craft & the long arc. Thirty-five years later Bach took this chorus, re-carved it as the Crucifixus of the B minor Mass — tightening, deepening, adding the sinking burial ending. The two versions side by side are the timeline's supreme revision lesson: what a 29-year-old wrote, a 64-year-old understood.

Why it matters. Liszt built a piano variation-monument on its bass; the lament-chaconne thread (Chaconne, Goldberg ground, Crucifixus) runs through Bach's whole life — and it starts, datably, here.
