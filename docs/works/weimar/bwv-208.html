Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Was mir behagt, ist nur die muntre Jagd! (the Hunt Cantata)

BWV 208 · F major · 1713
documented
Forces — Horns, recorders, oboes, strings, four mythological soloists, choir
Occasion — Ducal birthday, after the hunt
Dating — February 1713, for the birthday of Duke Christian of Saxe-Weissenfels; Bach's earliest surviving secular cantata.
Recommended recording — Masaaki Suzuki, Bach Collegium Japan, Secular Cantatas (BIS)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. Bach's earliest surviving secular cantata: mythological flattery (Diana, Endymion, Pan, Pales — goddess of the hunt and friends, singing a duke's praises) for a Weissenfels birthday banquet after the hunt, 1713 — the 27-year-old organist moonlighting in courtly entertainment, hunting horns and all.

Listen for. One aria has outlived the occasion by three centuries: Pales' Schafe können sicher weiden — "Sheep may safely graze" — two recorders rocking over a walking bass, the safest music ever written: flocks secure where a good shepherd (read: duke) rules. Its afterlife is its own reception story — piano transcriptions, weddings, film scores; most who love it have no idea it's political pastoral for a hunting party.

Craft. Early evidence of a career-long truth: Bach's secular and sacred manners are one language — Pales' aria would sit comfortably in a church cantata, and material from this work did later serve sacred parody (movements resurface in cantatas; the First Brandenburg's early version is a suspected sibling of this festivity).

Why it matters. The secular-cantata shelf opens here: occasional music, written to be forgotten, refusing to be. Study it as the missing link between the Weimar church style and the Cöthen serenatas.
