Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Seven Keyboard Toccatas

BWV 910–916 · f#, c, D, d, e, g, G · 1710

What it is. Young Bach unchained: seven multi-section toccatas in the north-German stylus phantasticus (Buxtehude's manner, harpsichord-sized) — free flourishes, brooding slow sections, and fugues (often two per toccata) strung into improvisatory sequences: the closest the keyboard works come to a transcript of the young virtuoso playing at you.

Listen for. The D-major (BWV 912) — the extrovert: swagger, a hymn-like adagio, a gigue-fugue ending in mock-tragic theatrics; the F-sharp minor (910) and C minor (911) — the depth pair, their fugues already unmistakably him; the E-minor (914), compact enough to be every student's first.

Craft. The before-photograph: sectional fantasy preceding the Italian formal discipline (Weimar's conversion) — hear these, then the English Suite preludes, and the arrival of architecture in Bach's keyboard music is audible as a life event. Several survive via the Ohrdruf brother's manuscript collections — the guardian archiving the ward.

Why it matters. The wild youth the mature monuments grew from — kept in the catalog to make the growth visible.
