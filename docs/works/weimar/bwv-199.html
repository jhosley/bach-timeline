Weimar: the organ decade and the Italian conversion

Mein Herze schwimmt im Blut

BWV 199 · C minor (Weimar version) · 1714
documented
Forces — Solo soprano, oboe, strings, continuo
Occasion — 11th Sunday after Trinity
Dating — Weimar, 1714 (12 August); revived and re-scored by Bach at Cöthen and Leipzig — a portable favorite.
Recommended recording — Lorraine Hunt Lieberson, Craig Smith (Nonesuch), Bach: Cantatas BWV 82 & 199  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The great solo-soprano cantata: one voice's journey from self-loathing ("my heart swims in blood") to restored joy, in the most operatic sacred music of the Weimar years — a psychological monodrama in eight movements that Bach carried with him and revived at each subsequent post.

Listen for. The opening recitative's raw immediacy — confession without formality; the central aria Tief gebückt ("deeply bowed"), long-arched contrition over full strings; and the turn: a chorale verse sung over a busy viola obbligato (the ensemble's humble middle voice carrying the consolation — a deliberate, touching casting choice), before the final gigue-aria dances the forgiven soul out.

Why it matters. The cantata-as-inner-drama at its purest — and, via Lorraine Hunt Lieberson's celebrated performances (paired with BWV 82), one of the works through which modern audiences rediscovered what these pieces do to a room. Arc III will return to that recording.
