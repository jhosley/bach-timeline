What it is. The young Bach's first architectural colossus: an eight-bar ground bass announced alone in the pedals, then twenty variations over it, then — where Buxtehude's ostinato works simply end — a full double fugue on the theme itself: variation and fugue, the two great cumulative forms bolted into one arch.

Listen for. The build as terrain: variations clustering in waves (figuration migrating from hands to feet, the theme rising out of the bass into the treble mid-cycle — a coup: the ground leaves the ground), the texture thinning to near-silence around variation 12 before the long climb. Then the fugue's arrival — the theme suddenly a subject, sprouting countersubjects — and the famous ending: a drive to a shattering deceptive cadence, one breath of silence, resolution.

Craft. The keystone of the ostinato thread: Buxtehude's passacaglias behind it, the Chaconne and Crucifixus ahead — the R4 ground-bass essay's organ anchor.

Why it matters. Brahms studied it; the Romantics orchestrated it; organists structure recitals around it. The first work where Bach's ambition of scale — the cathedral-sized single span — is fully achieved on paper.
