What it is. A funeral cantata by a composer of twenty-two that many musicians privately rank with anything he ever wrote. The scoring alone sets the room: two recorders and two viols — no violins, no brightness — the old instruments of mourning, in an E-flat glow. Its text is a compiled sermon: Old Testament verses on death as law, answered by New Testament verses on death as grace, arranged so that the theology happens in the music's form.

Listen for. The center: the choir winds through the old covenant's fugue ("it is the ancient law: man, thou must die") while the soprano floats the new covenant's plea above it ("yes, come, Lord Jesus") — and then the instruments fall away one by one until her voice is left alone, unaccompanied, mid-phrase: the single most breathtaking silence in Bach. Law and grace, argued contrapuntally, resolved by a child's voice hanging in air.

Craft. The R4 essays' first full case of architecture as theology — the symmetrical text-plan, the chorale quotations threaded under everything (an unsung hymn in the gamba lines that the first audience would have recognized without hearing).

Why it matters. Written within months of the marriage, possibly for the family dead — by the man who buried both parents at nine. The timeline's family and craft tracks meet here more nakedly than anywhere else in the early years.
