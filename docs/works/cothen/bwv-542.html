What it is. The most harmonically ferocious organ work Bach wrote: a Fantasia that lurches between massive rhetorical chords and recitative-like solos, modulating with a violence that startles even now — some progressions barely explicable before the nineteenth century — paired with a big, buoyant fugue on one of his most tuneful subjects.

Listen for. The Fantasia's argument-by-extremes: proclamation, collapse, chromatic descent, again — the Chromatic Fantasia's sibling, transferred to the cathedral. Then the fugue's subject: a rolling, almost folk-like melody — by tradition modeled on a Dutch popular song as a courtesy to the Amsterdam-trained Reincken, which is why the pair is linked (unprovenly, the flag says) to the 1720 Hamburg audition. Fantasy at the edge of order; fugue as pure geniality — the pairing principle at its widest stretch.

Craft. The R4 chromaticism essay's organ centerpiece: how far dissonance and remote modulation could go within functional harmony — the vocabulary of the Crucifixus and Komm, süßes Kreuz, unleashed instrumentally.

Why it matters. If the Hamburg association holds even loosely, this is the sound of Bach playing for his lineage's last patriarch, months after burying Maria Barbara — biography and harmony darkening together. Even without it: the great G minor stands with the Chaconne among his supreme minor-key monuments.
