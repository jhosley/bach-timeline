Six works for a violin and nothing else — no continuo, no accompaniment of any kind — and yet, against all reason, they are full of fugues, four-part chords, and harmony that wants for nothing. That is the standing paradox of the set Bach copied out in his exquisite fair-copy hand in 1720 under the title Sei Solo — "six solos." Violinists have long cherished what the title accidentally also says, since in Italian sei solo can be read as a sentence: you are alone. Whether or not Bach intended the pun, the music earns it. Composition likely stretched over the preceding years, but the 1720 manuscript presents the six as a finished, ordered whole: three sonatas alternating with three partitas. The sonatas each follow the four-movement church pattern, and in each of them the second movement is a fugue — polyphony's strictest discipline, assigned to the instrument least equipped to deliver it. The partitas are dance suites, and no two of them are designed alike.

How does one instrument, capable of sustaining only one or two notes at a time, play a fugue at all? The answer is the set's central miracle, and the single most important thing to listen for: implied polyphony. Bach writes single lines that outline multiple voices — a melody that leaps between registers in such a way that your ear separates the strands and supplies the counterpoint itself, hearing a tune in conversation with its own bass even though only one string is sounding. The violin plays one line; you hear two, three, four. It is a collaboration between composer and listener, and once you learn to hear it you cannot unhear it — and you begin noticing the same technique threaded through Bach's keyboard writing and his cello writing, where a single voice is forever being asked to behave like a choir.

The set contains two summits that the craft essays return to again and again. The fugue of the C-major sonata (BWV 1005) is the longest fugue Bach wrote for any instrument — not for organ, not for keyboard, but for a violin alone, as if to prove the paradox at maximum scale. And the D-minor partita ends in the Chaconne, a movement so vast and so storied that it has its own card (BWV 1004), including the memorial tradition that links it to the death of Maria Barbara, Bach's first wife, in the same year as the fair copy. The essays on fugue and on implied polyphony both anchor here: this is the corpus where Bach's contrapuntal thinking is stripped to a single unaccompanied line and still holds.

The reception story is one of the timeline's strangest. For roughly a century after Bach's death these works went nearly unplayed — regarded not as concert music but as études, finger-training for advanced students, too austere and too naked for the public stage. Nineteenth-century violinists began the rescue, with Mendelssohn and Schumann among the rescuers — but the terms of the rescue are telling: both wrote piano accompaniments for them. True solitude was, to Romantic ears, unthinkable; a violin reciting alone seemed to need harmonic clothing before it could decently appear in public. Only in the twentieth century did performers and audiences learn to hear the works bare, as Bach wrote them — to trust that the missing voices were never missing at all, but waiting in the listener's ear. Each of the six works has its own card, BWV 1001 through 1006, where the individual movements get their due.

The set's gravitational center has a card of its own: the D-minor Chaconne, BWV 1004.
