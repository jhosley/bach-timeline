Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Three Sonatas for Viola da Gamba and Harpsichord

BWV 1027–1029 · G, D, g · 1720
disputed
Forces — Viola da gamba and obbligato harpsichord
Dating — DISPUTED. Tradition places them at Cöthen (Leopold and the virtuoso C. F. Abel both played gamba); much modern scholarship dates the surviving versions to Leipzig, late 1730s–early 1740s. Filed under Cöthen for the traditional association, with this flag.
Recommended recording — Jordi Savall & Ton Koopman, Sonatas for Viola da Gamba and Harpsichord (Alia Vox)  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. Three sonatas pairing the harpsichord (again a fully written-out partner — see the violin sonatas card) with the viola da gamba: the six- or seven-string fretted bass viol, aristocratic, soft-grained, and in Bach's lifetime already fading before the louder cello. The first sonata is Bach's own arrangement of a trio sonata for two flutes — visible proof of the three-voice thinking underneath.

Listen for. The G-minor sonata (BWV 1029), the masterpiece of the three: its opening movement drives like a concerto, and its texture — gamba, keyboard right hand, keyboard left hand — is a three-way argument no voice wins. The tender Andante of BWV 1028 shows why gambists refuse to surrender this repertoire to cellists.

Dating. A model case for this timeline's honesty policy. The Cöthen story (music for Prince Leopold's own instrument, or for Abel) is attractive and may hold for lost early versions; the surviving sources point to Leipzig decades later. Both claims are shown, neither silently chosen — and the card teaches why dating Bach is hard: he revised constantly and rarely dated chamber scores.
