Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 in D major

What it is. The birth certificate of the keyboard concerto. The harpsichord had always been the ensemble's servant — the continuo instrument, filling harmony from the back. Here Bach promotes it to soloist alongside flute and violin, and then, near the end of the first movement, dismisses the entire orchestra for a colossal written-out cadenza — sixty-plus bars of solo harpsichord, the longest such eruption anyone had put on paper. The servant seizes the palace.

Listen for. The moment the accompaniment texture starts to molt: the harpsichord's figuration grows denser and more insistent through the movement until the other instruments simply fall silent. First listeners would have felt the transgression physically. The middle movement strips to the three soloists alone — chamber music inside the concerto — and the finale is a gigue-fugue hybrid whose swing conceals its rigor.

Craft. A case study in dramatized texture: the piece's subject is the social order of the ensemble itself. And a biographical portrait — the man at the harpsichord in Cöthen was Bach, playing the court's glamorous new Berlin instrument. Every later keyboard concerto — Mozart's, Beethoven's — descends from this movement; his own Leipzig harpsichord concertos (Arc I, Leipzig II) formalize what is seized here.
