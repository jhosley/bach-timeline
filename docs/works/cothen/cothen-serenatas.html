Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

The Cöthen Serenatas (birthday and New Year cantatas for Prince Leopold)

BWV 66a, 134a, 173a, et al. · · 1718
documented
Forces — Solo voices, small orchestra
Occasion — Prince Leopold's birthday (10 December) and New Year's Day
Dating — Composed for court occasions 1718–1722; most survive only through their later Leipzig sacred parodies.
Recommended recording — (surviving parodies) Various — see individual Leipzig cantata cards in R3  ·  Recordings Shelf
Score — IMSLP (free score)

What it is. The one regular vocal duty of the Cöthen years: congratulatory serenatas — festive secular cantatas of arias, duets, and recitatives on allegorical texts (Time, Fame, Fortune, and friends singing the prince's praises) — for Leopold's birthday each 10 December and for New Year's Day. Anna Magdalena, court soprano, sang in them; this music is likely how the Bachs met.

Listen for. Mostly, you can't — not in original form. The scores were occasional pieces, used once; what survives is their afterlife: back in Leipzig, needing music fast, Bach re-texted the best of them as church works. Der Himmel dacht auf Anhalts Ruhm (BWV 66a, birthday 1718) became the Easter cantata Erfreut euch, ihr Herzen (BWV 66); the New Year serenata BWV 134a became the Easter cantata BWV 134; Durchlauchtster Leopold (BWV 173a) became the Pentecost cantata BWV 173.

Craft. The timeline's introduction to parody technique — Bach's systematic re-texting of existing music for new purposes, which peaks with the B minor Mass and Christmas Oratorio. The scandal it presents to Romantic notions of sincerity (praise for a prince becoming praise for the risen Christ, unchanged music) dissolves once you grasp the era's view: well-made music was craft object, transferable to any worthy text — and studying what Bach altered in transfer is one of the best windows into what he thought music means.
