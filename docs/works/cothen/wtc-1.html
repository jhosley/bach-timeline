Twenty-four preludes and twenty-four fugues, one pair in every major and minor key, ascending chromatically from C major to B minor: that is the whole design, and its simplicity is deceptive, because nothing like it had been carried through before. Each prelude is a free character piece — this one an arpeggio study, that one an aria, another a two-part invention, another a French overture in miniature — bound by no rule except its key. Each fugue is the opposite: a strict demonstration of imitative counterpoint in anywhere from two to five voices, the most law-governed music Bach knew how to write. The pairing is the point. Fantasy and law, freedom and rigor, set on facing pages twenty-four times over, as if to insist that neither is complete without the other.

Bach dated the autograph fair copy 1722 and put its purpose on the title page: "for the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning." Many of the preludes had already existed in earlier form in the little keyboard book he compiled for his eldest son Wilhelm Friedemann in 1720 — this was teaching music first, drawn from the household workshop, and its systematic sweep through all twenty-four keys doubled as an argument for the "well-tempered" tuning that made every key playable on one instrument. That practical claim is why the book's very existence is a landmark: before well-tempering, the remote keys were not so much avoided as effectively uninhabitable.

Do not try to hear it as a sequence; treat it as a gallery, and take a first visit in a few marked rooms. Start where Bach starts, with the C-major prelude — pure arpeggiated harmony, chords unrolled into rippling motion, the keyboard twin of the first cello suite's prelude. Then C-sharp major, a key that had barely existed as usable music before well-tempering, here given a glittering pair as if to christen new territory. Then the E-flat minor prelude, one of the book's grief-laden depths, followed by its austere fugue. And finally the end of the journey: the B-minor fugue, whose chromatic subject contains all twelve tones — so that the book closes at the very edge of the tonal world it has just finished mapping, gesturing at the chromatic universe beyond its own borders.

As craft, this is the central text twice over: the fugue essay and the temperament essay are both, at bottom, commentaries on this book. Individual cards for all forty-eight preludes and fugues arrive in phase R3 of the timeline; this pilot card holds the set's place until then.

The reception story is a paradox worth savoring. The book was not published until 1801 — eighty years after its title page — and yet it is the most influential "book" in the history of keyboard music, because for those eighty years it circulated the old way, in hand copies, passed from teacher to student across Europe like a manuscript gospel. Mozart encountered its fugues and arranged them for strings. Beethoven played the whole book as a boy and made his reputation on it. Chopin's Préludes are a direct answer to it; Shostakovich's twenty-four preludes and fugues, op. 87, answer it again in the twentieth century. Composer after composer, era after era, has treated the same forty-eight pieces as the standing challenge: here is every key; what can you say in them? When the ribbon of Arc II dims — the decades when Bach's music slid toward oblivion — this is the brightest thread still glowing in the dark, the one part of the legacy that never needed reviving because it never stopped being copied, studied, and played.
