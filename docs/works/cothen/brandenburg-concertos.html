Six concertos, six ensembles, and no two of them alike. The title Bach wrote at the head of the set he dedicated to Margrave Christian Ludwig of Brandenburg on 24 March 1721 — Six Concerts avec plusieurs instruments, six concertos "with several instruments" — sounds like a shrug of description, and is in fact a manifesto. What he had assembled, from music composed and revised across the Weimar and Cöthen years, some of it reworked from earlier material, was the most systematic exploration of what "concerto" could mean that anyone had attempted. Several of the instrumental combinations inside the set were not merely unusual; they were unprecedented — line-ups no composer had thought to put on a page before.

The raw material was Italian. In Weimar, Bach had absorbed — above all from Vivaldi — the concerto grosso: a small solo group set against the full ensemble, the whole thing organized by the ritornello principle, a recurring orchestral refrain that frames the solo episodes so the ear always knows where home is and how far the soloists have wandered from it. Most composers inherited that design as a settled convention, a reliable machine for making orchestral music. Bach treated every term of the contract as negotiable. Across the six concertos he pushed each variable the form possessed: who gets to solo, who must accompany, and whether the distinction between the two even holds.

That is why the set rewards being heard in order, as a designed survey rather than an anthology. The First is the most orchestral and courtly of the six, the concerto closest to its ceremonial origins. The Second balances four wildly unequal soloists against one another and dares them to blend. The Third abolishes soloists entirely: nine equal strings, every player a principal, the solo–ensemble boundary simply dissolved. The Fourth runs the experiment in reverse, hiding a full violin concerto inside a chamber texture. The Fifth lets the harpsichord — until then the ensemble's dutiful accompanist — stage a coup and seize the stage. And the Sixth removes the violins altogether, so that the ensemble's dark lower half, the voices that normally serve, get to speak for themselves. Six line-ups, six designs, one question asked six different ways: what is the relationship between the one and the many? Which is also, not coincidentally, the core question of counterpoint — the discipline of independent voices sharing one music — so that the set's instrumentation games and Bach's deepest compositional instincts turn out to be the same inquiry conducted at different scales.

Two things are worth holding together about the workmanship. The first is the encyclopedic instinct — the drive to take a form, map its whole territory, and present the map as a single ordered set — which the era essay traces through nearly everything Bach touched in these years. The second is how unspeculative it all was. These were not abstractions composed for an unknown patron's hypothetical band. They were repertory pieces, written for the actual personnel of the Cöthen Capelle, the virtuosi Bach rehearsed and performed with week by week; the exotic scorings are a roster, not a fantasy. Each concerto has its own card detailing its particular design — the survey above is only the aerial view.

The reception history begins with silence. The Margrave, so far as anyone can tell, did nothing with his gift, and the set went unpublished until 1850, more than a century after its dedication. Then the twentieth century turned the neglect inside out. The Brandenburgs became the gateway drug of the early-music revival — for generations of listeners, the first place they heard what old instruments and old proportions could do, the door through which the whole historical-performance movement was entered. And in 1977 the reversal became cosmic. The first movement of No. 2 opens the Voyager Golden Record: the first music our species chose to send out of the solar system. A set of concertos its dedicatee never bothered to hear is now, quite literally, the leading edge of humanity's introduction of itself — Arc III of this timeline, compressed into a single launch.
