Cöthen: the instrumental golden age

What it is. A quiet revolution in chamber music. The standard Baroque sonata gave the violin a melody and the keyboard a figured bass — a shorthand harmony to improvise from, the keyboardist as accompanist. Bach instead writes out the harpsichord's right hand as an equal melodic voice, creating three real parts: violin, harpsichord right hand, harpsichord left hand. The result is a trio sonata for two players — and the direct ancestor of the duo sonata as Mozart, Beethoven, and Brahms understood it: two partners, not soloist-plus-servant.

Listen for. Any slow movement (start with the first sonata's opening Adagio, the violin keening over paired harpsichord voices): count the independent lines — almost always three, conversing as equals. The finales are compact three-voice fugues in all but name.

Craft. The set's premise is a counterpoint lesson: equality of voices as a compositional ethic, extended even to the social arrangement of the players. The trio-texture essay treats these sonatas alongside the organ trio sonatas as Bach's purest three-voice thinking.
