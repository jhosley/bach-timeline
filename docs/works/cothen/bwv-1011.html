What it is. The dark tower of the set. Bach instructs the cellist to retune (scordatura — the top A string lowered to G), dimming the instrument's brightest register and putting unusual chords under the fingers. Galanterie pair: two Gavottes.

Listen for. The Prelude is a French overture: a slow, jagged, dotted-rhythm proclamation — the style invented to announce Louis XIV — followed by a fast fugal section, which on an unaccompanied cello means a fugue implied by one line dropping subject entries into different registers. The Sarabande is the most famous desolation in the cello literature: no chords at all, a single bare line circling dissonant intervals — counterpoint reduced to its ghost. Casals reportedly chose it among his last performances; Rostropovich called the suite's darkness sacred.

Craft. Two craft essays anchor here: the fugue essay (for the Prelude's one-voice fugue — the technique at its most ascetic) and the dance essay (for a sarabande stripped to pure skeleton). Bach's own lute arrangement (BWV 995) lets students compare the harmonies he heard with the line he wrote.
