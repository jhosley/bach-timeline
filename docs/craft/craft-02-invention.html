The Inventions as a method: finding, developing, singing

Bach left almost no words about how music should be made — no treatise, no essays, a handful of terse letters. The great exception is one page long: the title page of the Inventions and Sinfonias (1723), the closest thing we have to his teaching philosophy in his own hand. It deserves reading slowly, because every clause is a claim.

An upright guide, it begins, by which lovers of the keyboard are shown a plain way "(1) to learn to play cleanly in two voices, but also, after further progress, (2) to deal correctly and well with three obbligato parts" — obbligato meaning obligatory: three real voices, none of them filler — and along the way "to acquire good inventiones, and to develop the same well," and "most of all to achieve a cantabile style of playing, and alongside all this to gain a strong foretaste of composition."

Three promises, ascending. The first is mechanical: hand-independence, two voices then three. The third is the one every pianist quotes: cantabile — the singing touch. But the middle promise is the radical one, and it hides in a Latin word borrowed from an unexpected discipline. Inventio is a term of classical rhetoric — the orator's first task: not "making something up," but finding the core idea of your argument, the seed from which everything else can be developed (elaboratio) and arranged (dispositio). Bach and his contemporaries — schooled, as he was, on Cicero — thought about composing in exactly these terms. A piece of music was an argument; its opening idea was its thesis; the composer's craft was to find a thesis rich enough to sustain development, then develop it exhaustively.

Now watch the method work. Open Invention No. 1: the first seven notes — a rising scale-step figure with a little arpeggio turn — are the inventio, and the entire piece is that figure examined: traded between the hands, run up and down in sequence, turned upside down (inversion: every rising interval becomes a falling one, and vice versa — the melodic shape mirrored). Nothing else happens in the piece. That's the lesson: not "here is a nice tune," but here is how much music one well-chosen idea contains. Each of the fifteen Inventions poses the same problem with a different kind of seed — a canon-ready line, a chase figure, a chromatic lament — and each Sinfonia re-poses it with three voices, culminating in the F minor's three simultaneous subjects carrying real grief. Development is expression; the method and the meaning never separate.

Two features of this pedagogy are worth stealing for any kind of study. First, it teaches composition and performance as one literacy. A student who has traced how Invention 1 grows from its seed doesn't just play it better — they can write a passable invention, and Bach's students did, as assignments. He would give them a figured bass or a subject and demand elaboration; composition was learned the way languages are: by speaking, from day one, inside strict grammar. Second, the constraints are the curriculum. Two voices only; one idea only; every note accounted for. Freedom, in this classroom, is what you earn by exhausting a constraint — a principle that scales all the way up: the Goldberg Variations are the Inventions' method under a thirty-two-bar constraint; the Art of Fugue under a single subject for an entire book.

The pipeline these little pieces fed was concrete and domestic: begun in Friedemann's notebook for a nine-year-old, refined for the parade of private students who lived and learned in the Bach household, and aimed squarely at the Well-Tempered Clavier, whose title page — "for the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning" — is the same voice, one flight up. The whole keyboard curriculum, from first two-voice piece to five-voice fugue, was a single graded staircase, and it still works: it is, essentially unchanged, the counterpoint sequence conservatories teach today.

One last thing the title page says between the lines. Bach wrote it at Cöthen, a Kapellmeister with no church duties, at the height of his instrumental powers — and what he chose to perfect, alongside the Brandenburgs and the solo-violin monuments, was a beginners' book. Teaching was not what Bach did when he couldn't compose; it was one of the forms his composing took. The thirty little pieces are as finished as anything he made — which is why, alone in his output, they have never once been out of use since the ink dried.
