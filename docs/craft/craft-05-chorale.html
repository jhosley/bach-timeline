The chorale as classroom

Every tradition of teaching harmony on earth — conservatory, university, YouTube — eventually hands the student the same assignment: harmonize a chorale in four parts, in the style of Bach. It's worth pausing on how strange that is. Three centuries on, the universal grammar-school of Western harmony is still one Lutheran cantor's way of clothing his congregation's hymns. This essay is about why that happened — and why, for Bach himself, the chorale was even more than that: raw material, structural steel, shared language, and classroom, all in one.

Start with what a chorale is, because the word means two things. First: the hymn itself — melody plus German text, made for whole congregations to sing. Luther's Reformation (this timeline's Eisenach inheritance) put vernacular song at the center of worship, and by Bach's day Lutheran Germany owned a repertoire of hundreds of these tunes that everyone — literally everyone — knew by heart from childhood. Keep that fact loaded whenever you study this music: the chorales were the shared memory bank of an entire civilization, and quoting one was like quoting scripture or proverb — instantly recognized, arriving with its text and associations attached even when played without words.

Second: the four-part setting — the hymn harmonized for soprano, alto, tenor, bass, the melody on top, the other three voices moving in the same rhythm beneath. Bach wrote hundreds (the ~371 collected after his death became the textbook), mostly as the closing movements of cantatas: the congregation's tune, returned to them at the sermon's end, in harmony finer than they could make but singable in spirit by all. And here is why they became the universal exercise: a Bach chorale is maximum harmonic content at minimum scale. Four voices, four phrases, forty seconds — and inside: every cadence type, passing tones, suspensions, secondary dominants, the occasional breathtaking chromatic swerve (Arnstadt's consistory complained about exactly these). Each setting is a complete, graded harmony lesson that also happens to be a finished work of art. Compare three of his harmonizations of the same tune — the Passion chorale's five appearances in the St. Matthew, each darker — and you're watching harmony mean something: same melody, different griefs.

But settings were only the chorale's smallest job in Bach's workshop. Ascending by scale: the chorale prelude (the Orgelbüchlein's form) treats the tune as a text to be glossed — presented once, whole, while one invented accompanying figure interprets it: falling sevenths under the Fall, tolling bells under mortality. The chorale partita (Böhm's legacy) runs the tune through variation after variation. The chorale fantasia — the great opening choruses of the 1724–25 cycle — scales the gloss up to civic size: the congregation's tune in long notes, an independent orchestral and choral world built around it (BWV 20's French overture, BWV 78's lament-chaconne, BWV 140's watchmen-march). And the per omnes versus cantata (BWV 4) commits a whole work to a single hymn, every movement a different technique. One tune-repertoire, five structural technologies — the chorale was to Bach's vocal music what the ground bass was to his instrumental thinking: the fixed point that makes invention legible.

For his first audiences, all of this carried a dimension we have to reconstruct deliberately: recognition. When BWV 127's orchestra threads the Passion chorale under a different hymn, or the Christmas Oratorio opens its cradle-scenes with the same melody that will bleed at Golgotha, Leipzig heard the allusion the way you catch a film score quoting its own love-theme at the deathbed. Studying the cantatas without knowing the core chorales is studying poetry in a language whose proverbs you've never heard. The practical advice is almost absurdly simple: learn ten tunes — sing them until they're yours (O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden, Wachet auf, Nun komm der Heiden Heiland, Ein feste Burg, Christ lag in Todes Banden, Wie schön leuchtet, Jesu meine Freude, Was Gott tut, O Ewigkeit du Donnerwort, Vom Himmel hoch) — and a third of this timeline's vocal catalog opens like a door you'd been pushing instead of pulling.

And for the composer himself? The chorale was where his art and his stated purpose — a well-regulated church music to the glory of God — fused most completely: the one place where the highest craft in Europe and the voice of the whole congregation were, by construction, the same music. The four-part chorale is Bach's counterpoint democratized — everyone gets a real line, and the lines together make the faith audible. That, more than pedagogy, is why it never left the classroom: it remains the smallest complete model of what he thought music was for.
