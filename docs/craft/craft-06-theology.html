Counterpoint as theology

Handle this topic with tongs, because it attracts two opposite errors. The first — the Romantic error — makes Bach a mystic whose every interval encodes a sermon, finds the Trinity in every group of three, and counts letter-numbers until B+A+C+H=14 explains the universe. It is the error of the nineteenth century's "Fifth Evangelist" cult, and it survives today in program notes that treat the scores as ciphers waiting for a key. The second — the modernist error — makes him a pure structuralist whose church employment was incidental, the texts mere scaffolding for abstract design: a mind that happened to be paid by a church the way a physicist happens to be paid by a university. This error had its heyday in the mid-twentieth century, when Bach was played on synthesizers and analyzed as proto-mathematics, and it survives in the instinct that the "real" Bach is the instrumental Bach, with the cantatas as contractual obligation.

The documents support neither, and this timeline's whole method — documented, tradition, disputed — matters more here than anywhere else in the Craft section. Theology is where wishful thinking runs hottest in Bach studies, in both directions: those who want him devout and those who want him secular have each, historically, found what they came for. So this essay proceeds the slow way. First, what we actually know — the physical evidence. Then the frame that evidence sits in: what Lutheranism taught about music, and how a church calendar became a production system. Then the interpretive layers, in strict order of decreasing certainty. And at the end, what any of it is worth to you at the music desk.

The evidence: a pastor's shelf in a musician's house

Start with his library, because it is the least ambiguous witness. When Bach died in 1750, the court that settled his estate inventoried his possessions, and the inventory survives. It lists over eighty theological volumes — two complete editions of Luther's collected works, and the major commentators of Lutheran orthodoxy alongside them. This was not a gentleman's decorative shelf. It was a working pastor's reference library, in a musician's house, acquired at a musician's expense; some of the volumes were costly, and Bach was not a man who spent money ornamentally. A theologian could have preached from that shelf. The cantor who owned it evidently read against it — because we can watch him reading.

We have his own Bible: the three-volume study edition prepared by the seventeenth-century orthodox theologian Abraham Calov, scripture interleaved with commentary drawn largely from Luther. The set vanished after Bach's death and resurfaced, improbably, in Michigan in the 1930s, where scholars confirmed the ownership inscriptions and — decisively — annotations in Bach's own hand: corrections to the printed text, underlinings, and marginal comments. Two of the annotations bear directly on this essay. Beside 1 Chronicles 25 — the chapter where King David appoints the temple musicians, organizing them by families into a rota — Bach wrote: "This chapter is the true foundation of all God-pleasing church music." A working Kapellmeister reading an ancient personnel roster and recognizing his own job in it. And beside 2 Chronicles 5, where music fills the newly built temple and the glory of God descends in a cloud, he wrote: "NB. Where there is devotional music, God with his grace is always present." That sentence, in his own ink, is the closest thing we have to Bach's theology of his art — and note carefully what it claims. Not that music describes grace, or symbolizes it, or reminds the congregation of it. That it is an occasion of presence. For the man who wrote that marginal note, a cantata was not about something sacred; it was where something sacred happened.

Around the library stand the habits. At the head of his scores Bach regularly wrote J.J. — Jesu juva, "Jesus, help" — and at the end, S.D.G. — Soli Deo Gloria, "to God alone the glory." Hold on to the second half of that sentence: the inscriptions appear not only on church cantatas but on secular and instrumental works as well, a fact whose meaning this essay will circle back to, because it quietly detonates the sacred/secular border. And we have the declarations of purpose from his own pen: the 1708 resignation letter from Mühlhausen, naming his life's Endzweck — final purpose — as "a well-regulated church music to the glory of God"; and the Orgelbüchlein's title-page couplet, dedicating the collection to the honor of the most high God and the instruction of the neighbor. One more institutional fact belongs in the pile: on taking the Leipzig cantorate in 1723, Bach was formally examined in doctrine and subscribed to the Lutheran confessional documents. A Thomaskantor was not a contractor providing music services; he was an officer of the church, doctrinally vetted, whose product was understood as part of the proclamation itself.

That is the documented frame, and it is solid. The question this essay must handle with tongs is how far down into the notes it goes.

Music next to theology: Luther's wager

None of the above makes sense without the two-hundred-year-old wager underneath it. Liturgy — the fixed public order of worship, the sequence of readings, prayers, and music a congregation moves through — was, for most Christian traditions after the Reformation, a zone of suspicion where music was concerned. Several reforming movements stripped music from worship as sensual distraction. Luther bet the other way, and bet everything. He was a trained singer and a passionate amateur musician; he wrote hymns himself; and he made his position doctrinal, declaring music worthy of the highest honor next to theology — the formulation is his own, from a 1530 letter to the composer Ludwig Senfl, and he repeated the idea throughout his life. Music, for Luther, was not decoration on the gospel. It was a divine gift with the power to drive out the devil and gladden the troubled heart — something very close to a means by which the gospel actually reaches people.

The institutional consequence was the chorale: the congregational hymn in German, plain and strong, meant for every voice in the room. The essay before this one traces what that repertoire became in Bach's hands; the Eisenach card shows him absorbing it as a schoolboy in Luther's own town, under the castle where Luther translated the Bible. The point to carry forward here is vocational. In a church where music ranks next to theology, the town cantor is something like an associate preacher. His weekly cantata was heard as the sermon's musical twin — an exposition of the same scripture the preacher would take up an hour later. Bach's job title made him a civil servant; his job description, read theologically, made him an expositor of the Word in tones. Everything strange about his career — the eighty theological volumes, the annotated Bible, the fury of his fights with employers over the dignity of the music budget — becomes ordinary the moment you take that job description at face value.

One complication, because Bach lived it. Lutheranism in his lifetime was split between two tendencies. Orthodoxy — in this context a technical term, not an insult — held to formal doctrine, the full liturgical inheritance, and elaborate church music as a fitting offering. Pietism, a reform movement from the late seventeenth century, urged inward personal devotion, plain worship, and practical holiness — and its stricter voices suspected concerted church music of being worldly display, opera smuggled into the sanctuary. The split was not academic for Bach: at Mühlhausen his own pastor leaned Pietist while the music-loving clergyman served the other church, and the resignation letter is the sound of an ambitious church musician discovering he stood in the wrong pulpit's parish. Institutionally, then, Bach belonged with orthodoxy — his library, his employers, his confessional signature all say so. But listen to the texts he set: the arias speak the intimate, first-person devotional language the Pietists prized — the soul addressing Jesus as bridegroom, the single trembling conscience. The scholarship's sober conclusion is that the binary was a spectrum and Bach occupied a broad stretch of it: orthodox in doctrine and loyalty, Pietist-adjacent in devotional temperature. Keep that in mind whenever a program note assigns him a party.

The calendar as compositional engine

Now the machine that turned doctrine into deadlines. The church year — the liturgical calendar — is the annual cycle of seasons and feasts through which Lutheran worship moved: Advent (the four weeks of preparation before Christmas), Christmas, Epiphany, the pre-Lenten Sundays, Lent (the forty penitential days before Easter), Easter, Ascension, Pentecost, Trinity, and then the long count of Sundays after Trinity until Advent comes round again. Attached to the calendar was a lectionary: a fixed table assigning each Sunday and feast its scripture readings — an Epistle and, crucially, a Gospel. In Leipzig the same Gospel returned to the same Sunday every year. The system was old, stable, and universally known, and it is the single most underrated fact in Bach studies, because it means that every sacred cantata has a Sunday, and every Sunday has a text. A cantata was not a freestanding composition on a religious theme. It was a commentary on that week's Gospel, performed inside the main service — which in Leipzig began at seven in the morning and ran toward noon — positioned after the Gospel reading and before the sermon, expounding what had just been read and priming what was about to be preached. We even have Bach's own note of the Leipzig order of service, jotted on a cantata cover during his first Advent in the job: the composer writing out the liturgy like a stage manager marking his cue.

Understand the calendar and you understand the workload. The cantata machine — a new work nearly every week for years, composed, copied, rehearsed, and performed inside seven days — was not an artistic choice; it was the calendar's demand, and the calendar was also the source of the machine's variety. The lectionary handed Bach a different text, therefore a different problem, every week: comfort one Sunday, judgment the next, a wedding at Cana, a storm on a lake. Composers who work to commission know how generative constraint can be; Bach had the most articulate constraint system in music history, and it renewed itself annually.

The calendar also shaped the year's dramatic contour, because it included silence. In Leipzig, Advent after its first Sunday and the whole of Lent were tempus clausum — "closed time" — when concerted music fell silent in the churches as an act of communal restraint. Two consequences matter for your listening. First, the outbursts: the first Sunday of Advent was a threshold moment — the church year's new-year's-day — and Bach treated it as such (BWV 61 opens the year by dressing Luther's ancient Advent chorale as a French royal overture: the King arrives); and Christmas, arriving after weeks of silence, detonated — the Christmas Oratorio spreads one work across six feast days from Christmas morning to Epiphany, using the calendar itself as its large-scale form, the season as the concert hall. Second, the Passion. Because Lent was silent, the year's greatest music was concentrated into a single evening: Good Friday Vespers, the afternoon service where, in a Leipzig practice only a couple of years old when Bach arrived, the Passion story was sung complete. All that withheld music, all that liturgical darkness, discharging in one three-hour work once a year — the 1724 St. John premiere and the 1727 St. Matthew were the biggest musical events of their respective years by design of the calendar itself. No composer ever had a better dramaturg than the church year.

Three layers of certainty

So the frame is documented and the machine is documented. The contested question is the one this essay's title asks: does the theology reach into the counterpoint — and how would we know? Here is a sober middle way, in three layers of decreasing certainty. The layering is the method: refuse to let claims from different layers borrow each other's confidence.

Layer one: rhetoric you can hear

Bach's era understood music through the lens of rhetoric — the ancient discipline of persuasive speech. A composition was conceived as an oration: it had a thesis (the theme), an argument (the development), and a job to do — namely, to move the listener. The listener's movable states were the affections (Affekten): the codified passions of Baroque psychology — grief, joy, rage, longing — which music was thought to arouse by technical means, a body of theory modern scholars call the Affektenlehre, the doctrine of the affections. And to arouse them, composers deployed figures: conventional musical gestures with conventional meanings, catalogued by German theorists from Joachim Burmeister's Musica poetica (1606) onward, in an analytical tradition now called the Figurenlehre — the doctrine of figures. A descending chromatic line signified lament; harsh dissonance, sin and piercing; a rising scale, ascent and resurrection; a rapid running bass, flight or rage.

Two honesty-flags before the examples, because this is where the tongs come out. First: the neat word Figurenlehre suggests a single agreed system, and there was none — the treatises span more than a century, disagree with one another, and were written by theorists, not by Bach, who left no treatise at all. Modern scholarship treats the "doctrine of figures" less as a rulebook Bach followed than as a family of period habits — a lens, not a decoder ring. Second: the lens is nonetheless period-authentic, not a modern imposition. When Scheibe attacked Bach in 1737, the defense came from Johann Abraham Birnbaum, a Leipzig rhetoric lecturer, who testified in print that Bach knew the parallels between musical composition and the parts of an oration so thoroughly that it was a pleasure to hear him talk about them. That is a documented window into how Bach's own circle framed his art: as eloquence.

And the practice is audible, with documentable consistency. The grinding chromatic descent under "weeping, wailing" in BWV 12 — the lament bass, a figure a century old when Bach used it. The stabbing dissonances of Buß und Reu — penitence and remorse — in the St. Matthew Passion, where contrition is made physically sharp. The halo of sustained strings around Christ's words in the same work — and its extinction at the cry from the cross. The pizzicato strings in BWV 61 knocking, knuckle on wood, under "Behold, I stand at the door and knock." The viola da gamba — the old instrument of mourning — carrying Es ist vollbracht in the St. John Passion, until "the hero of Judah triumphs" and the music breaks into trumpet-figures. This layer — call it text-painting at the local level, rhetoric at the level of the whole discourse — is essentially certain. It was the period's public musical language, taught in its textbooks, and reading it is simply literacy.

Layer two: architecture that preaches

The second layer claims more: not that Bach illustrated words, but that the structure of a work — its form, its symmetries, its long-range returns — sometimes carries the theological argument itself. This is interpretive, and must be argued piece by piece with the score open. But in the strongest cases the design is too pointed to dismiss.

The compact exhibits first. The St. Anne fugue: a triple fugue — three subjects, progressively revealed and finally combined — in a key of three flats, standing at the end of a published volume of music on the catechism, Lutheranism's summary of doctrine. Three-in-one construction, in that context, is Trinitarian architecture, and blatantly so — symbolism meant to be noticed, which is precisely what distinguishes it from the cipher-hunting of layer three. The Actus tragicus: at the center of this early funeral cantata, the choir fugues on the old covenant's sentence — "it is the ancient law: man, thou must die" — while above it a solo soprano sings the new covenant's answer — "yes, come, Lord Jesus." The fugue dissolves; the solo voice remains. Law and grace, the fundamental Lutheran distinction, argued contrapuntally — the theology is not set to the music; it is enacted as the relationship between the voices.

Now the great forms. The two surviving Passions have opposite theological shapes, and the shapes match their Gospels. John's Gospel presents a sovereign Christ — in control, reigning from the cross, his trial the world's trial — and the St. John Passion is correspondingly swift, confrontational, and architectural: the crowd choruses of the trial scene (the turba, Latin for "crowd" — the choir playing the mob) are arranged in a mirror-symmetric arch, the same music returning with opposite texts, and at the arch's exact keystone sits a chorale on freedom won through Christ's captivity. The drama's most chaotic stretch is secretly its most ordered — which is Johannine theology: beneath the apparent triumph of chaos, a hidden sovereignty. Matthew's Gospel dwells on human response — Peter's tears, the disciples' failure — and the St. Matthew Passion accordingly braids three musics: the narrative itself, carried by the Evangelist; the arias, in which a single conscience takes the story personally; and the chorales, in which the whole community answers. Narrative, individual, congregation — a complete Lutheran anthropology of how scripture is received, built as a formal scheme.

And then the Mass in B minor, where structure preaches at the scale of a lifetime. Assembled in 1748–49 from three decades of his finest sacred music — the Missa presented at Dresden in 1733, a Sanctus from 1724, movements re-texted from cantatas back to 1714 — the Mass lays out an architecture of styles: Renaissance stile antico (the "old style" of church polyphony, with plainchant running in canon through the Confiteor) beside high-Baroque concerto choruses beside the modern galant manner of his sons' generation. Sacred music's whole history, curated into one work — a claim, in structure, about the church's song across time. At its center, the Crucifixus sinks through a chromatic ground bass into the key of burial, and Et resurrexit detonates without a breath between. And at the end, the Dona nobis pacem — "grant us peace" — returns the music of the Gratias — "we give thanks" — so that supplication and gratitude are revealed as one act. Gardiner's phrase for this whole layer is the right one: Bach as a composer who "makes theology audible." Layer two is where that phrase earns its keep — strong, but interpretive, and honest analysis says so.

Layer three: numbers, held with tongs

The third layer is the famous one, and most of it the timeline flags tradition or worse. The claim: Bach encoded meanings numerically — counting notes, bars, and entries, and using the number alphabet (a natural-order cipher, A=1, B=2, and so on), by which B+A+C+H = 2+1+3+8 = 14, and J+S+B+A+C+H = 41, its mirror. The traditions cluster around real documented facts: Bach really did become the fourteenth member of Mizler's learned society, and the tradition that he waited for that number is just that — a tradition, unprovable either way. And some numerical symbolism is real precisely because it is blatant: the three flats and three subjects of the St. Anne fugue are meant to be noticed, like a triptych's three panels.

But the hidden-number industry is another matter, and here the provenance history is itself instructive. The systematic number-hunting dates largely to the mid-twentieth century, when the scholar Friedrich Smend counted his way through the scores and found 14s and 41s everywhere. Decades later, Ruth Tatlow's methodical study of the number-alphabet tradition showed how weak the historical footing was: no document connects Bach to gematria practice, the counting methods were flexible enough to find any desired number, and a technique that can prove anything proves nothing. That is not quite a debunking of every numerical observation — proportional design in Bach remains a live and serious research area — but it is a debunking of the decoder-ring method. The working rule of thumb this timeline uses: if a symbol required a calculator to find, Bach's congregation couldn't hear it — and everything documented about Bach, from the Orgelbüchlein's dedication to the neighbor's instruction onward, says he wrote to be heard. Treat layer three, in the main, as reception history: evidence about what later generations needed Bach to be. That story — the posthumous manufacture of Bachs — is exactly where this timeline's next arc begins.

The weekday and the altar: parody without hypocrisy

One finding reframes the whole question, and it is among the best-documented things in this essay: the sacred/secular line barely exists in Bach's practice. The same S.D.G. that closes church cantatas closes a princely birthday serenata. A coffee-house comedy and a five-voice Kyrie come from the same pen in the same seasons. And above all there is parody technique — the standard eighteenth-century term (no mockery implied) for re-texting existing music: fitting new words to old notes, usually moving a piece from one occasion to another.

Bach's parody practice ran overwhelmingly in one direction — secular to sacred — and at the highest level of his art. The Christmas Oratorio is the definitive exhibit: its most brilliant choruses began as birthday and homage cantatas for the Saxon royal family, written in the two years before — "Sound, ye drums!" for the queen becoming "Shout, rejoice!" for the Christ-child. The four Lutheran Masses are assembled almost entirely from his own cantata movements. The lost St. Mark Passion was built substantially from the Trauer-Ode, a funeral ode for a queen. And the B minor Mass — the summa itself — is parody nearly throughout, three decades of music re-texted into the Latin Ordinary.

To modern instincts this looks like a scandal in need of explanation: how does music written to flatter a prince become the Christmas Gospel without hypocrisy? The evidence dissolves the scandal twice over. Practically, scholars believe the major transfers were planned — the court works written with their sacred afterlife already in view, first-class music parked in one-performance occasions until its permanent text arrived; the surviving sources show Bach re-fitting declamation syllable by syllable, craftsman's work, not paste-up. Theologically, the Lutheran doctrine of vocation (Beruf — calling) had already erased the ontological line the scandal assumes: in Luther's teaching, all honest work — the cobbler's, the prince's musician's — is divine service, and the "secular" is not the ungodly but simply the weekday. Music well made was already praise; changing the text merely changed the addressee of record. When Bach wrote Soli Deo Gloria under a birthday serenata, the evidence suggests he meant it.

Which yields this essay's real conclusion. If counterpoint was theology for Bach, it was not because fugues smuggled sermons in code. It was because ordered, independent voices cohering into harmony was his experience of how creation is put together — a conviction he could exercise identically in church, court, or coffee house, because it was prior to all three rooms. The Calov annotation says where he thought that order pointed. The parody workshop shows he thought it portable. The late summa shows him, at the end, offering the order itself — fugue as such, the Mass as such — as the largest S.D.G. he could construct.

At the music desk: using theology in performance and analysis

Finally, the payoff for your own work, because everything above converts into practice. Five habits.

Read the Sunday before you read the score. For any cantata, find its liturgical occasion and the prescribed Gospel (Dürr's handbook gives both; so do most editions). Then ask of each movement: what job does it do in the sermon's argument — proclamation, application, communal response? Analysis that starts from the Gospel almost always discovers why the movements are in this order and this key; analysis that ignores it is describing a machine without knowing what it manufactures.

Learn the chorale — the whole hymn. When a cantata or Passion quotes a chorale, find the complete text, including the stanzas Bach did not set: for his listeners the unsung stanzas resonated behind the sung one, and allusions the modern analyst labors to justify were, as the previous essay argues, simply audible. A performer who knows what O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden says in all its verses phrases its five Passion harmonizations differently — and should.

Treat layer one as performance instruction. The figures are not trivia; they are the composer's expressive markings, written in the period's public language. A lament bass wants weight and inevitability; the dissonances of penitence want to hurt slightly before they resolve; a turba chorus wants the diction of a mob, not a madrigal choir. Reading the rhetoric fluently is the closest thing we have to Bach's expression marks, which his notation mostly omits.

Argue layer two with the score open — and against yourself. Structural-theological claims are legitimate and powerful, but they are earned one piece at a time. Before asserting that a symmetry preaches, check that the symmetry is really there, that it is specific to this work rather than generic to the form, and that you could defend it to the most source-skeptical reader you know. The difference between the St. John's documented arch and a numerologist's harvested 14s is exactly this discipline.

File layer three under reception history — and use it there. The number traditions tell you little about Bach's desk and a great deal about his afterlife: what the nineteenth century's mystics and the twentieth century's cryptographers each needed the music to contain. That story is genuinely illuminating — it is the story of the eclipse and the revival — as long as it is shelved as their history rather than his intention.

And one habit beneath the five. You do not have to share Bach's faith to perform or analyze this music seriously — but you do have to know that the music itself, on the evidence of its maker's own marginal note, believes it is doing something: that where there is devotional music, grace is present. Treat every sacred score as written by a man who meant that, and the tempos, the weight, the seriousness tend to find their own level. That is not piety. It is source study.

Terms of Art

Definitions for every specialized term used above — generous on purpose, assuming no prior background.

  • Affect (Affekt) / Affektenlehre — In Baroque thought, an "affect" is a codified emotional state (grief, joy, rage, longing) that music was believed to arouse in listeners by specific technical means; a movement typically sustains one affect throughout. Affektenlehre ("doctrine of the affections") is the modern umbrella term for this body of period theory.
  • Aria — A self-contained solo song within a larger vocal work (cantata, Passion, opera), in which narrative time stops and a single voice reflects on the situation, usually with rich instrumental accompaniment.
  • Basso continuo (continuo) — The Baroque ensemble's foundation: a bass line played by cello or violone plus a chord-playing instrument (organ or harpsichord) that fills in the harmony above it. Virtually all of Bach's concerted music rests on it.
  • Calov Bible — Bach's personal three-volume study Bible, prepared by the orthodox theologian Abraham Calov, with commentary largely from Luther; rediscovered in Michigan in the 1930s with underlinings and marginal annotations in Bach's own hand.
  • Cantata — A multi-movement vocal work (typically choruses, recitatives, arias, and a closing chorale, around twenty to thirty minutes) performed within the Lutheran service, expounding the day's prescribed Gospel reading.
  • Cantor (Thomaskantor) — The church musician responsible for a town's principal liturgical music and for music instruction in its school; Bach held Leipzig's chief version of the post, based at the St. Thomas School, from 1723 until his death.
  • Chorale — A Lutheran congregational hymn: a German text wedded to a sturdy melody that the whole community knew by heart. Also, by extension, Bach's four-part harmonizations of such hymns.
  • Chorale cantata — A cantata built entirely on a single hymn: its melody anchoring the opening chorus, its inner verses paraphrased into arias and recitatives, its final verse closing the work plainly. Bach wrote a nearly complete annual cycle of them in 1724–25.
  • Church year (liturgical year) — The annual calendar of seasons and feasts (Advent, Christmas, Epiphany, Lent, Easter, Pentecost, Trinity and its long tail of Sundays) that organized Lutheran worship — and therefore Bach's entire production schedule of sacred music.
  • Credo — The third main section of the Mass Ordinary: the creed ("I believe in one God..."), the church's summary statement of doctrine. In the B minor Mass it contains the Crucifixus and Et resurrexit.
  • Da capo aria — The standard Baroque aria form: a first section, a contrasting middle, then the instruction da capo ("from the top") — the first section repeated, traditionally with the singer adding ornaments. Bach adapted the operatic convention to sacred use.
  • Evangelist — In a Passion setting, the tenor soloist who sings the Gospel narrative itself in recitative — the storyteller role, distinct from the characters (Christ, Peter, Pilate) and from the commenting arias and chorales.
  • Figurenlehre — "Doctrine of figures": the modern name for the Baroque tradition of cataloguing conventional musical gestures (a falling chromatic line for lament, a rising scale for ascent) as rhetorical devices. Period-authentic as a family of habits, but not the single unified system the name implies — treatises disagreed, and Bach left none.
  • Gematria (number alphabet) — Assigning numbers to letters (A=1, B=2 ...) so that words acquire numerical values — by which BACH = 14. Documented as a Baroque parlor practice; its systematic use by Bach is a modern speculation that scholarship treats with heavy skepticism.
  • Gloria — The second main section of the Mass Ordinary ("Glory to God in the highest"), a hymn of praise. With the Kyrie, it forms the short "Missa" of Lutheran practice.
  • Ground bass (chaconne) — A short bass line repeated over and over while the upper music varies above it; a chaconne is a piece built this way. The Crucifixus of the B minor Mass is a chaconne over a chromatically descending ground.
  • Jesu juva (J.J.) — "Jesus, help": the abbreviation Bach habitually wrote at the head of his scores before beginning work.
  • Kyrie — The opening section of the Mass Ordinary ("Lord, have mercy"), a threefold plea; the B minor Mass opens with a five-bar cry on this text followed by an immense fugue.
  • Lament bass — A bass line descending by half-steps through the interval of a fourth, the Baroque era's most recognizable emblem of grief; it underpins BWV 12 and, decades later, the Crucifixus built from it.
  • Lectionary — The fixed table assigning specific scripture readings (Epistle and Gospel) to each Sunday and feast of the church year. Because the cycle repeated annually, every cantata's text-world was set years in advance.
  • Liturgy — The fixed public order of a worship service: the prescribed sequence of readings, prayers, hymns, and music. Bach's sacred works were designed as parts of a liturgy, not as concert pieces.
  • Lutheran orthodoxy — The doctrinally conservative mainstream of Lutheranism in Bach's era, committed to formal confessional theology, the full liturgical inheritance, and elaborate church music as a fitting offering to God.
  • Mass Ordinary — The five fixed texts of the Latin Mass that never change with the calendar: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus), and Agnus Dei (ending "Dona nobis pacem" — grant us peace). The B minor Mass sets all five at maximal scale.
  • Missa (Kyrie–Gloria Mass) — In Lutheran usage, a short Latin Mass consisting of only the Kyrie and Gloria — the portion of the Latin rite Leipzig retained for feast days. Bach presented such a Missa to the Dresden court in 1733; it later became the opening of the B minor Mass.
  • Musical rhetoric — The Baroque conception of a composition as an oration: theme as thesis, development as argument, the whole aimed at persuading and moving the listener, analyzed with vocabulary borrowed from classical rhetoric.
  • Parody technique — The standard eighteenth-century practice (no mockery implied) of fitting new words to existing music, usually to move a work from one occasion to another — in Bach's case, overwhelmingly from secular occasions to sacred permanence.
  • Passion — A musical setting of one Gospel's account of Christ's suffering and crucifixion, performed on Good Friday; Bach's surviving examples (St. John, St. Matthew) interleave the Gospel narrative with contemplative arias and congregational chorales.
  • Pietism — A Lutheran reform movement of the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries emphasizing inward personal devotion and practical holiness; its stricter voices distrusted elaborate concerted church music as worldly display.
  • Recitative — Speech-like solo singing with light accompaniment, used to deliver narrative and dialogue efficiently between the reflective set pieces (arias, choruses); the Evangelist's storytelling medium.
  • Soli Deo Gloria (S.D.G.) — "To God alone the glory": the abbreviation Bach habitually wrote at the end of scores — sacred, secular, and instrumental alike.
  • Stile antico — "Old style": deliberate composition in the manner of sixteenth-century church polyphony (long note values, smooth vocal lines, chant-based), used by Bach in the B minor Mass to represent the church's ancient musical inheritance.
  • Tempus clausum — "Closed time": the penitential stretches of the church year (in Leipzig, Advent after its first Sunday, and Lent) during which concerted music fell silent in the churches — making the music that framed those silences all the more explosive.
  • Text-painting (word-painting) — Making the music enact the meaning of the words at the local level: a knocking rhythm for "I stand at the door and knock," a rising line for resurrection, harsh dissonance for sin.
  • Turba — Latin for "crowd": in a Passion, the choruses in which the choir plays the mob (or disciples, or priests) — short, ferocious interjections within the Gospel narrative.
  • Vespers — The afternoon or evening service of the liturgical day; in Leipzig, the Good Friday Vespers was the annual home of the Passion performance.
  • Vocation (Beruf) — Luther's doctrine that every honest station and trade — not only the clergy's — is a divine calling, and that ordinary weekday work done well is service to God. The theological ground on which Bach's secular and sacred music-making stood as one practice.
