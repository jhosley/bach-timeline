Fugue: the discipline

A fugue is not a form. That's the first correction this essay owes you, because the textbook diagram — exposition, episode, entry, stretto, coda — suggests a mold into which music is poured, like sonata form. Better to say: a fugue is a procedure, a discipline, a way for several voices to discuss one subject — and what happens after the opening is as free as an essay after its thesis paragraph. Bach's fugues share machinery, not floor plans; the Well-Tempered Clavier's forty-eight are precisely a demonstration that the procedure yields as many shapes as there are subjects.

The machinery, defined once, plainly:

The subject is the theme — stated alone by the first voice, which is why fugues begin with the most exposed moment in music: one naked line, whose profile you must remember for the next five minutes. Subjects are therefore built for identity: a four-note cross, a hammered repeated note, a leap and a silence, six full bars of paragraph — anything, so long as you'll know it when it returns.

The answer is the second voice's entry: the subject transposed to the dominant (the key a fifth up — harmony's natural counterweight). If it's transposed exactly, it's a real answer (I/9's textbook case); usually a note or two must bend to keep the harmony coherent — a tonal answer. While the second voice answers, the first continues with new material; if that continuation recurs faithfully against later entries, it's a countersubject — the subject's standing companion (and some fine fugues, like I/6, decline to keep one). When all voices have entered, the exposition is over — the only part of "fugue form" that's actually standardized.

After that: alternation. Episodes — connective passages developing fragments of the subject, usually in sequence (the same idea restated stepwise higher or lower, harmony traveling) — lead to fresh entries of the subject in related keys. The proportion of episode to entry is the fugue's character: entry-dense fugues feel monumental, episode-rich ones conversational.

Then the special devices, deployed when wanted — never obligatory. Stretto: entries overlapping before the subject finishes — the discussion becoming excited, everyone talking at once (climactic in I/1; structural at II/5's four-voice pile-up). Inversion: the subject upside down (I/6; the drama of II/22 is upright-versus-inverted combat). Augmentation: the subject at doubled note-values — time slowed, gravity doubled (II/2's late entrance; I/8's learned grief). Diminution: halved values, the subject quickened. Pedal point: the bass freezing on one note while harmonies churn above — the arrival platform (I/20 ends over one two hands can't even hold). And the multi-subject species: double and triple fugues, where two or three subjects get separate expositions and are then combined — the summit gesture of II/14, the St. Anne, and the unfinished last page of the Art of Fugue.

Now the corrective examples, because Bach's greatness here is range, not rigor alone. A fugue can be a dance (the passepied of I/11, the gigue of II/4, the gavotte with a trill for a hat, II/13); a costume drama (the French-overture fugue, I/5); a joke with a stumble for a punchline (II/11); a two-voice sprint (I/10); or a five-voice liturgy (I/4). It can renounce every device and live on charm (I/3, I/13) — proof the devices are vocabulary, not virtue. And it can scale from keyboard miniature to orchestra, unaccompanied violin, and double choir: fugue is a property of thought, indifferent to medium — this timeline's most repeated lesson.

How to study one — a practical liturgy, usable on any of the 48: (1) Learn the subject cold: sing it, transpose it, know its skeleton. (2) Count the voices and mark every entry — pencil in the score or ears on the recording; the verified reference table tells you how many speakers to listen for. (3) Find the episodes and name what fragment each develops. (4) Locate the one structural event the fugue is built toward — a stretto, a combination, a pedal, a silence. Every Bach fugue has one; finding it is finding the piece. (5) Only then ask the expressive question — and notice you can now answer it in evidence rather than adjectives.

The discipline's deepest teaching is the one the first essay promised: in a fugue, law and freedom stop being opposites. The subject constrains everything; the constraint is why the freedom means something. Bach spent his whole life saying that — in a teaching book, in a king's parlor, and finally with his own name stitched into the unfinished summit — and the fugue was how he said it most completely.
