Fugue: the discipline

A fugue is not a form. That's the first correction this essay owes you, because the textbook diagram — exposition, episode, entry, stretto, coda — suggests a mold into which music is poured, like sonata form. Better to say: a fugue is a procedure, a discipline, a way for several voices to discuss one subject — and what happens after the opening is as free as an essay after its thesis paragraph. Bach's fugues share machinery, not floor plans; the Well-Tempered Clavier's forty-eight are precisely a demonstration that the procedure yields as many shapes as there are subjects. Everything below is an unpacking of that one sentence: first the machinery, piece by piece, then the freedom, and finally a method for studying any fugue you meet.

The procedure, not the mold

Hold the two words apart for a moment, because the whole essay turns on the distinction. A form, in the textbook sense, is a floor plan: a fixed sequence of sections with prescribed jobs, so that knowing the form means knowing, roughly, what happens when. Sonata form — the plan governing most first movements from Haydn onward — really works that way: an exposition presents themes in two keys, a development destabilizes them, a recapitulation restores them, and a movement that skipped the recapitulation would feel broken. A procedure is different: a set of moves and constraints, like the rules of chess rather than the script of a play — games share an opening logic and a repertoire of tactics; no two share a plot.

Fugue is chess. Only one stretch of a fugue is genuinely standardized — the opening, where the voices enter one by one with the theme, called the exposition — and after that the composer owes you nothing but coherence. There is no prescribed number of later theme-entries, no required key plan, no obligatory climax-device, no fixed proportions. The nineteenth century, wanting fugue to be teachable the way sonata form was teachable, drew the diagram anyway, and generations of students have gone looking in Bach for a "second episode" the music never promised. The corrective is empirical: put any ten of the forty-eight side by side and try to overlay their plans. They won't overlay. What they share is a way of behaving — one theme, several independent voices, imitation as the engine — which is why the best short definition remains conversational: a fugue is several voices discussing one subject, seriously, to a conclusion.

That is also why Bach wrote forty-eight of them into two books. The Well-Tempered Clavier and its second book are not two dozen fillings of one mold; they are a demonstration, key by key, that one discipline can produce a dance, a lament, a piece of church polyphony, a joke. The sections that follow take the machinery first, because you cannot see the freedom until you know exactly what is constrained.

One naked line: the subject

Every fugue begins with the most exposed moment in music: one voice, alone, stating the theme — the subject — with no harmony beneath it, no texture around it, nothing but profile. You must remember that profile for the next five minutes, because the entire piece consists of its returns and its consequences. So subjects are engineered for identity, the way a face is: a few features, unmistakably arranged. The range of solutions in the forty-eight is itself a lesson. A subject can be a four-note cross figure, austere as a woodcut; a hammered repeated note, vehement before it goes anywhere; a leap of a minor ninth answered by a rhetorical silence — the interval, wider than an octave and unstable, is itself the event; a six-bar paragraph, the longest subject in either book, that has argued half a case before the second voice ever speaks; a chorale-like line that creeps down by semitones; even a subject that touches all twelve chromatic notes — the complete pitch alphabet folded into one theme. Anything can be a subject, so long as you'll know it when it returns.

But memorability is only the visible requirement. A working subject carries three hidden ones. First, it must imply its own harmony: a good fugue subject is a melody that suggests the bass line underneath it, outlining its key's essential chords, so that when other voices join, they harmonize what the line already promised. (A key, if the word has gone soft from familiarity: the gravitational system centered on one home note, the tonic, toward which the music's tensions resolve.) Second, it must break well: episodes — the connective passages we'll come to — are built from fragments of the subject, so a subject made of two or three detachable motives is a subject with a future. Third, it must have a shape in time: typically a distinctive head (the identity-bearing opening gesture) and a more generic, running tail. The head is what you recognize; the tail is what the composer can extend, sequence, and dissolve.

Learn to hear subjects this way — as engineered objects, not just tunes — and the opening bars of any fugue become a set of predictions: a leaping subject predicts drama, a stepwise vocal subject predicts church polyphony, a dance-rhythm subject predicts that the strict procedure is about to go dancing. The first naked line is the fugue's genome.

The answer, the countersubject, and the exposition

The second voice enters with the same theme — and here the procedure makes its first genuinely technical move, worth slowing down for. The second entry, called the answer, does not repeat the subject at the same pitch. It transposes it to the dominant: the key built a fifth above the tonic, harmony's natural counterweight, the tonic's closest relative and its standing opposite number. (Why the fifth? Because in tonal music the pull between tonic and dominant chords is the basic engine of motion — every cadence, every phrase, leans on that axis — so answering at the fifth sets the two poles of the key spinning against each other from the second entry onward.) The alternation continues as the voices accumulate: subject in the tonic, answer in the dominant, subject, answer. Older theory gives the pair Latin names — dux, the leader, and comes, the companion — which capture the relationship better than our colorless terms: the answer follows the subject the way a shadow follows a walker, same shape, displaced ground.

If the answer transposes the subject exactly, interval for interval, it is a real answer. Bach's E major fugue in Book I is the textbook case: ten entries of the subject across the little piece, every answer real. But most answers are not real. Usually one or two notes bend — the transposition is adjusted at a strategic point — and the result is a tonal answer. The why is the interesting part, and it is not pedantry. An exact transposition to the dominant is, harmonically, a small act of emigration: it starts pulling the music into the dominant key before the home key has finished establishing itself. If the subject opens by emphasizing the fifth degree of the scale, or leaps prominently from tonic to dominant, an exact answer would emphasize the fifth of the dominant — a note two removes from home — and the exposition would drift loose from its anchor within seconds. So the answer bends: most commonly, where the subject moved from tonic to dominant, the answer moves from dominant back to tonic, the leap inverted in size, the mutual embrace preserved. The rule of thumb: the answer is adjusted exactly enough, and no more, to keep the exposition orbiting the tonic while still sounding like the theme. Finding the bent note — or verifying there isn't one — is the first piece of real analysis you can do on any fugue, and it teaches more about tonal gravity than a chapter of theory. (One more small term while we're in the gears: between entries, the texture sometimes takes a few free beats to re-set the harmony for the next voice — a codetta, the exposition clearing its throat.)

Meanwhile the first voice has not fallen silent — silence would collapse the whole premise of independent voices. It continues with new material against the answer, and what that material is determines a good deal of the fugue's economy. If the continuation is ad hoc, spent once and discarded, it's mere counterpoint. But if it recurs faithfully — if, nearly every time the subject appears for the rest of the piece, this same companion line appears against it — it earns the name countersubject: the subject's standing partner, present at every appearance and never the headline.

A countersubject is much harder to write than it looks, for a reason with its own name: invertible counterpoint. In a fugue the subject migrates through the texture — now in the top voice, now in the bass, now in the middle — which means the countersubject must sound correct both above the subject and below it. That is not automatic. When you flip two lines (invert them at the octave, in the trade's phrase), every harmonic interval between them turns upside down: thirds become sixths, which is fine, but fifths become fourths — and the fourth, in this style, is a dissonance against the bass. A pair of lines that sounded serene one way up can turn illegal the other way up. Writing a countersubject therefore means designing a combination that survives its own inversion. The reward is compounding: once the pair is engineered, every later entry of the subject arrives pre-harmonized, in either orientation, and the composer's real work shifts from writing notes to deploying assets. This is the deep sense in which a fugue composer composes combinations rather than tunes — and it is the direct payoff of the discipline described in the counterpoint essay, where independence of voices was the whole game.

None of it is obligatory, and Bach is happy to show you the option declined: the D minor fugue of Book I keeps no regular countersubject at all, freeing every entry to find fresh company. Other fugues keep two countersubjects in permanent rotation, so that three lines circulate through the voices in six possible stackings — triple invertible counterpoint, machinery humming quietly under music that sounds merely eloquent.

When every voice has made its first entry — subject, answer, subject, answer, as many times as there are voices — the exposition is complete. This is the only stretch of "fugue form" that is actually standardized, the one paragraph every fugue writes the same way, and even here Bach permits himself variety in the details. How many voices? Anywhere from two to five in the forty-eight: a single two-voice sprint; a majority in three and four; and a pair of five-voice edifices — the C-sharp minor and the B-flat minor of Book I — where the texture thickens toward the choral. (A voice, note, is a strand of the polyphonic fabric, not a singer: a keyboard fugue "in four voices" maintains four simultaneous melodic lines under two hands, and part of the spectator sport is wondering how.) Episodes: how a fugue travels

After the exposition, the procedure's honest answer to "what happens next?" is: alternation. Passages where the subject is present alternate with passages where it is absent, and the absences have a name — episodes — and a job: travel. An episode takes fragments of the subject or countersubject (this is why subjects must break well) and develops them in freer texture while steering the harmony toward a new key. Its favorite vehicle is the sequence: a short idea restated two or three times, each restatement shifted stepwise higher or lower, the harmony walking a staircase. Sequence is tonal music's basic unit of motion, and in a fugue it does double duty: the fragment keeps the subject's DNA audible while the ground moves underneath.

Where does the travel go? To the related keys — the small family of keys sharing most of their notes with home: the dominant a fifth up, the subdominant a fifth down, and the relative major or minor, the sibling key sharing the identical set of notes but centering it on a different tonic (C major and A minor are relatives; so are G minor and B-flat major). The shift from one key's gravity to another's is modulation, and a fugue's middle is a itinerary of modulations, each arrival stamped by a fresh entry of the subject in the new key — a middle entry. So the fugue breathes in a long rhythm you can learn to feel before you can name it: episode (fragmentary, sequential, traveling, the theme withheld) — entry (the subject lands whole in a new key, planting a flag) — episode — entry. Sometimes the composer teases: a false entry starts the subject's head and abandons it after a few notes, the theme glimpsed rounding a corner.

Here is the analytical payoff, the single most useful idea in this essay for actual listening: the proportion of episode to entry is the fugue's character. A fugue dense with entries feels monumental, liturgical, carved — the subject is the music's whole substance. A fugue rich in episodes feels conversational, airy, discursive — the subject is a topic the music keeps leaving and returning to, the way good talk does. Neither is superior; they are temperaments. And the procedure scales down as gracefully as up: Book II's G major fugue is brief enough to be, in effect, a fughetta — a fugue in miniature — while in Book I the A major prelude is itself a strict little three-voice fughetta, fugal procedure caught moonlighting on the other side of the double bar.

The devices

Then there is the special equipment — the celebrated devices that the textbook diagram places in the fugue's later stages, and that Bach deploys exactly when wanted and not otherwise. Take them one at a time, because each is a distinct idea about what a theme is.

Stretto (Italian for "tightened") is entries overlapping: a second voice starts the subject before the first has finished it, the theme crowding in on itself — the discussion becoming excited, everyone talking at once and, miraculously, still making sense. It works only if the subject was designed for it; a theme that harmonizes with its own delayed copy is a rare engineered object (the canon essay lives entirely inside that problem). Bach uses stretto climactically in the very first fugue of Book I — a fugue so saturated with overlapping entries that it has almost no episodes at all — and structurally in Book II's D major fugue, which gathers the subject into all four voices at once near its close: the pile-up as arrival.

Inversion turns the subject upside down: every rising interval falls, every falling interval rises. The identity survives — you recognize a face in a reflection — but the sense reverses, ascent becoming descent, question becoming answer. The D minor fugue of Book I plays with its subject's mirror; Book II's B-flat minor fugue builds its whole drama as upright-versus-inverted combat, the theme fighting its own reflection.

Augmentation states the subject in doubled note-values — the theme at half speed, a procession passing at walking pace through hurrying traffic. Time slows; gravity doubles. Bach saves it for gestures of weight: in Book II's C minor fugue the fourth voice withholds its entrance until late and then delivers the subject in augmentation — the last speaker turning out to be the eldest; in the E-flat minor fugue of Book I augmentation belongs to the music's learned grief. Diminution is the same operation reversed — note-values halved, the subject quickened into a sprinting version of itself — and the two can sound together: theme, half-speed theme, double-speed theme, stacked, the same thought at three tempos of thinking.

Pedal point is the simplest device and the most physical: the bass freezes on a single sustained note — usually the dominant, then the tonic — while the upper voices churn above it, harmonies grinding against ground that refuses to move; then the bass resolves and the whole structure settles. It is the fugue's arrival platform, the sound of an ending being manufactured. The A minor fugue of Book I ends over a pedal point that two hands famously cannot even hold — evidence, mid-keyboard-book, of an organist's feet in the composer's imagination.

Finally the multi-subject species. A double fugue engages two subjects, a triple fugue three; in the grandest design each subject gets its own exposition and then — the summit gesture — the subjects are combined, sounding simultaneously, and you discover that these separately introduced characters were engineered from the start to be compatible. It is the procedure's most theatrical proof of premeditation. Book II holds one triple fugue, the F-sharp minor, its three subjects folded together in the closing pages, and double fugues in several manners — the G-sharp minor, whose slithering chromatic second subject changes the piece's temperature the moment it appears, and the B major, which opens in the old vocal style before its livelier companion arrives. Beyond the keyboard books stand the St. Anne, the great organ triple fugue, and the unfinished final page of the Art of Fugue, where a third subject spelling B–A–C–H had just entered the combination when the manuscript stops.

Against all this glitter, hold on to the corrective: the devices are vocabulary, not virtue. The C-sharp major fugue of Book I is free of the standard devices entirely, and the F-sharp major nearly so — no canon, no inversion — and both live happily on charm. A fugue is not better for containing a stretto, any more than an essay is better for containing a syllogism. The devices exist for the moments when the discussion needs them.

The range: dance, joke, liturgy

Now the corrective examples in earnest, because Bach's greatness here is range, not rigor alone — and range is precisely what the procedure-not-form thesis predicts. If fugue were a form, all fugues would be relatives. Because it is a procedure, a fugue can be anything that can sustain a discussion.

It can be a dance — not metaphorically, but by genre: the F major fugue of Book I is a passepied, a quick courtly triple-time dance, its learned entries landing on dancing feet; Book II's C-sharp minor fugue is an Italian gigue, skipping in 12/16; the F-sharp major of Book II is a gavotte in all but name — and a galant gavotte at that, written in the light modern style of Bach's sons' generation, opening with a trill for a hat: the old discipline wearing the new fashion, and wearing it well. A fugue can be a costume drama: Book I's D major fugue struts in the dotted rhythms of a French overture, the pomp of Versailles processed through four strict voices. It can be a joke with a stumble for a punchline: in Book II's F major fugue the final bass entry trips over an extra leap — a wrong-footing Bach wrote in cold blood, the fugue's last word delivered deadpan. It can be a two-voice sprint (the E minor of Book I, the only two-voice fugue in either book, all elbows and daylight) or a five-voice liturgy (the C-sharp minor, whose cross-shaped subject and massed texture make a cathedral of a keyboard). And it can be a deliberate act of historical ventriloquism: Book II's E major fugue is written in stile antico — the "old style," the smooth vocal polyphony of the Renaissance church, Palestrina's grammar — after a model by Fischer, Bach folding a century-old manner into his modern collection as one more thing the procedure can be.

Line these up — passepied, gavotte, overture, prank, sprint, liturgy, antique — and the thesis stops being a slogan. No mold produces that list; a discipline does.

Beyond the keyboard

Fugue is a property of thought, indifferent to medium — this timeline's most repeated lesson, and nowhere clearer than in how casually Bach carries the procedure across instruments. He writes fugue for orchestra, the concerto's brilliance and the fugue's rigor riding the same movement. He writes fugue for unaccompanied violin — counterpoint implied by a single bowed instrument, the listener's mind conscripted to sustain what the strings can only sketch. He writes fugue for double choir, eight vocal lines in two facing teams. He writes fugue for chorus: the B minor Mass opens with a brief cry and then a vast Kyrie fugue that spends its length earning the cry, and closes with a fugue reused from its own Gratias — thanksgiving and supplication set to the same subject. And on the organ, his first instrument, the procedure attains civic scale: the E minor "Wedge", its subject opening outward in widening leaps like a diagram of its own nickname, builds a fugue whose middle erupts into free toccata figuration before the strict opening returns note for note — fugue as dramatic architecture, law, fantasy, law.

He could also do it without paper. At Potsdam in 1747, handed a long chromatic theme at the keyboard, Bach improvised a three-voice fugue on it extempore, before the king; asked for six voices on the same subject, he declined — not every theme, he said, will bear six — and improvised a six-part fugue on a theme of his own instead. The refusal is as instructive as the feat: fugue-thinking begins with judging what a subject contains. Two months later the Musical Offering delivered in print the six-voice ricercar he would not fake in the room — ricercar being the deliberately archaic word for fugue, chosen to face a fashionable court with the full dignity of the learned style. And at the end stands the Art of Fugue, a whole volume that is nothing but the procedure examining itself: one subject taken through simple fugues, counter-fugues, mirror fugues, and the unfinished summit — fugue about fugue, the discipline written up as its own treatise.

A study liturgy

How to study one — a practical liturgy, usable on any of the forty-eight, expanded here into the full office. Do the steps in order; each one gives the next its material.

  • Learn the subject cold. Sing it, away from the instrument, until you can start it on any note. Transpose it. Find its skeleton — the few structural pitches under the decoration — and its seams, where it breaks into fragments. You are memorizing a face; the whole piece is that face returning.
  • Count the voices and mark every entry. Pencil in the score or ears on the recording; the verified reference table tells you how many speakers to listen for. Every entry — middle entries hide in inner voices, and finding them is the exercise.
  • Classify the answer. Real or tonal? If tonal, find the bent note and ask what it protects. This is five minutes of work that permanently sharpens your sense of key.
  • Interrogate the countersubject. Is there one? Does it recur faithfully? Watch it flip above and below the subject and notice the invertible engineering. (Or note its absence, and what freedom the absence buys.)
  • Map the episodes. Name the fragment each one develops and the key each one travels to. The proportion you discover — entry-dense or episode-rich — is the fugue's temperament, stated in percentages.
  • Locate the one structural event. A stretto, a combination, an augmentation, a pedal, a silence, a stumble. Every Bach fugue is built toward one; finding it is finding the piece.
  • Only then ask the expressive question — what is this fugue about? — and notice that you can now answer it in evidence rather than adjectives.

Where to begin: the two-voice E minor, because two speakers are followable on a first hearing; then the compact three-voice C minor of Book I; then one fugue from the sections above, chosen for the device you most want to hear work.

The discipline's deepest teaching is the one the first essay promised: in a fugue, law and freedom stop being opposites. The subject constrains everything; the constraint is why the freedom means something. Bach spent his whole life saying that — in a teaching book, in a king's parlor, and finally with his own name stitched into the unfinished summit — and the fugue was how he said it most completely.

Terms of Art

A working glossary for this essay and for score-in-hand study of the forty-eight — generous on purpose, assuming nothing.

  • Answer — The second statement of a fugue's theme, sung by the second voice to enter, transposed to the dominant (a fifth above or fourth below the original). Answers alternate with subject-statements throughout the exposition.
  • Augmentation — Restating a theme with all its note-values lengthened, usually doubled, so it moves at half speed. The slowed theme reads as weightier and more solemn than its source.
  • Cadence — A melodic-harmonic formula that closes a phrase or section, the musical equivalent of punctuation; the strongest cadences move from dominant harmony to tonic.
  • Chromatic — Using notes outside the prevailing key's seven-note scale, typically moving by semitones. Chromatic lines sound tense, searching, or grief-laden against plain (diatonic) surroundings.
  • Coda — A closing passage after the main argument is complete; in fugues, often the music above or following a final pedal point.
  • Codetta — A brief connective passage inside the exposition, between two entries, that re-sets the harmony so the next voice can enter cleanly.
  • Counterpoint — The craft of combining two or more simultaneous melodies, each independently singable, which together make coherent harmony. Fugue is counterpoint's most developed procedure.
  • Countersubject — A recurring companion line that accompanies the subject at (nearly) every appearance after its first. To do its job it must be written in invertible counterpoint, sounding correct above or below the subject.
  • Diminution — Restating a theme with all its note-values shortened, usually halved, so it moves at double speed; the opposite of augmentation.
  • Dominant — The fifth degree of the scale, and the key or chord built on it; the tonic's chief counterweight. The pull between dominant and tonic is tonal music's basic engine, which is why fugue answers transpose to the dominant.
  • Double fugue — A fugue with two subjects. In the grandest type each subject receives its own exposition before the two are combined in simultaneous counterpoint.
  • Dux and comes — Latin for "leader" and "companion": older theory's names for the subject and its answer, respectively.
  • Entry — Any complete statement of the subject (or answer) by one voice. Analysts map a fugue by marking every entry.
  • Episode — A passage in which the complete subject is absent, built from its fragments, usually sequential, and usually modulating; the connective, traveling tissue between entries.
  • Exposition — A fugue's opening paragraph, in which each voice enters in turn with the subject or answer until all have spoken. The only genuinely standardized stretch of fugal procedure.
  • False entry — A teasing partial statement: a voice begins the subject's head and abandons it after a few notes, raising the expectation of an entry without fulfilling it.
  • Fughetta — A small-scale fugue: complete exposition, modest continuation, miniature dimensions.
  • Fugue — A contrapuntal procedure in which a fixed number of independent voices enter one by one with a single theme (the subject) and then develop it through entries, episodes, and optional learned devices. A discipline and a texture, not a fixed form.
  • Imitation — One voice echoing another's melodic material shortly after it, at the same or a different pitch. The general principle of which fugue and canon are strict applications.
  • Interval — The distance in pitch between two notes, named by counting scale-steps: second, third, fourth, fifth, octave, and so on. Intervals are classed as consonant (stable) or dissonant (tense, requiring resolution).
  • Inversion (melodic) — Turning a melody upside down: each rising interval becomes an equal falling one and vice versa, producing a mirror image that preserves the theme's identity while reversing its sense.
  • Invertible counterpoint — Two (or more) lines engineered so they sound correct in either vertical arrangement — either one on top. Essential for countersubjects, since the subject migrates between high and low voices. Also called double (or triple) counterpoint.
  • Key — The gravitational system of a passage: a home note (tonic) plus the scale and chords oriented toward it. Named by tonic and mode, e.g. C major, F-sharp minor.
  • Middle entry — Any complete statement of the subject after the exposition, typically in a related key reached by a modulating episode.
  • Modulation — The process of moving from one key to another, so that a new tonic takes over the music's gravity. Fugue episodes are the usual vehicles of modulation.
  • Pedal point — A single bass note sustained (or repeated) while the harmonies above it change and clash against it; named for the organ pedals that hold such notes. Commonly signals an approaching ending.
  • Real answer — An answer that transposes the subject exactly, interval for interval, into the dominant, with no adjusted notes.
  • Relative key — The major and minor key sharing the same set of notes but different tonics (C major and A minor; B-flat major and G minor). The most frictionless destination for modulation.
  • Ricercar — An older, learned genre of strict imitative keyboard counterpoint; by Bach's day, a deliberately archaic word for a serious fugue, as in the Musical Offering's two ricercars.
  • Scale degree — A note's position within its key's scale, numbered 1 through 7 from the tonic; degree 1 is the tonic, degree 5 the dominant.
  • Sequence — Immediate restatement of a short musical idea two or more times, each time transposed stepwise higher or lower; tonal music's standard device for traveling, and the staple content of fugue episodes.
  • Stile antico — "Old style": deliberate imitation of smooth Renaissance vocal polyphony (Palestrina's idiom) within later music — long note-values, stepwise motion, strict dissonance treatment.
  • Stretto — Overlapping entries: a voice begins the subject before the previous statement has finished, compressing the imitation. An intensifying device, often reserved for a fugue's climax.
  • Subject — A fugue's theme: the melody stated alone by the first voice and developed by all voices throughout. Its design — memorable profile, implied harmony, detachable fragments — largely determines the fugue's possibilities.
  • Texture — The weave of simultaneous musical lines: how many there are and how they relate (melody plus accompaniment; or polyphony, several independent lines, as in fugue).
  • Tonal answer — An answer in which one or more intervals are adjusted (typically tonic-and-dominant emphases swapped) so the exposition stays anchored in the home key rather than drifting to the dominant.
  • Tonic — The home note and chord of a key, the point of maximal rest toward which the key's tensions resolve; the first scale degree.
  • Triple fugue — A fugue with three subjects, each typically given its own exposition before all three are combined; the multi-subject procedure's summit.
  • Voice — One strand of a polyphonic texture — a continuous melodic line, whether sung or played. A "fugue in four voices" maintains four such lines, whatever the instrument.
