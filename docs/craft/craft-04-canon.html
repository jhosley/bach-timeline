Canon: the strictest game

A canon is counterpoint with the safety catch removed. In a fugue, imitation is a resource — voices echo the subject when the music wants them to, and the composer adjusts freely everywhere else. In a canon, imitation is law: one voice states a melody, and another must reproduce it exactly, at a fixed delay, for the entire piece — while the combination remains correct harmony throughout. The composer writes one line and a rule; the rule writes the rest. It is the closest music comes to proving a theorem.

The variables make the game. The following voice can enter at any interval — repeat the leader at the unison, or transposed up a second, a third, a fifth — each interval a different constraint (canon at the unison is hardest to keep interesting; canon at the second is hardest to keep correct). It can follow at any distance — two bars, one bar, half a beat, as in the Sixth Brandenburg's seasick opening chase. And it can transform as it follows: in inversion (the follower mirrors the leader upside down), in augmentation (the follower at half speed, falling ever further behind like a long memory), even in retrograde — the crab canon, the melody against itself backwards. Each added transformation multiplies the difficulty of the one real task: designing a leader-line that remains beautiful while satisfying its own future.

Bach's canonic masterwork hides in plain sight inside the Goldberg Variations: every third variation is a canon, and the interval grows each time — variation 3 at the unison, 6 at the second, 9 at the third, on up to 27 at the ninth — a systematic ladder through the whole space of the game, disguised as entertainment. Here is the point this essay most wants to make: listen to them cold and you will not notice they are canons. Variation 9 sounds like a tender duet; variation 15, canon at the fifth in inversion, sounds like the work's first grief; variation 21 like a dark aria. The strictness is complete and completely inaudible — law experienced as inevitability, which was, one suspects, precisely the theology of it (essay six again). When Bach's personal copy of the print surfaced in 1974 with fourteen further canons (BWV 1087) penciled in the back — all on the same eight bass notes — it confirmed what the published thirty imply: this game, for him, had no bottom.

The other canonic monuments each add a register. The Canonic Variations on Vom Himmel hoch — his scientific-society credential — keep a Christmas carol singing plainly through five escalating canons: strictness as devotion, the child's tune never obscured. The Musical Offering's ten canons on the royal theme add wit: several were engraved as riddle canons — one melodic line plus a cryptic Latin clue, the solver left to deduce where and how the second voice enters. One spirals upward, modulating a whole tone with each repetition, inscribed "as the notes ascend, so may the King's glory": a canon that is also a courtier's compliment that is also, played long enough, a joke (it never stops climbing). And the Fourteen Canons' private notebook-page shows the game with no audience at all — Bach solving Bach, for nobody.

Why should a student care about the strictest game? Two reasons, one technical, one larger. Technically, canon is the ultimate test of line-design — the discipline of writing melodies whose every note serves double duty — and studying even one canon closely (start with Invention No. 2, a strict canon most players never notice) permanently upgrades your sense of what a "good melody" is: not a pretty surface but a structure with load-bearing walls. And larger: canon is where Bach's aesthetic makes its purest claim — that maximum constraint and maximum expression are not enemies, that the tightest rule, fully mastered, disappears into song. You can hear that claim argued in every genre on this timeline; in the canons it is simply demonstrated, QED, over and over, in music you'd love before you knew it was a proof.
