What counterpoint is, and why Bach bet his life on it

Start with the word. Contrapunctus — "point against point," note against note: the craft of combining two or more melodies that sound at the same time, each one a real tune you could sing alone, which together also make correct, expressive harmony. That double requirement is the whole discipline. Anyone can write one good melody. Anyone can write a chord progression and float a tune over it — that's what most music, from opera arias to pop songs, actually is: melody plus accompaniment. Counterpoint refuses the division of labor. In a contrapuntal texture there is no accompaniment; everyone is a soloist, and the harmony is what their agreements and frictions add up to.

Hear the difference in one comparison from this timeline. In a Vivaldi concerto — the music young Bach transcribed by the crate — the violin sings and the orchestra mostly pulses beneath: brilliant, clear, one voice sovereign. In the slow movement of Bach's Double Concerto, two violins sing the same melody at different times, overlapping so that each one's phrase-ending becomes the other's accompaniment. Nobody is sovereign; the beauty is the relationship. That is counterpoint doing emotional work, and it's why the movement feels like a portrait of friendship rather than a serenade.

The rules that make it possible are older than Bach by centuries. The Renaissance church — Palestrina is the name the textbooks keep — worked out how independent voices can move without chaos: which intervals are stable (consonances), which are tense (dissonances), and above all how dissonance must be prepared and resolved — approached smoothly, left by step — so that friction reads as feeling rather than error. A dissonance handled by the rules is called, in one classic form, a suspension: one voice holds its note while the others move, creating a clash that leans, aches, and resolves. If you've done species counterpoint exercises, you've drilled exactly this; if you haven't, play Invention No. 11 slowly and listen for the holds and releases. Every "expressive" moment in Bach is, at the level of machinery, a prepared and resolved tension. The rules aren't the opposite of the feeling; they're its delivery system.

Bach inherited this craft from every direction at once — the family workshop, Pachelbel via his brother, the northern organists, the choir library at Lüneburg — and then made the strange, career-defining choice this timeline keeps circling: as his century abandoned counterpoint, he doubled down. The fashionable music of his mature years — the galant style his own sons wrote — deliberately simplified: one lovely melody, light chords, easy pleasure. Critics like Scheibe said the quiet part loudly: all that artifice is against nature. Bach's answer, in words (through his defender Birnbaum) and in works, was that art is nature brought to its perfection — that reality is many true voices sounding at once, and music that renders only one voice is the simplification.

Why did it matter so much to him? Three answers braid through this timeline, and you don't have to choose among them. Craft-pride: counterpoint is the hardest thing music does, and Bach was a guildsman's son who measured himself by mastery. Pedagogy: independence of voices is teachable — it's a discipline of constraints, which is why his teaching books could promise composition to beginners. Theology: for a Lutheran of Bach's stamp, many free voices cohering into one harmony was not a metaphor he invented but a picture of created order he inherited — essay six takes that up in full.

For your own study, one reorientation matters more than any definition: stop listening for the tune and start listening for the conversation. Bach rewards — really, requires — a kind of listening most music never asks of us: tracking two, three, four simultaneous speakers, the way you follow a good dinner-table argument. The skill is buildable, and this timeline's work cards build it deliberately: the Inventions teach two-voice hearing, the Sinfonias three, the WTC fugues three to five. Six months of that ladder changes what your ears can do — and every other repertoire you study, from Renaissance masses to Brahms, comes into focus with it. That is the practical case for counterpoint, and it was Bach's own: he taught it not as history's strictest game but as the fullest way of hearing what music can be.
